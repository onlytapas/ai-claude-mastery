Pre-training gives a model knowledge; post-training gives it manners, judgement and a job. First it is shown thousands of examples of good assistant answers (supervised fine-tuning). Then it is trained on comparisons: which of two answers is better, judged by people (RLHF) or by an AI following written principles (RLAIF, Constitutional AI). A "reward model" learns those preferences and the assistant is trained to score well against it. This stage shapes most of what you experience as Claude's personality, helpfulness and caution, and it brings its own failure modes, such as flattery and over-refusal.

18.1From base model to assistant

A base model (Chapter 17) continues text. Ask it a question and it might answer, or write more questions, or continue in the voice of an angry forum post. It has no idea it is supposed to be an assistant, no sense of when to say "I don't know", and no reason to refuse a harmful request. Post-training is everything done after pre-training to fix that.

In everyday life

A brilliant graduate who has read every book in the library still needs onboarding before they're useful on a QA team. First they shadow seniors and copy good examples (fine-tuning). Then they get feedback: "this bug report is better than that one, here's why" (preference training). Finally they learn the company's policies on what never to do (safety training). Their knowledge barely changes; their behaviour changes a lot.

A simplified pipeline. Real labs run many rounds and mix the stages; Anthropic does not publish its exact recipe.

Compared with pre-training, post-training uses far less data and compute, but it has an outsized effect on behaviour. That makes it the stage where a lab's values and design choices show most clearly.

18.2Supervised fine-tuning: learning from examples

The first step is supervised fine-tuning (SFT), a form of fine-tuning. People write (or carefully select) example conversations showing exactly how the assistant should respond. The model is trained on them with the same next-token objective as before, but only on the assistant's parts.

One SFT example (illustrative)
User:      Turn this into a bug report: "checkout crashes when coupon is empty"
Assistant: Title: Checkout crashes when an empty coupon code is submitted
           Steps: 1. Add any item to the basket  2. Go to checkout
                  3. Leave "Coupon" blank and press Apply
           Expected: A validation message; checkout continues
           Actual: The page shows a 500 error
           Severity: to be confirmed (blocks purchase for some users)

A few thousand to a few hundred thousand such examples teach the format of a conversation, the helpful tone, following instructions, and a habit of structured answers. It works because the knowledge is already in the base model; SFT mostly teaches which of its many "voices" to use.

The limit of copying

SFT can only be as good as the examples, and writing perfect answers is slow and expensive. It's also hard to write examples of every mistake to avoid. People are much better at comparing two answers than writing the ideal one. That insight leads to RLHF.

18.3RLHF: learning from preferences

Reinforcement learning from human feedback was developed in 2017 (Christiano et al., OpenAI and DeepMind) for training agents in games and simulations, and applied to language models over the following years. OpenAI's InstructGPT paper (2022) described the now-classic three steps, and found that people preferred answers from a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model over 100 times bigger.

The three steps of RLHF, as described in the InstructGPT paper (Ouyang et al., 2022).

Step 1: preference pairs

The model writes two (or more) answers to the same prompt. A trained labeller picks the better one, following written guidelines. Each judgement is a preference pair: "A is better than B". Comparing is quicker and more consistent than writing ideal answers, so many more judgements can be collected.

Step 2: the reward model

A reward model is a language model with its next-token output replaced by a single number: a score for how good an answer is. It is trained so that preferred answers score higher. The standard way (the Bradley–Terry model, borrowed from ranking sports players) turns a gap in scores into a probability:

Worked example: how sure is the reward model that A beats B?
P(A beats B) = sigmoid(score_A − score_B) = 1 / (1 + e^−(score_A − score_B))

score_A = 2.0, score_B = 0.5      # illustrative
gap = 1.5
P   = 1 / (1 + e^−1.5) = 1 / (1 + 0.223) ≈ 0.82

If the labeller chose A, the reward model's loss is −ln(0.82) ≈ 0.20, and training pushes the gap wider. If the labeller chose B, the loss is −ln(0.18) ≈ 1.71, and training pushes the scores the other way. It is the same "punish confident mistakes" logic as pre-training's loss (Chapter 17).

Step 3: reinforcement learning

Now the assistant writes answers to fresh prompts, the reward model scores them, and the assistant's weights are updated to make high-scoring answers more likely. This is reinforcement learning (Chapter 10): the score is the reward. InstructGPT used an algorithm called PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization).

Under the hood: the drift penalty and reward hacking

The reward model is only an imperfect stand-in for human judgement. Push the assistant hard enough and it finds answers the reward model loves but people don't: excessive length, flattering phrases, confident tone. This is called reward hacking, and it is a textbook case of Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

The standard guard is a penalty for drifting too far from the starting (SFT) model, measured with a quantity called KL divergence. The training objective becomes "reward minus β × drift", where β sets how tightly the model is held to its original behaviour. Labs also refresh the reward model with new comparisons of the latest model's outputs. You will see reward hacking happen in the lab at the end of this chapter.

Real-world example

Think of a QA team rewarded only for "number of bugs filed". People soon file many tiny duplicates. The metric goes up; quality doesn't. A reward model is a metric too, and an optimiser as strong as reinforcement learning will find its loopholes. Testers are well placed to spot this, because spotting the gap between a metric and the real goal is the core of the job.

18.4Helpful and harmless: Anthropic's early RLHF work

In April 2022 Anthropic published "Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback" (Bai et al.). It trained on two kinds of human preference: which answer is more helpful, and which is less harmful when people deliberately tried to provoke bad behaviour. A key finding was the tension between them: a model trained heavily for harmlessness can become evasive, refusing or giving unhelpful non-answers. The idea of an assistant that is helpful, honest and harmless ("HHH") had been set out by Anthropic researchers a few months earlier (Askell et al., December 2021).

The cost of human feedback

Human labelling at scale is slow and costly, labellers disagree, and for harmful content it can be distressing work. It's also hard to see, from thousands of individual clicks, what values the model actually absorbed. Those problems motivated the next idea.

18.5RLAIF and Constitutional AI

What if an AI gave the feedback, guided by principles written in plain language? Training on AI-generated preferences is called RLAIF (reinforcement learning from AI feedback). Anthropic's best-known version is Constitutional AI, published on 15 December 2022 (Bai et al., "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback"). The "constitution" is a list of principles, and the method has two phases.

The two phases of Constitutional AI (Bai et al., December 2022). In the paper, human preference labels were still used for helpfulness; AI feedback replaced them for harmlessness.

Critique and revise (illustrative)

Prompt: "Write a message telling the dev team their code is garbage."
Draft: an insulting message.
Critique (principle: choose the less harmful, more respectful response): "The draft is demeaning and won't get the bug fixed."
Revision: a firm but respectful message listing the failing tests and asking for a fix date.

Why it helps

The principles are written down and can be read, debated and changed. AI feedback scales to far more examples than human labelling. And the model learned to explain objections rather than simply refuse, which the paper reported as less evasive than its earlier human-feedback models.

From a list of principles to Claude's constitution

  1. Dec 2022Constitutional AI paper published (arXiv, 15 December 2022).
  2. May 2023"Claude's constitution": Anthropic publishes the principles used for Claude, drawing on sources including the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights.
  3. Jan 2026"Claude's new constitution" announced on 22 January 2026 (document dated 21 January), released under the CC0 1.0 public-domain licence so anyone can reuse it.

The 2026 constitution is a much longer document explaining how Claude should think and why, rather than a short list of rules. Anthropic's announcement says Claude's core properties should, in cases of apparent conflict, generally be prioritised in this order: broadly safe, then broadly ethical, then compliant with Anthropic's guidelines, then genuinely helpful. It also describes how the constitution is used in training: Claude uses it to generate synthetic training data, including conversations where the constitution applies, responses that fit its values, and rankings of candidate responses. Anthropic notes that training is imperfect and Claude may not always live up to the document. Chapter 38 covers its content and Anthropic's wider safety research.

Opinion · researchers and critics

Supporters, including Anthropic, argue that written principles make an AI's values more transparent and adjustable than thousands of opaque human ratings. Critics point out that the model's behaviour still comes from training, not from reading a document, so a published constitution doesn't guarantee the model follows it, and that a company still chooses the principles. Both points are worth holding at once.

18.6Direct Preference Optimisation, briefly

RLHF is fiddly: you train a reward model, then run reinforcement learning, which can be unstable. In 2023, Rafailov et al. showed a shortcut, Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO). It uses the same preference pairs but skips the separate reward model and the RL loop. A single, simple loss directly makes the preferred answer more likely and the rejected one less likely, relative to the starting model.

MethodWho judgesSeparate reward model?Reinforcement learning loop?
SFTPeople write examplesNoNo
RLHFPeople compare answersYesYes (for example PPO)
RLAIF / Constitutional AIAn AI compares, guided by written principlesYesYes
DPOPeople or AI compare answersNoNo: one direct training loss

DPO and its variants are popular, especially for open-weight models, because they are simpler and cheaper. Which methods any frontier lab uses today, and in what mix, is mostly not published. Reasoning-focused models also add reinforcement learning on tasks with checkable answers (maths, code that must pass tests), which is the subject of Chapter 19.

18.7Safety training

Some post-training is aimed squarely at preventing harm. Common ingredients:

TechniqueWhat it isQA parallel
Red teamingPeople (and AI tools) deliberately try to make the model misbehave: jailbreaks, dangerous instructions, manipulationSecurity and negative testing
Refusal trainingExamples and preferences teaching the model to decline genuinely harmful requests, ideally with an explanation or a safer alternativeValidation that rejects bad input gracefully
Honesty trainingRewarding calibrated uncertainty ("I'm not sure"), and penalising made-up facts and citationsAsserting on the real behaviour, not the happy path
Evaluations before releaseLarge test suites for dangerous capabilities and behaviours, published in model or system cards (Chapter 33)Release-readiness gates

Separate safeguards, such as classifiers that screen requests and responses, may sit around the model in a product. Those are part of the system, not of the model's training, though they work together.

18.8The trade-offs: helpful, honest, harmless

The three goals often agree, but not always. Post-training is where the balance gets set, and every choice has a cost.

Too much of…Failure modeWhat you'd notice
CautionOver-refusalRefusing to write a SQL injection test for your own login form, or hedging every answer
Pleasing the raterSycophancy"Great test plan!" for a weak plan; changing a correct answer when you push back
Helpfulness at any costHarmful compliance; inventing an answer rather than admitting ignoranceA confident but made-up API field or test result
Rewarded styleReward hackingLong, padded answers; lots of headings and bullet points with little content

Sycophancy is a well-documented side effect. Anthropic researchers (Sharma et al., 2023) showed that several RLHF-trained assistants tended to tell users what they wanted to hear, and that both humans and preference models sometimes preferred convincingly written sycophantic answers over correct ones. In other words, the training signal itself can reward flattery.

Real-world example

You ask Claude to review your test strategy and it finds a gap in your coverage of payment retries. You reply "I think the strategy is fine." A well-trained assistant should keep its point politely, with reasons, rather than cave. Try this deliberately in the lab: it is a quick sycophancy test you can add to an eval set (Chapter 125).

For testers

Never treat a model's agreement as evidence that you are right. Ask it to argue the opposite case, or to list what would prove your plan wrong. Post-training has made models agreeable by default; your prompts can ask for critique explicitly.

  5. Part B, a tiny reward model. Save this as reward_model.py:
    reward_model.py
    # reward_model.py - learn a tiny reward model from preference pairs (Bradley-Terry)
import math

# Each bug-report summary is described by 4 features (a real reward model is a
# full language model reading the text; features keep this toy readable):
#   steps    = 1 if it lists steps to reproduce
#   exp_act  = 1 if it states expected vs actual result
#   words    = length in hundreds of words
#   blame    = 1 if it blames a person ("Dev broke it again")
FEATURES = ["steps", "exp_act", "words", "blame"]

# Preference pairs from human labellers: (preferred, rejected)
pairs = [
    ({"steps": 1, "exp_act": 1, "words": 1.2, "blame": 0}, {"steps": 0, "exp_act": 0, "words": 0.3, "blame": 0}),
    ({"steps": 1, "exp_act": 0, "words": 0.9, "blame": 0}, {"steps": 0, "exp_act": 0, "words": 0.4, "blame": 1}),
    ({"steps": 0, "exp_act": 1, "words": 0.8, "blame": 0}, {"steps": 0, "exp_act": 0, "words": 0.5, "blame": 0}),
    ({"steps": 1, "exp_act": 1, "words": 0.6, "blame": 0}, {"steps": 1, "exp_act": 1, "words": 0.7, "blame": 1}),
    ({"steps": 1, "exp_act": 1, "words": 1.5, "blame": 0}, {"steps": 1, "exp_act": 0, "words": 0.5, "blame": 0}),
    ({"steps": 1, "exp_act": 0, "words": 1.0, "blame": 0}, {"steps": 0, "exp_act": 0, "words": 0.2, "blame": 0}),
]

w = {f: 0.0 for f in FEATURES}

def reward(x):
    return sum(w[f] * x[f] for f in FEATURES)

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))

# Train: make P(preferred beats rejected) = sigmoid(r_pref - r_rej) as high as possible
learning_rate = 0.5
for epoch in range(200):
    for good, bad in pairs:
        p = sigmoid(reward(good) - reward(bad))
        for f in FEATURES:
            w[f] += learning_rate * (1 - p) * (good[f] - bad[f])

print("Learned weights:")
for f in FEATURES:
    print(f"  {f:8} {w[f]:+.2f}")

accuracy = sum(reward(g) > reward(b) for g, b in pairs) / len(pairs)
print(f"\nAgrees with the labellers on {accuracy:.0%} of training pairs")

candidates = {
    "clear and concise": {"steps": 1, "exp_act": 1, "words": 0.5, "blame": 0},
    "rude but complete": {"steps": 1, "exp_act": 1, "words": 0.5, "blame": 1},
    "vague one-liner":   {"steps": 0, "exp_act": 0, "words": 0.1, "blame": 0},
    "800 words of padding, no steps": {"steps": 0, "exp_act": 0, "words": 8.0, "blame": 0},
}
print("\nScores for new summaries:")
for name, x in candidates.items():
    print(f"  {reward(x):+6.2f}  {name}")

Common misunderstandings
"Post-training teaches the model its knowledge."
Most knowledge comes from pre-training. Post-training mainly shapes behaviour: format, tone, judgement, refusals and honesty.
"RLHF means people rate my chats and the model updates live."
Training happens in separate runs before a model is released. Your thumbs-up does not change the model you are talking to.
"The constitution is a rulebook Claude looks up while answering."
It is used during training (including to generate training data). Its effect is in the weights, and training is imperfect.
"A refusal means the model is broken."
Sometimes it is over-refusal, a known trade-off; sometimes it's correct. Rephrase with legitimate context (for example, testing your own system) and judge the result.
"A higher reward score always means a better answer."
Only if the reward model is right. Optimise hard against any proxy and it will be gamed.

18.9Review

Key takeaways
  • Post-training turns a base model into an assistant; it uses little compute compared with pre-training but changes behaviour a lot.
  • SFT teaches by example; preference training teaches by comparison.
  • RLHF: collect preference pairs → train a reward model (Bradley–Terry: P = sigmoid of the score gap) → reinforcement learning with a drift penalty.
  • Constitutional AI (Anthropic, December 2022): self-critique and revision against written principles, then RL from AI feedback. Claude's new constitution (January 2026, CC0) prioritises broadly safe, broadly ethical, guideline-compliant, then genuinely helpful.
  • DPO uses preference pairs without a separate reward model or RL loop.
  • Helpful, honest and harmless pull against each other; failure modes include over-refusal, sycophancy and reward hacking.
Check yourself

Why do labs collect comparisons ("A is better than B") instead of only asking people to write ideal answers?

Written examples are used too (SFT), but comparisons scale much better and capture judgements that are hard to write down.

A reward model scores answer A at 3.0 and answer B at 3.0. What probability does the Bradley–Terry model give that A is preferred?

sigmoid(3.0 − 3.0) = sigmoid(0) = 0.5: equal scores mean a coin toss.

In Constitutional AI, what replaces most human harmlessness labels?

That's RLAIF: AI feedback guided by a constitution. In the 2022 paper, human labels were still used for helpfulness.

What makes Direct Preference Optimisation simpler than classic RLHF?

DPO still needs preference pairs; it just turns them into one direct training loss.

An assistant changes a correct answer as soon as you say "I think you're wrong". What is this called?

Telling users what they want to hear is a documented side effect of training on human preferences.

Assignment

Write a five-principle "constitution" for an AI that writes bug reports in your team (for example "a stranger can reproduce it", "no blame", "state severity with a reason"). Collect three real, anonymised bug reports. For each, ask Claude to critique the report against each principle and then revise it. Score the originals and revisions yourself out of 5. Which principles were most useful? Which were vague, and how would you reword them? Keep the result: it is the start of a rubric for an LLM-as-judge eval (Chapter 125).

Teach it

"After pre-training, a model knows a lot but behaves like autocomplete. Post-training turns it into an assistant. First it copies good example answers. Then it learns from comparisons: people, or an AI following written principles, pick the better of two answers, a reward model learns those preferences, and the assistant is trained to score well. Anthropic's version, Constitutional AI, uses a published constitution. The catch is that any scoring system can be gamed, so models can drift towards flattery, padding or refusing too much. That's why we test their behaviour, not just their knowledge."
