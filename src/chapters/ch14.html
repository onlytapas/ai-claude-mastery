A language model never sees letters or words the way you do. Before Claude reads your message, a program called a tokenizer cuts the text into small pieces called tokens. A token is often a whole short word, sometimes part of a longer word, a space, a digit or a punctuation mark. Each piece becomes a number. The model reads those numbers and writes its answer back as more numbers, which are turned back into text. Tokens are also the unit of everything practical: how much text fits in the model's memory, how much a request costs and how long it takes.

The easiest way to understand tokens is to see some. The splits below are illustrations: every company's tokenizer cuts text slightly differently, and even one company changes its tokenizer over time. But the patterns are the same everywhere.

In everyday life

Think of a set of children's building blocks. Some blocks are big and common: a whole wall piece, a roof. Others are small and odd-shaped, for details. A common word is like a big block: one piece does the job. A rare word, a long number or an unusual name has to be built from several small blocks. The model only ever handles blocks.

14.2Why not letters, or whole words?

Engineers had two obvious options and both have serious problems. Tokens are the compromise between them.

OptionAdvantageProblem
One letter at a timeOnly a few hundred symbols to learn; nothing is ever "unknown"Text becomes very long: "regression" is 10 steps instead of 1–2. Models are slower and see less text at once.
One whole word at a timeShort sequencesMillions of words, names, typos and new terms. Any word not in the list becomes "unknown", so a new product name or a typo breaks things.
Tokens (word pieces)Common words are one piece; rare words are built from pieces; nothing is ever unknownSplits can look odd to people, and some tasks (like counting letters) get harder

With tokens, a model can read any text at all, including a brand-new error message, a misspelt word or a language it rarely saw, while keeping most text short.

14.3Token IDs: the model's numbered dictionary

Every tokenizer comes with a fixed list of all the pieces it knows. This list is called its vocabulary. Modern vocabularies contain tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand entries. Each entry has a number, its token ID.

Your text"The test passed"
Split into tokensThe · ␣test · ␣passed
Look up IDse.g. 791 · 1296 · 5946 (made-up numbers)
ModelReads the numbers, predicts the next ID
Back to textThe predicted ID is looked up and shown as a word piece

So when people say a model "predicts the next word", it actually predicts the next token ID, one at a time. A 500-word answer is roughly 600–700 predictions in a row. Chapter 15 shows the next step: each ID is turned into a long list of numbers that captures its meaning (an embedding).

Real-world example

When you watch Claude's answer appear piece by piece in the chat, each little burst is roughly one token, or a few tokens sent together. That "typing" effect is the model's real working rhythm, not decoration.

How a tokenizer is built: byte-pair encoding, step by step

Most modern tokenizers use a method called byte-pair encoding (BPE) or a close relative. It was originally a 1994 data-compression trick, adapted for language models in 2015–16. Here is the idea with a tiny made-up example.

Start: take a training text and split it into single characters. Suppose the text is the words test, tests, tester, testing.

Step 0: everything is single characters
t e s t   t e s t s   t e s t e r   t e s t i n g

Step 1: count every pair of neighbouring pieces. t e, e s and s t each appear 4 times, more than any other pair. Pick one of the most frequent, say e s, and merge it into a new piece es. Add es to the vocabulary.

t es t   t es t s   t es t e r   t es t i n g

Step 2: count again. Now t es appears 4 times. Merge it into tes.

tes t   tes t s   tes t e r   tes t i n g

Step 3: tes t appears 4 times. Merge into test.

test   test s   test e r   test i n g

Keep going until the vocabulary reaches the size you want. With a huge training text, frequent words like the and test become single tokens, common endings like ing and ed become tokens, and rare words stay as several pieces.

Two details make this robust. First, many tokenizers start from bytes (the 0–255 values from Chapter 2) instead of letters, so any text in any language, emoji included, can always be represented, even if it costs several tokens. Second, the merges are learned once and then fixed: the same text always splits the same way with the same tokenizer.

This also explains why languages that were rare in the training text cost more tokens: fewer merges were learned for them, so their words stay in smaller pieces.

14.4Different models, different tokens

Each company trains its own tokenizer on its own data, so the same sentence gives a different count in Claude, GPT and Gemini. A company can also change its tokenizer between model generations.

Verified 9 Nov 2026

Anthropic's documentation says Claude Opus 4.7 (April 2026) and later models use a newer tokenizer, and that the same text produces approximately 30% more tokens than on earlier models; the exact increase depends on the content. Anthropic advises recounting prompts against the model you plan to use, rather than reusing counts measured on an older model.

Two practical consequences:

  • Don't compare token counts across companies. "Model A has a bigger context window than model B" only means something if you also know how many tokens each needs for your text.
  • Re-measure after a model change. A prompt that fitted comfortably and cost a known amount on an old model may be bigger and pricier on a new one.

14.5Rules of thumb

Exact counts need a tool (14.8), but rough estimates are handy for planning. Treat these as approximate:

ContentRough guide
Ordinary English proseAbout ¾ of a word per token, so 100 tokens ≈ 75 words. Newer Claude tokenizers produce more tokens, so allow extra.
A typical page of text (about 500 words)Roughly 650–900 tokens
Code, JSON, logs, stack tracesNoticeably more tokens per character than prose, because of symbols, indentation and IDs
Numbers, dates, IDs, hashesMany small tokens. A long ID or a hash can cost more tokens than a sentence.
Non-English textOften more tokens than English for the same meaning, especially in non-Latin scripts
Real-world example: a QA view

A 2,000-line test log full of timestamps, thread IDs and stack traces is far "heavier" in tokens than a 2,000-line novel. That's why it's better to have Claude search a log for the error (Claude Code can run grep) than to paste the whole thing into the conversation.

14.6Why tokens matter: space, cost and speed

1. Space: how much fits

A model's context window is measured in tokens. It holds everything in the conversation: instructions, your messages, files, tool results and the model's replies. The current Claude models (verified 9 Nov 2026) have a context window of 1 million tokens and can write up to 128,000 tokens in one reply. Chapter 20 explains how to use that space well.

2. Cost: what you pay

On the Claude API, you pay per million tokens (often written "MTok"), with a separate price for tokens you send (input) and tokens Claude writes (output). Output costs more, because each output token needs its own step of computation.

Model (Verified 9 Nov 2026)Input per MTokOutput per MTok
Claude Fable 5.1$10$50
Claude Opus 5.5$4$20
Claude Sonnet 5.5$2$10
Claude Haiku 5.5$0.10 (prompts up to 100K tokens)$0.50 (prompts up to 100K tokens)

A worked example on Sonnet 5.5: you send a 2,000-token prompt and get a 500-token reply.

Cost arithmetic
input:  2,000 tokens × $2  / 1,000,000 = $0.004
output:   500 tokens × $10 / 1,000,000 = $0.005
total:                                   $0.009   (less than one cent)

One request is cheap. A test suite that calls the API 50,000 times a night is not, which is why token budgets matter when you build AI into pipelines (Chapter 90). On a claude.ai or Claude Code subscription you don't pay per token, but your plan's usage limits are still driven by how many tokens you use.

3. Speed: how long it takes

The model writes one token after another, so a long answer takes longer than a short one. Reading input is much faster per token than writing output, but very long inputs still add time.

14.7Odd failures that tokens explain

Some famous AI "blunders" make sense once you know the model sees tokens, not letters.

FailureWhy it happens
Miscounting the letter r in "strawberry"The word may arrive as two or three tokens (for example straw + berry). The model never "sees" ten separate letters, so it has to reason its way to the count.
Reversing a word or spelling it backwardsSame reason: letters are hidden inside tokens.
Small slips with long numbersA number like 1234567 is split into chunks, so digit-by-digit arithmetic is harder without a calculator or code.
Trouble with exact character limits"Write exactly 160 characters" is hard when you work in tokens of varying length.

Modern models handle these much better than older ones, partly because they can reason step by step (Chapter 19) and, in tools like Claude Code, can simply run a small piece of code to count. The lesson for testers: when exact characters or digits matter, ask Claude to use a tool or write code, and check the result.

Two different kinds of "token"

In this chapter a token is a piece of text. You will also hear "token" used for something completely different: an access token or API token, a secret string that proves who you are to a service, like the API key in the example above, a GitHub personal access token, or a Jira API token used to connect an MCP server. AI tokens are about text and cost; access tokens are about security. Mixing them up causes real confusion in teams. Chapter 98 covers access tokens, API keys and how to keep them safe.

Common misunderstandings
"A token is a word."
Often, but not always. Long, rare and non-English words, numbers and code split into several tokens.
"Token counts are the same in every AI tool."
Each company uses its own tokenizer, and tokenizers change between model generations. Claude 4.7 and later produce about 30% more tokens for the same text than earlier Claude models.
"A bigger context window means I should paste everything."
More tokens cost more, take longer and can dilute the model's focus. Send what's relevant (Chapter 20).
"AI tokens and API tokens are the same thing."
No. AI tokens are pieces of text. API or access tokens are secret passwords for services. Never paste an access token into a chat.
"If it can't count letters, it can't reason."
Letter-counting trips over how text is split, not general reasoning. Give the model a tool and it gets it right.

14.9Review

Key takeaways
  • A tokenizer cuts text into tokens: common words are one token; rare words, numbers, code and many non-English words are several.
  • Tokens balance the problems of letters (too long) and whole words (too many, and unknown words).
  • Each token has an ID in the model's vocabulary; the model reads and predicts token IDs one at a time.
  • Tokenizers are learned from data, often with byte-pair encoding, so every company's tokenizer differs, and Claude 4.7+ produce about 30% more tokens than earlier Claude models.
  • Tokens set how much fits (1M-token context for current Claude models), what it costs (priced per million tokens, output dearer than input) and how long it takes.
  • Count with /context and /usage in Claude Code, or the API's token-counting endpoint.
  • AI tokens (text pieces) are completely different from access tokens (secrets).
Check yourself

Which of these is most likely to use the most tokens?

Timestamps, IDs and hex strings break into many small tokens.

Why do language models use tokens rather than whole words?

Word pieces avoid "unknown word" problems without making text as long as letter-by-letter reading.

You move a prompt from an older Claude model to Claude Opus 5.5. What should you expect?

Claude 4.7 and later use a newer tokenizer that produces about 30% more tokens for the same text.

On the Claude API, which costs more per token?

For example, Sonnet 5.5 is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens (verified 9 Nov 2026).

A colleague says "I pasted my Jira token into Claude to save tokens." What's wrong?

An access token is a password-like secret. Keep it out of chats; Chapter 98 explains how to handle it safely.

Teach it

"AI models don't read words; they read tokens, which are small pieces of text. Common words are one token, while rare words, numbers, code and other languages are several. Every token is a number in the model's vocabulary, and the model predicts the next one, one at a time. Tokens decide how much fits, what it costs and how fast it is. And don't confuse them with API tokens: those are passwords."
