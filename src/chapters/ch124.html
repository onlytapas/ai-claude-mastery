Exploratory testing is where a tester's curiosity and judgement matter most, so AI doesn't replace it. It makes you faster around the edges. Claude can suggest areas to explore, take notes while you test, drive a browser to check ideas quickly, read a 5,000-line log in seconds, help you narrow down the exact steps that trigger a bug, and draft a clear bug report. You still decide what's worth exploring, whether something is really a bug, and how serious it is.

124.1What exploratory testing is, and where AI fits

Exploratory testing means learning about the product, designing tests and running them all at the same time, guided by what you discover. It isn't random clicking. A good exploratory session has a mission (a "charter"), a time box, and notes on what was covered and found.

PlanPick a charter: what to explore and why
ExploreTry things, follow surprises, vary inputs
InvestigateSomething odd? Narrow it down, collect evidence
ReportWrite the bug, notes and coverage

In everyday life

Think of a detective with a very fast research assistant. The assistant can pull records, read hundreds of pages and draft notes in minutes. But the detective decides which leads are worth following, interviews the witnesses, and stands up in court. In exploratory testing, you're the detective and Claude is the assistant.

124.2Generating charters with heuristics, tours and personas

A charter is a short mission statement for a session. A common format is: Explore [target] with [resources] to discover [information]. For example: "Explore the checkout with expired, invalid and stacked coupons to discover pricing errors."

Testers use heuristics (rules of thumb) to think of things to try. Claude knows the well-known ones and can apply them to your feature, which is a great way to avoid blind spots.

HeuristicWhat it meansExample for a checkout page
SFDIPOT ("San Francisco Depot")A checklist of product elements, from James Bach's Heuristic Test Strategy Model: Structure, Function, Data, Interfaces, Platform, Operations, TimeTime: what happens if a coupon expires while the cart is open?
ToursExplore the product as if on a themed tour, an idea popularised by James Whittaker"Money tour": follow every path where money changes hands
PersonasExplore as a specific kind of userA first-time buyer on a slow phone; a power user with 200 items
Boundaries and odditiesEdges, empty values, huge values, special charactersQuantity 0, 1, 999, -1; a name with an emoji or an apostrophe
InterruptionsBreak the flowBack button during payment; two tabs; session timeout
Prompt: generate charters
Here is the user story and acceptance criteria for the new coupon feature:
<story>
...paste the story...
</story>

Act as an experienced exploratory tester.
1. Using SFDIPOT, list the risks you see for this feature.
2. Turn the top 6 risks into session charters in the form
   "Explore <target> with <resources> to discover <information>".
3. For each charter, suggest 3 concrete things to try first,
   one persona to test as, and how long the session should be.
Don't invent requirements: if something is unclear, list it as a question.
Real-world example

A tester gets a story for "apply a discount code at checkout". Claude's SFDIPOT pass flags something the story never mentions: what happens to the discount if the customer removes an item and drops below the minimum spend? That becomes a charter, and the session finds the discount stays applied. That's a real pricing bug, found because the AI widened the tester's thinking, not because it tested anything itself.

124.3Letting AI explore: Playwright MCP as a test partner

With the Playwright MCP server connected (Chapter 102), Claude can open a browser, click, type and read the page. That makes it useful for quickly checking many variations you'd find tedious by hand.

Prompt: a guided exploration
Using the Playwright browser, open http://localhost:3000/checkout as the
test user in our CLAUDE.md. Charter: explore coupon codes to discover pricing errors.
Try: an expired code, a lowercase version of a valid code, a code with spaces
around it, two codes in a row, and removing items after applying a code.
After each attempt, record: what you did, the total shown, any message shown.
Don't submit any real payment. Stop and ask me if anything needs a real card.
Finish with a table of attempts and anything that looked inconsistent.

What an AI explorer is good at

  • Trying many input variations quickly and consistently
  • Recording exactly what it did (great session notes)
  • Reading page text, error messages and the structure of forms
  • Spotting inconsistencies between what the page says and the spec you gave it
  • Checking accessibility basics like missing labels, using the accessibility tree

What it tends to miss

  • Things that "look wrong": layout glitches, overlapping elements, odd colours (it reads structure, not pixels, unless it takes and studies screenshots)
  • Timing problems a human feels, like a sluggish button or flicker
  • Business context it wasn't given: "that price is obviously wrong for this customer"
  • Real-device behaviour: touch, slow networks, assistive technology
  • Anything outside the charter you gave it
A good split

Let Claude run the wide, repetitive variations while you spend your own attention on the hunches, the odd behaviour and the "what would a real customer do here?" questions. Then compare notes.

124.4Session-based testing with AI note-taking

Session-based test management (SBTM) gives exploratory testing structure: time-boxed sessions, each with a charter, notes, bugs found, issues (questions and blockers) and a debrief. The tedious part is the notes. Claude is excellent at turning rough notes into a clean session report.

Prompt: turn rough notes into a session report
Here are my rough notes from a 60-minute exploratory session:
<notes>
10:02 started on staging build 4.12.0, user qa_buyer_03
coupons: SAVE10 ok. save10 lowercase -> "invalid code"?? spec says case-insensitive
removed 2nd item, total < £50 min spend, discount still applied!!
checked w/ product (Priya) - min spend should be enforced. bug.
back btn during payment -> blank page, couldn't repro 2nd time
...
</notes>

Write a session report with these sections: Charter, Build and environment,
Areas covered, Bugs (with one-line summaries), Issues and questions,
Not covered / next charters. Keep my facts exactly; don't add anything I didn't note.
Mark the back-button issue as "not yet reproducible".

The last two instructions matter. You're asking Claude to organise, not to invent. Always reread the report against your notes.

124.5Investigating a bug: narrowing it down

Finding a bug is half the job. The other half is making it easy to fix: the shortest reliable steps, the evidence, and ideally where in the system it comes from. Claude can speed up each part.

Narrowing the reproduction steps

Start from the long, messy path you took and ask Claude (with the browser connected) to find the smallest version that still fails.

Prompt: minimise the repro
I can reproduce this bug with the 11 steps below. Using the Playwright browser,
try removing steps one at a time to find the shortest sequence that still
shows the discount applied below the minimum spend. Report each attempt and
the final minimal steps. Use the test account only.

Reading logs and stack traces

A stack trace is the list of functions a program was in when it crashed, from the innermost upwards. It looks frightening but follows a pattern, and Claude reads them well.

An example error from a server log
2026-11-09T10:14:22Z ERROR checkout.pricing  CouponValidationError: min_spend check skipped
  at applyCoupon (src/pricing/coupons.ts:88:11)
  at recalculateCart (src/cart/cart.ts:142:5)
  at removeItem (src/cart/cart.ts:97:3)
  at CartController.delete (src/api/cart.controller.ts:41:12)
Prompt: explain the trace
Here is an error from the staging log (above). In plain words:
1. What was the system doing when this happened? Read the stack from the bottom up.
2. Which file and line is the most likely place to look first, and why?
3. Search the logs/ folder for other occurrences between 10:00 and 10:30 and
   tell me if they all follow a "remove item" action.
Don't change any code.

Read from the bottom: the user deleted an item (removeItem), the cart recalculated, and coupon validation ran but skipped its minimum-spend check. That gives the developer a precise starting point, coupons.ts line 88, and fits your exploratory finding.

Finding the change that caused it, with git bisect

If a bug appeared recently, the fastest way to find the cause is often to find which change introduced it. git bisect does this by binary search: you tell Git one version that's good and one that's bad, and it checks out the commit halfway between. You test it and say "good" or "bad", and it halves the range again. Even across 1,000 commits, it needs only about 10 checks.

Terminal
git bisect start
git bisect bad                 # the current version has the bug
git bisect good v4.11.0        # this release didn't
# Git checks out a commit in the middle. Test it, then:
git bisect good                # or: git bisect bad
# ...repeat until Git names the first bad commit...
git bisect reset               # go back to where you started

If you have an automated test that shows the bug, Git can run it for you at each step with git bisect run followed by the test command, which exits with 0 for good and non-zero for bad. In Claude Code you can ask: "Use git bisect between v4.11.0 and HEAD, running this Playwright test at each step, to find the commit that introduced the bug. Show me the commit and explain the change." Approve the commands as it goes, and check the final answer by reading the named commit's diff.

124.6Writing excellent bug reports

A great bug report gets fixed quickly because the developer can reproduce it on the first try and understands why it matters. Claude is very good at turning messy findings into this shape, especially with a team skill that holds your format (Chapter 80).

Why "TO CONFIRM" matters

Asked to fill a template, a model may fill the gaps with plausible guesses: a browser version, a build number. Telling it to mark missing facts explicitly turns a hidden risk into a visible to-do.

124.7Duplicates, severity and priority

Checking for duplicates with Jira MCP

With the Jira connection from Chapter 101, Claude can search existing issues before you file a new one:

Prompt: duplicate check
Before I file this bug, search Jira project SHOP for open or recently closed
issues about coupons, discounts or minimum spend from the last 90 days.
List likely duplicates with key, title, status and why they might match.
Don't create or change any issue.

Similar wording isn't the same bug. Read the candidates yourself. Two reports with similar titles can have different causes; two with different titles can be the same defect.

Severity and priority stay human

Severity is how bad the impact is; priority is how soon it should be fixed. Claude can suggest them with reasons, which is useful as a second opinion, but the decision needs context it usually lacks: how many customers use this path, whether a release is due, contractual or legal exposure, and what the business cares about this week. Record the human decision, not the model's suggestion.

124.8When AI "finds" bugs that aren't real

This is the biggest risk in AI-assisted bug hunting. A model asked to look for problems is inclined to find some. It may:

  • Misread the spec and report correct behaviour as a bug.
  • Report something it didn't actually observe, such as a message it expected to see. This is a form of hallucination.
  • Confuse its own mistakes with product bugs: clicking the wrong element, typing into the wrong field, or reading a page before it finished loading.
  • Overstate severity because "critical" sounds more helpful.
  • Be steered by text on the page: content in the app could contain instructions that mislead it (prompt injection, Chapter 131).
The rule

No bug is filed until a person has reproduced it. Ask Claude for evidence with every finding (the exact steps, the page text or screenshot, the log line) and reproduce it yourself before it goes into Jira. A false bug report costs a developer time and costs the QA team trust.

Prompt: make it prove its findings
For each problem you reported, give: the exact steps you performed, the exact
text or element you saw (quote it), and whether you saw it once or repeatedly.
If you're inferring rather than observing, say "inferred". Then re-run each one
and tell me which ones you could reproduce.

Common misunderstandings
"AI can do exploratory testing for me."
It can run variations and take notes. Exploration is driven by curiosity, context and judgement, and those remain yours.
"If Claude says it saw an error, it did."
Ask for the quoted text, steps and repeat runs. Reproduce before filing.
"Similar titles mean duplicate bugs."
Duplicate detection gives candidates. A person decides whether they share a cause.
"A long, detailed report is a good report."
The best reports are short: minimal steps, clear expected vs actual, evidence and impact.

124.9Review

Key takeaways
  • Exploratory testing is structured learning: charter, time box, notes, debrief. Claude speeds up each stage.
  • Use heuristics like SFDIPOT, tours and personas with Claude to generate charters and widen your thinking.
  • Playwright MCP lets Claude run many variations quickly; it's weaker at visual, timing and business-context problems.
  • For investigation, Claude can minimise reproduction steps, explain stack traces, search logs and help with git bisect.
  • Draft bug reports with Claude (ideally via a team skill), marking missing facts as "TO CONFIRM".
  • Duplicate checks, severity and priority are suggestions; people decide.
  • Nothing is filed until a person has reproduced it.
Check yourself

What is a test charter?

"Explore [target] with [resources] to discover [information]."

Which kind of problem is an AI exploring through Playwright MCP most likely to miss?

It mostly reads the page's structure, not its visual layout, unless it takes and studies screenshots.

How should you read a stack trace?

The bottom shows where the action started; the top shows where it failed.

Claude reports a "critical" bug in the checkout. What do you do first?

AI findings can be real or invented. Reproduce before filing, every time.

What does git bisect help you find?

It binary-searches the history between a good and a bad version.

Assignment

Run two exploratory sessions on the same feature in your real work: one on your own, and one with Claude as a partner (charters, browser variations, notes and investigation). For each, record time spent, bugs found, bugs that turned out not to be real, and coverage notes. Write half a page on what changed. This becomes evidence for the value discussion in Chapter 126.

Teach it

"Use Claude as an exploration partner, not a replacement. It suggests charters with heuristics like SFDIPOT, tries lots of input variations in the browser, takes notes, reads logs and stack traces, and drafts bug reports. You decide what's worth exploring, what's really a bug and how serious it is. And the golden rule: nothing gets filed until a person has reproduced it."
