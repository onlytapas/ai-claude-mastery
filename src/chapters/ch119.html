Once you know what Claude can do, the next question is "what should my team actually use it for?" This chapter is the answer in the form of a catalogue: over seventy ideas, organised by testing stage, each with the Claude feature to use, a rough rating of the effort it saves and the coverage it adds, and how hard it is to start. Use it three ways: browse it for inspiration, filter it for your current problem, and bring it to a team workshop to choose your first experiments. The goal isn't to do all of them. It's to find the five that make the biggest difference for your team.

119.1Where AI helps across the testing lifecycle

AI can help at every stage of testing, not just automation. In fact the biggest gains often come early, when finding a gap in a requirement prevents a bug from ever being written. That's the idea behind shift-left testing, and AI makes it far cheaper.

RefineFind gaps in stories before development
PlanRisks, strategy, estimates
DesignTest cases, data, techniques
BuildAutomation, API tests, tooling
RunExploratory, non-functional, CI
LearnTriage, reporting, knowledge

Each stage has a "Claude-shaped" opportunity. In early stages it's mostly reading and reasoning (claude.ai Projects, or Claude reading Jira through MCP). In middle stages it's producing and running code (Claude Code, Playwright). In later stages it's summarising and spotting patterns (headless runs in CI, Artifacts for reports).

The Low/Med/High ratings are the author's rough guidance for a typical web-product QA team, not measured data. 119.5Example prompts for five ideas

#1 Ambiguity hunt (claude.ai Project holding the product docs)
Here is user story CHK-412 and its acceptance criteria:
<story> …paste… </story>
Act as a senior tester in refinement. List:
1. Ambiguous or untestable statements, quoting each one.
2. Missing scenarios (errors, limits, permissions, concurrency).
3. Contradictions with the product docs in this Project.
Phrase each as a question I can ask the product owner. Don't write test cases yet.
#11 Boundaries and partitions
The "age" field accepts whole numbers from 18 to 65; "postcode" must match
the UK format. For each field, list the equivalence partitions and boundary
values, then give test cases in a table: ID, input, partition, expected result.
Mark anything where the requirement doesn't tell you the expected result.
#18 Synthetic test data (Claude Code, in the test repository)
Write a script in scripts/ that generates 200 fake customers as CSV for our
test environment. Include: names with accents and apostrophes, very long
addresses, empty optional fields, customers in three time zones, and 5 with
duplicate emails in different cases. No real people or real email domains.
Run it once and show me the first 10 rows.
#51 Smart regression selection (Claude Code)
Compare this branch with main. List the changed files, explain in plain words
what behaviour each change could affect, and map them to our test suites in
tests/. Recommend which suites to run before merge and which can wait for the
nightly run. Explain your reasoning; don't run anything yet.
#64 An eval set for an AI feature
We're testing a support chatbot that answers questions from our help centre.
Draft 15 eval cases: 5 normal questions, 5 edge cases (vague, misspelt,
two questions in one), 5 adversarial (asks for other customers' data, tries
to override instructions). For each give: input, what a good answer must do,
what would count as a failure.

119.6Choosing: the impact × effort matrix

Seventy ideas are too many to try at once. A simple impact-effort matrix helps a team agree on where to start. Score each candidate idea for impact (effort saved plus coverage gained, for your team) and effort (setup, learning, approvals), then place it in one of four boxes.

High impact, low effort · Do first

Your quick wins. Start two or three of these this sprint.

High impact, high effort · Plan

Worth it, but needs time: CI integration, MCP connections, a custom server. Plan them as small projects with an owner.

Low impact, low effort · Fill in

Nice to have. Do them when someone has a spare hour, or as practice.

Low impact, high effort · Skip for now

Revisit later. Your product or setup may change their value.

Scoring ideas more carefully

For a more careful choice, score each idea from 1 to 5 on four questions and multiply impact by confidence, then divide by effort:

QuestionScore 1Score 5
Impact: how much time or risk does it address?Minutes a monthDays a sprint, or a major risk
Reach: how many people or releases benefit?One person, onceWhole team, every release
Confidence: how sure are we it'll work here?A guessWe've seen it work
Effort: how much work to set up and maintain?An hourWeeks, plus approvals

Score = (Impact × Reach × Confidence) ÷ Effort. The numbers aren't precise; their value is in making the team discuss the same questions. Confidence also tells you what to test first: a quick, cheap experiment that raises confidence is often the best next step.

119.7Running a brainstorm workshop with your team

The ideas that stick are the ones the team chooses. A 90-minute workshop is enough to get from "AI could help us" to three agreed experiments with owners.

MinutesStepWhat happens
0–10FrameState the goal: "Pick three experiments that save effort or add coverage, to try for two weeks." Remind everyone of the company AI policy.
10–25Pain pointsEach person writes their three most time-consuming or frustrating QA tasks on sticky notes (physical or digital). Group similar ones.
25–45Match ideasFor the top pain points, look up matching ideas in this catalogue (filter by stage). Add your own ideas too.
45–65PrioritisePlace candidates on the impact × effort matrix together. Discuss disagreements; they usually reveal hidden effort or hidden value.
65–80Design experimentsFor the top three: owner, what you'll try, how you'll measure it (time before vs after, cases found), and what "success" means.
80–90CommitAgree a date to share results. Record everything in a shared page or an Artifact.
Real-world example (illustrative)

A five-person web QA team runs this workshop. Their top pain points are "writing regression test cases", "preparing test data for each environment" and "chasing unclear requirements". They choose three experiments: an ambiguity hunt on every story before refinement (#1), a synthetic data generator (#18) and a bug-report skill (#46). Two weeks later they compare time spent with the previous sprint and decide to keep two, rework one, and try the next three ideas from their matrix. This example is illustrative, not a measured case study; Chapter 129 shows more before-and-after scenarios.

Common misunderstandings
"We should start with the most impressive idea."
Start with quick wins. Early, visible successes build trust and skills for the bigger projects.
"AI is mainly for test automation."
Some of the largest gains are in requirements, test design, data and reporting, before and after automation.
"The ratings tell us what we'll save."
They're rough guidance. Measure your own before-and-after times (Chapter 126).
"Every idea needs MCP or coding."
About a third of the catalogue works with a chat or a Project alone. Start there if your setup is limited.
"Once chosen, an idea is done."
Treat each one as an experiment: try it, measure it, then keep, adjust or drop it.

119.8Review

Key takeaways
  • AI helps at every testing stage; some of the biggest gains come early, in requirements and test design.
  • Each idea maps to a specific Claude feature: chat and Projects for reasoning, Claude Code for code and data, MCP for real tools, headless mode and scheduling for CI, the API for your own tools.
  • Start with quick wins (high impact, low effort), then plan the bigger projects.
  • Use the impact × effort matrix and a 90-minute workshop to choose experiments together.
  • Treat every idea as an experiment and measure it; the ratings here are only rough guidance.

Assignment

Run the 90-minute workshop from 110.7 with at least two colleagues (or, if you're on your own, with Claude playing the other roles). Produce: the pain-point list, the impact × effort matrix, and three experiments, each with an owner, a measure and a review date. Share it with your lead. In two weeks, record the results; you'll use them in Chapter 126 to build a value case.

Teach it

"There are dozens of ways Claude can help QA, at every stage from requirements to reporting, not just automation. Each one fits a specific feature: a Project for reasoning over documents, Claude Code for scripts and data, MCP for Jira and Playwright, headless mode for CI. Start with quick wins like the ambiguity hunt, boundary values and synthetic data, pick the next ones with an impact-effort matrix, and measure every experiment."
