Most office work follows the same chain: gather information, make sense of it, write it up, and share it with people who need to decide something. Claude can help with every link in that chain, and it is fastest where the work is reading, summarising, restructuring and drafting. It is weakest where a single wrong number or invented fact does real damage. So the skill is not "ask AI to do the report". It is: give Claude the right material, ask for work you can check, check the parts that matter, and stay the author of anything you send.

116.1The business work chain

Whether you are a tester writing a release report, an analyst preparing a monthly pack or a manager preparing for a steering meeting, the work has four stages. AI helps differently at each one.

GatherFind and collect: exports, documents, notes, web sources
AnalyseCount, compare, spot patterns and outliers
WriteTurn findings into a report, email, document or slides
Share and decidePresent, answer questions, record decisions
StageWhere Claude helps mostWhere to be careful
GatherSearching the web with citations, reading long PDFs, pulling notes togetherSources it summarises badly, or sources that don't say what the summary claims

WriteFirst drafts, restructuring, tone, shortening, tailoring for different readersConfident wording that overstates what the data shows
Share and decideSlides, speaker notes, likely questions, action lists from meetingsAction items assigned to the wrong person; decisions recorded that were never made
In everyday life

Think of Claude as a very fast, well-read new colleague in their first week. They can draft the report by lunchtime and it will read beautifully. But they don't yet know which numbers your director always questions, which system's export is unreliable, or that "Payments" was renamed last quarter. You would read their work before it went to the board. Do the same here.

116.2Analysing data and spreadsheets

The most common business request is "here's a spreadsheet, what does it tell me?" Chapter 46 covers what you can upload and the file limits. Here we focus on doing the analysis well.

When you upload a CSV or Excel file and code execution is switched on, Claude usually writes and runs a short program to read the file, rather than reading the numbers like text. That matters: a program counts every row exactly, while a language model "reading" a long table can lose its place, just as you might if you tried to add up 2,000 numbers by eye.

A five-step routine

  1. Describe the data first. Ask: "Before analysing, describe this file: columns, row count, date range, blanks and anything odd." This catches the wrong file, a header row in the middle, or dates stored as text.
  2. State the question and the reader. "Our release manager needs to know whether open defects are trending down and which component is riskiest."
  3. Ask for the method, not just the answer. "Show the calculation you used and the code." You can then see whether "open" meant status Open, or status Open plus Reopened.
  4. Check the headline numbers independently. A pivot table, a filter in Excel, or a ten-line script (the lab below has one). Check the two or three figures that will drive a decision, not every number.
  5. Ask what the data can't tell you. "What would make this conclusion wrong? What's missing?" Good analysts always state limits.
Real-world example: the vanishing defects

A test lead asked an AI assistant to summarise a 600-row defect export. The summary said "37 critical defects open". The real number was 41. Four rows had "critical" in lower case and the analysis grouped by exact text. Nothing was invented; the method was just slightly wrong. Asking for the code would have shown the grouping, and a quick filter in the spreadsheet caught it. This kind of quiet error is far more common than wild hallucination.

Under the hood: why "show your code" is the single best habit

A language model produces text one token at a time. When it states "the average is 3.4" without running code, it is predicting what a plausible answer looks like, which is often right for small tables and unreliable for large ones (Chapter 22). When it runs code, the arithmetic is done by an ordinary program and is exact, so the remaining risk moves to the method: which rows, which columns, which definition. Methods are written in the code, so you can read them. That shifts your checking job from "redo all the maths" to "is this the right recipe?", which is much quicker and something a non-programmer can learn to do by reading the comments.

Working inside Excel

If your organisation uses Microsoft 365 and has the Claude add-ins (paid plans; Excel launched in beta in November 2025), you can work on a workbook in place rather than uploading a copy. The same routine applies: ask it to explain formulas it writes, and check the key cells. Availability and features vary by plan and admin settings; see Chapter 51.

116.3Reports that people read

Most business reports fail not because the numbers are wrong but because nobody can find the point. Claude is very good at restructuring, so use it to make reports answer the reader's question first.

The shape of a useful status report

PartWhat goes in itQA example
HeadlineOne sentence: the decision or status"Release 4.2 is ready to ship, with one known risk in Checkout."
WhyThree to five facts that support itPass rate, open criticals, coverage of changed areas
RisksWhat could go wrong, how likely, what we're doingBUG-105 workaround documented; monitoring added
AsksDecisions or help needed, with a date"Product to accept BUG-105 risk by Thursday."
DetailTables and charts, for those who want themDefects by component and severity
Prompt: from analysis to a one-page report
Using the defect analysis above, write a one-page release-readiness
report for our release manager and product owner.

Structure: headline (one sentence), why (3-5 bullet facts with numbers),
risks (table: risk, likelihood, impact, mitigation), asks (who, what, by when),
then the detail tables.

Rules:
- Use only numbers from the analysis. If a number isn't there, write [CHECK].
- Don't use words like "significant" unless you give the number.
- Plain English, no jargon, British spelling.
- End with a short "Limits of this data" paragraph.

Two rules in that prompt do most of the work. "Use only numbers from the analysis… write [CHECK]" stops Claude filling gaps with plausible figures. "Don't use words like significant unless you give the number" stops vague, confident wording that a director might act on.

The tone trap

AI drafts often sound more certain and more positive than the facts justify. "Quality has improved substantially" from a sample of 20 defects is overstatement. Before sending, read every adjective and adverb and ask: "Could I defend this word in the meeting?"

116.4Documents and slides

Claude can create real Word, Excel, PowerPoint and PDF files (Chapter 46 has the details), and it can build interactive pages as artifacts (Chapter 48). For business documents, the useful question is which output fits the reader.

You needGood outputTip
Something to edit with colleagues in Word or Google DocsA .docx fileGive it your template's headings; ask for styles, not manual bold
A deck for a meetingA .pptx file, or the PowerPoint add-inAsk for one message per slide and speaker notes; keep numbers on slides few and checked
A table others will filter or recalculateAn .xlsx file with formulasAsk for formulas, not pasted values, so others can audit them
A dashboard or checklist people click throughAn artifactGood for living tools; check who can see it before sharing
A quick summary in chat or emailPlain textOften the best option. Not everything needs a file

Slides: one message per slide

Ask for the storyline before the slides. "Give me the titles of eight slides, each a full sentence stating the point" produces a deck people can follow from the titles alone. Then ask for content and speaker notes. Finally, ask: "What three questions will the audience ask, and which slide answers each?" It is a cheap rehearsal.

Real-world example: the steering committee deck

A QA manager needs five slides for a quarterly steering committee on test automation. She uploads last quarter's deck, the automation dashboard export and her rough notes, and asks for a storyline first. Claude proposes titles such as "Automated regression now runs in 40 minutes, down from 3 hours (illustrative)". She corrects two titles that misread the dashboard, then asks for the .pptx in the old deck's structure. Total time: under an hour instead of an afternoon, and she checked every number on the slides against the dashboard herself.

116.5Research with sources

Business research means answering questions such as "What are competitors doing about accessibility?" or "What does the new regulation require of us?" Claude has two modes for this (Chapter 49): web search, which runs a few searches and answers with citations, and Research mode (paid plans), which runs many connected searches and writes a longer report.

FrameQuestion, scope, time window, what "good evidence" means
SearchWeb search or Research mode, with citations
VerifyOpen the cited sources for any claim you'll repeat
SynthesiseYour conclusions, separated from the evidence

The weak point is the third box. A citation shows where Claude looked, not that the source says exactly what the summary says. For any claim that will appear in a decision document, open the source and find the sentence. This is the same discipline as checking a test's evidence before closing a bug.

Regulation and legal questions

Claude can help you read and summarise a regulation and draft questions for your legal team. It is not legal advice, and summaries of laws can miss exceptions that matter. Chapter 133 covers AI law; for your organisation's obligations, ask your legal or compliance team.

Prompt: a research brief that is easy to check
Research question: How are large retailers testing website accessibility
against WCAG 2.2? Time window: publications from 2025 onwards.

Give me:
1. A summary of no more than 200 words.
2. A table: claim | source title | URL | date | how strong the evidence is
   (official standard, company statement, news report, opinion).
3. A list of what you could NOT find or verify.
Keep your opinions in a separate section labelled "Interpretation".

116.6Meeting notes and actions

Turning a meeting transcript or rough notes into decisions and actions is one of the highest-value, lowest-risk uses of AI, as long as someone who attended checks the result.

Prompt: from transcript to actions
Here is the transcript of today's defect triage meeting.

Produce:
- Decisions made (only if explicitly agreed; quote the line that shows it)
- Actions: owner | action | due date (write "no owner stated" if none)
- Open questions nobody answered
- A 3-sentence summary for people who missed it

Do not invent owners or dates. If something is unclear, say so.

"Quote the line that shows it" is the key instruction. It forces each decision to be tied to evidence in the transcript, so the person checking can confirm it in seconds.

Consent and recording

Recording and transcribing people may need their consent, and your organisation may have rules about which tools may process meeting content. Check before you upload a recording or transcript, especially with external participants or HR topics.

116.7Team knowledge

Every team has knowledge scattered across wikis, chat threads and people's heads. Claude can help in three ways.

Answering from your documents

A Project holding your test strategy, environments guide and glossary lets colleagues ask "Which environment has production-like data?" and get an answer grounded in your documents (Chapter 47). Connectors can reach live sources such as Google Drive or Confluence where your admin allows (Chapter 49).

Writing the knowledge down

Ask Claude to interview you: "Ask me one question at a time to document how we run regression on release day." Then turn the answers into a how-to page. This captures knowledge that otherwise leaves when a person does.

The third way is maintenance: "Here are our onboarding guide and last month's chat thread about the new VPN. What in the guide is now out of date?" Stale documentation is a quality problem, and spotting contradictions between two texts is something AI does well.

Opinion · common among knowledge-management practitioners

Many practitioners argue that AI makes good documentation more valuable, not less: an assistant answering from a messy, contradictory wiki will confidently repeat the mess. Their advice is to tidy and date the core documents before pointing an assistant at them. This is practitioner experience, not a measured finding.

116.9Data privacy: what never goes in

Business data is often sensitive. The rules for what Claude keeps and whether it trains on your data depend on whether you use a consumer plan (Free, Pro, Max, Ultra) or a commercial one (Team, Enterprise, API). Chapter 42 sets out the current policies and Chapter 132 goes deeper. Whatever the plan, your organisation's policy comes first.

Usually fine (on an approved tool)Needs care or maskingNever, unless explicitly approved
Public information; your own drafts; anonymised defect counts; test data you createdInternal plans and roadmaps; meeting transcripts; customer feedback with names removedCustomer personal data; passwords, API keys and other secrets; health or financial records; unreleased financial results; anything under a confidentiality agreement

Two habits remove most of the risk. First, mask before you paste: replace names, emails and account numbers with placeholders such as CUSTOMER_1. Second, use the organisation's approved account, never a personal one, for work material.

Personal accounts for work

Using a personal Free or Pro account for work documents is one of the most common ways company data ends up somewhere it shouldn't. Even if the content seems harmless, it bypasses your organisation's retention settings, audit logs and contracts.

  2. Create check_defects.py in the same folder:
    check_defects.py
    """Recalculate the defect figures so you can check Claude's analysis."""
import csv
import sys
from collections import Counter
from datetime import date

path = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "defects.csv"
with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    rows = list(csv.DictReader(f))

print(f"Total defects: {len(rows)}")
status = Counter(r["status"] for r in rows)
print("By status:", dict(sorted(status.items())))

open_rows = [r for r in rows if r["status"] == "Open"]
print("Open by severity:", dict(sorted(Counter(r["severity"] for r in open_rows).items())))
print("Open by component:", dict(Counter(r["component"] for r in open_rows).most_common()))

days = []
for r in rows:
    if r["closed"]:
        d = date.fromisoformat(r["closed"]) - date.fromisoformat(r["opened"])
        days.append(d.days)
print(f"Closed defects: {len(days)}, average days to close: {sum(days) / len(days):.1f}")

Common misunderstandings
"If Claude gives a number, it calculated it."
Only if it ran code. Otherwise it predicted a plausible number. Ask to see the method.
"A citation means the source supports the claim."
It means the source was consulted. Open it and find the sentence before you repeat a claim in a decision document.
"The polished report is the finished report."
Polish and correctness are different things. AI writing tends to sound certain; your job is to check numbers, wording and decisions.
"It's only a summary, so privacy doesn't matter."
The full content you uploaded was processed, not just the summary. Mask sensitive data and use your organisation's approved account.
"Meeting notes from AI are neutral."
They can record a discussion as a decision or assign an action to the wrong person. Ask for supporting quotes and have an attendee check.

116.10Review

Key takeaways
  • Business work is a chain: gather, analyse, write, share and decide. Claude helps at every stage, most of all with reading, restructuring and drafting.
  • For data, describe the file first, ask for the method and code, and independently check the two or three numbers that drive a decision.
  • Good reports answer the reader's question first: headline, why, risks, asks, then detail. Tell Claude to mark missing numbers rather than fill them.
  • Choose the output that fits the reader: plain text, .docx, .pptx, .xlsx with formulas, or an artifact.
  • Citations show where Claude looked; verify any claim you will repeat.
  • Meeting notes need supporting quotes and a human check.
  • Mask sensitive data, use approved accounts, and follow your organisation's policy over any general advice.

Assignment

Pick one recurring report you or your team produce (a weekly test status, a sprint quality summary, a monthly defect pack). Time how long it takes today. Next time, use the routine from this chapter: describe the data, ask for the method, check the key numbers, draft with the [CHECK] rule, and remove unsupported adjectives. Record the time taken, every correction you made, and which corrections would have mattered if missed. Write a half-page note for your manager on whether to adopt the routine. (To turn this into a measured business case, use Chapter 126.)

Teach it

"Most office work is gather, analyse, write, share. Claude speeds up all four, but the risk is quiet errors, not wild ones: a filter that drops four rows, an adjective that oversells, a decision nobody actually made. So: make it describe the data first, ask it to show its code, check the two or three numbers that matter, tell it to write [CHECK] instead of guessing, and keep customer data out unless you're on an approved account with masking. You stay the author."
