A benchmark is a standard exam for AI: a fixed set of tasks, a fixed way of scoring, so different models can be compared. Benchmarks drive the field, and every model launch quotes them. But they age. Questions leak into training data (contamination), models reach the top score (saturation), and once a number becomes a target it stops measuring what it was meant to (Goodhart's law). Scores also depend heavily on settings, and small gaps are often within the noise. Benchmarks tell you roughly where a model stands in general; only your own evals tell you whether it works for your task.

24.1What a benchmark is

A benchmark has three parts:

  1. A dataset of tasks: questions, coding problems, puzzles or real-world jobs.
  2. A scoring rule: exact match, unit tests passing, a human vote, a grading model.
  3. A protocol: how the model is run (prompt format, number of attempts, tools allowed, time limits).

Change any of the three and the score changes. That last part, the protocol, is the one launch announcements mention least.

In everyday life

A driving test is a benchmark for drivers. It's standard, so results are comparable, and passing tells you something real. But it doesn't tell you whether someone can drive a lorry through Mumbai at night, or whether they memorised the test route. And if driving schools teach only the test route, pass rates go up while road safety doesn't. Every problem in this chapter is a version of that.

Benchmark

Public, standardised, shared across the industry. Answers "how does this model compare with others in general?" Example: SWE-bench Pro.

Eval

Your own test set for your own task, often private. Answers "does this model do our job well enough?" Example: 50 of your team's Jira stories with expected test cases (Chapter 125).

Why no scores here

This table deliberately gives no model scores. They change with every release, are often self-reported, and depend on settings (section 24.3). For current numbers, read the model's own system card or announcement and an independent leaderboard, and compare the two (Chapter 33).

Under the hood: how SWE-bench grades a fix

Each SWE-bench task is built from a real pull request in a public Python project. The task gives the model the repository as it was before the fix and the text of the GitHub issue. The model must produce a code change (a patch). The grader then applies the patch and runs the tests that the real developers added in that pull request, which the model never sees. The task counts as resolved only if those "fail-to-pass" tests now pass and the existing "pass-to-pass" tests still pass.

For a tester this is familiar: it's a hidden acceptance test plus a regression suite. It's also why SWE-bench was so influential: grading is objective. Its weaknesses are just as familiar: if the hidden tests are too narrow, a wrong fix can pass; if they're too specific, a correct alternative fix can fail. Those flawed tasks, plus the public availability of the original fixes (contamination), are the reasons OpenAI gave for dropping the Verified subset.

24.3How a score is produced, and what changes it

"Model X scores 72% on benchmark Y" is incomplete without the protocol. These settings can each move a score by several points:

SettingWhy it matters
Attempts: pass@1 vs pass@kpass@1 counts only the first answer. pass@k counts a task as solved if any of k attempts succeeds. pass@10 is always at least as high as pass@1, and much less like real use.
Thinking and effortReasoning models score higher with more thinking time (Chapter 19). Claude's effort setting is one such dial.
Harness and toolsOn agentic benchmarks, the surrounding program (which tools, how many steps, what instructions) matters as much as the model. A strong model in a weak harness scores poorly.
Prompt formatFew examples vs none, answer format, chain-of-thought or not.
SubsetFull set, a "verified" subset, or the vendor's own filtered version.
Who ran itSelf-reported vendor numbers vs independent replication. The research notes for this book observe that they often differ.
Real-world example

Anthropic's own model guidance (November 2026) recommends starting with Claude Opus 5.5 for most workloads, and moving to Fable 5.1 when "evals on Opus 5.5 at higher effort still fall short". Notice the shape of that advice: not "pick the model with the highest benchmark", but "test on your task, try more effort, then step up". It treats effort as part of the result, exactly as this section says.

24.4Contamination: the leaked exam paper

Data contamination means benchmark questions, or their answers, were in the model's training data. Models are trained on huge web crawls (Chapter 17); benchmarks are published on the web, discussed on forums and copied into GitHub repositories. If a model has seen the answer, a high score measures memory, not ability.

In everyday life

A student who found last year's exam paper with answers will do brilliantly on a repeat of that paper and no better than before on a new one. The fix for exams is the same as for benchmarks: new questions, kept secret until the test.

Signs of contamination include: a model can recite a benchmark question word for word; scores drop sharply on freshly written questions of the same difficulty; or performance is much higher on older tasks than on tasks created after the model's cutoff. Benchmark makers respond with private held-out test sets, regularly refreshed questions, and tasks dated after the models' cutoffs.

Under the hood: checking for overlap with n-grams

One simple contamination test compares n-grams (runs of n consecutive words) between a benchmark question and the training text. If many 8-word or 13-word runs from the question appear verbatim in the training data, the question was probably seen. Model developers with access to their training data run checks like this and report them in technical reports. Outsiders can't see training data, so they rely on indirect signs such as the ones above. The lab includes a small n-gram overlap check.

24.5Saturation and Goodhart's law

A benchmark is saturated when top models score near the maximum, so it can no longer tell them apart. A 1–2 point gap near the top may just be the few questions with disputed or wrong answer keys. MMLU is the standard example: once a stretch goal, it's now largely saturated, and attention has moved to harder sets.

  1. LaunchHard and informative. Models score low; differences are meaningful.
  2. AdoptionEveryone reports it. It becomes a target in training and marketing.
  3. PressureContamination and overfitting. Questions leak; methods tuned to the benchmark's quirks.
  4. SaturationScores near the ceiling. Small gaps are noise or answer-key errors.
  5. ReplacementA harder successor. MMLU → MMLU-Pro; SWE-bench Verified → SWE-bench Pro; ARC-AGI-1 → 2 → 3.

The underlying force is Goodhart's law, usually paraphrased as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure". Testers know it from code coverage: set a 90% target and you get tests that execute lines without checking anything. AI benchmarks behave the same way. Chapter 22 showed one costly example: right-or-wrong grading rewards guessing, which encourages hallucination.

24.6Leaderboards and human-preference rankings

A leaderboard ranks models on one or more benchmarks. Some are run by benchmark creators, some by independent groups, some by vendors.

Arena-style leaderboards work differently. A user types a prompt, two anonymous models answer side by side, and the user votes for the better one. Hundreds of thousands of such votes are turned into a rating, in the spirit of the Elo rating used in chess: beating a strong opponent raises your rating more than beating a weak one.

Strengths

  • Real prompts from real people, not a fixed exam
  • Hard to contaminate: the questions keep changing
  • Measures something benchmarks miss: whether people like the answer

Limits

  • Voters reward style: length, formatting, confidence, friendliness
  • Prompts skew towards what Arena users ask, not your work
  • "Preferred" isn't "correct": a confident wrong answer can win a vote
  • Vendors can test many private variants and publish the best
Under the hood: from votes to ratings

The Chatbot Arena paper (Chiang et al., 2024) fits a Bradley–Terry model: each model gets a strength score, and the probability that model A beats model B depends on the difference between their scores. The scores that best explain all the votes become the ratings. The paper also reports confidence intervals, because a rating built from votes is an estimate. Two models whose intervals overlap are not reliably different, whatever their rank numbers say.

Opinion · widely held among evaluation researchers

Many researchers argue that public benchmarks now matter more for marketing than for decisions, and that the most trustworthy signals are private, refreshed test sets and evaluations on real tasks. Others point out that public benchmarks remain the only shared yardstick the field has, and that dropping them would make claims harder to check. Both views appear in 2026 commentary; the practical answer for a team is to use both and trust your own.

24.7Noise: when 2 points mean nothing

A score is a measurement, and measurements have error. Two sources matter:

  • Small test sets. On 100 questions, one question is a whole percentage point. On 20, it's five.
  • Run-to-run variation. Model outputs vary between runs (non-deterministic), so the same model can score differently on the same test.

The fix is to report a confidence interval (a range the true score plausibly sits in) and to repeat runs. The lab script computes one. With 20 questions, a model scoring 85% has a 95% interval of roughly 64% to 95%: wide enough that a second model scoring 95% may not be genuinely better.

24.8Why your own evals matter more

No public benchmark contains your Jira stories, your API, your test conventions or your definition of a good bug report. A model that tops a coding leaderboard may still write Playwright tests that ignore your page objects. A cheaper model may be just as good for triaging your tickets.

BenchmarksShortlist two or three candidate models
Your evalRun them on 30–100 real tasks from your work, graded your way
Trade-offsWeigh quality against cost and speed
Re-runRepeat when models or prompts change

Chapter 36 gives a complete, runnable harness for comparing models on your tasks, and Chapter 125 shows how to build eval sets for AI features you ship, with code checks, LLM-as-judge grading and regression runs.

Your own evals can be contaminated too

If you keep tweaking your prompt until your 30 test cases pass, you've tuned to the test set, just like a benchmark target. Keep a separate held-out set that you only run occasionally, and add new real cases every month.

Score a benchmark honestly, and check for leaks25 min · terminal and a browser
minibench.py (Python 3, no packages; data is illustrative)
# minibench.py - score a tiny benchmark honestly (Python 3, no packages)
import math, re

# Answer key for a 20-question multiple-choice test (illustrative)
KEY     = "BCADBBCADDABCCADBACD"
# Letters each model chose, in the same order (illustrative)
MODEL_A = "ACADBBCADDABCCADBBCA"
MODEL_B = "BCADBBCADDABCCDDBACD"

def wilson(correct, n, z=1.96):
    """95% interval for a pass rate: how far the true score could plausibly be."""
    p = correct / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / (1 + z * z / n)
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / (1 + z * z / n)
    return centre - half, centre + half

for name, answers in (("Model A", MODEL_A), ("Model B", MODEL_B)):
    right = sum(a == k for a, k in zip(answers, KEY))
    lo, hi = wilson(right, len(KEY))
    print(f"{name}: {right}/{len(KEY)} = {right / len(KEY):.0%}   "
          f"95% interval {lo:.0%} to {hi:.0%}")

# Contamination check: do benchmark questions appear in "training" text?
def ngrams(text, n=8):
    w = re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower())
    return {" ".join(w[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(w) - n + 1)}

QUESTIONS = [
    "Which HTTP status code means the client sent too many requests in a given amount of time",
    "In boundary value analysis for an age field accepting 18 to 65 which values should you test first",
]
TRAINING_TEXT = """Forum post, 2024: Q: which HTTP status code means the client sent
too many requests in a given amount of time? A: 429, everyone knows that."""

seen = ngrams(TRAINING_TEXT)
print()
for q in QUESTIONS:
    grams = ngrams(q)
    overlap = len(grams & seen) / len(grams)
    flag = "LIKELY CONTAMINATED" if overlap > 0.5 else "looks clean"
    print(f"{overlap:4.0%} of 8-word sequences found  ->  {flag}: {q[:45]}...")

Troubleshooting. ZeroDivisionError: a model string is empty or the key is empty. Scores look wrong: the model strings must be the same length as KEY, one letter per question; zip silently stops at the shorter string. The contamination check flags nothing after your edit: the copied sentence must contain at least 8 consecutive words that match the question (punctuation and case are ignored).

Common misunderstandings
"The model at the top of the leaderboard is the best model."
It's the best on that measurement, under those settings. For your task, a different model may be better, cheaper or faster.
"A 2-point lead is a clear win."
Often it's within the noise of the test set and run-to-run variation. Look for confidence intervals.
"High benchmark scores mean the model understands the subject."
They may reflect contamination, format tricks or a narrow skill the benchmark rewards.
"Arena measures correctness."
It measures which answer voters preferred. Style and confidence influence votes.
"Benchmarks and evals are the same thing."
Benchmarks compare models in general; evals check fitness for your specific job. You need your own.

24.9Review

Key takeaways
  • A benchmark is a dataset, a scoring rule and a protocol. Change any of them and the score changes.
  • Major 2026 names: MMLU (saturated), GPQA Diamond, Humanity's Last Exam, SWE-bench Verified (dropped by OpenAI in February 2026) and SWE-bench Pro, Terminal-Bench, ARC-AGI-3 and Arena.
  • Scores depend on attempts (pass@1 vs pass@k), thinking effort, harness, prompt format and who ran the test.
  • Contamination inflates scores; saturation hides differences; Goodhart's law turns targets into bad measures.
  • Human-preference rankings are hard to contaminate but reward style as well as substance.
  • Small gaps are often noise: ask for intervals and repeated runs.
  • Use benchmarks to shortlist; use your own evals to decide (Chapter 36, Chapter 125).

Assignment

Choose two current models your team might use. Collect three benchmark claims for each from official announcements or system cards, recording the protocol details from section 24.3 and whether each is self-reported. Then write 20 tasks from your own work (for example: generate test cases from five real stories, triage five real bug reports, explain five real failing tests, write five API checks), run both models, and score them with a simple rubric. Write a one-page decision record: what the benchmarks suggested, what your eval showed, the confidence you have given the test size, and your recommendation. Keep the tasks: they're the seed of the harness in Chapter 36.

Teach it

"A benchmark is a standard exam for AI models, so we can compare them. Launch posts quote them constantly, but read them like a tester. Was the exam leaked into training data? Is it so easy everyone gets nearly full marks? Did they get one attempt or ten, and how much thinking time? Who ran it? And a 2-point lead on a small test can be pure noise. Leaderboards where people vote are harder to cheat but reward answers that look good. So we use benchmarks to make a shortlist, and then we test the shortlist on our own real tasks. Our eval decides, not the leaderboard."
