AI research moves on several fronts at once. Agents are handling longer tasks, reasoning models spend more computation thinking, interpretability researchers are starting to see inside models, and robots are borrowing ideas from language models. Each front has real, published results and a much larger cloud of claims and predictions around it. This chapter maps what is known, what is open, and what people expect, keeping those three apart. Every prediction here sits in an Opinion box with the person or group who holds it.

140.1Reading the frontier like a tester

The frontier is the edge of what the most capable AI systems can do. News about it arrives in many forms: peer-reviewed papers, preprints (papers posted publicly before review, usually on arXiv), company blog posts, product launches, benchmark leaderboards, interviews and social media threads. They are not equally reliable.

A tester already has the right instinct. A release note says "performance improved". You ask: measured how, on what, by whom, compared with what? Apply the same questions to research news.

Kind of statementExampleHow much weight to give it
Established result"Transformers trained on more data and compute improve predictably" (scaling laws, Kaplan 2020, Chinchilla 2022)High. Replicated by many groups over years
Published finding"Frontier agents' task length has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019" (METR, 2025)Medium. Real data and a stated method, but one group, one task suite, and a trend that may not continue
Vendor claim"Our new model is the best at agentic coding"Low until checked. Self-reported, chosen benchmarks, chosen settings (Chapter 33)
Prediction"AI will do most knowledge work within five years"An opinion. Judge the reasoning and the person's track record, not the confidence
In everyday life

A weather forecast for tomorrow is usually right; a forecast for three weeks ahead is barely better than the seasonal average. AI forecasts work the same way. Next year's models will probably be somewhat better at what this year's models do well. Statements about ten years out are mostly about the forecaster's assumptions.

Who says it?Lab, independent group, journalist, investor
What's the evidence?Paper, data, demo, or none
What would prove it wrong?A claim nothing could refute isn't evidence
Does it apply to me?My tasks, my data, my risk

140.2Agents and long tasks

An agent is a model plus tools plus a loop (Chapter 107). Through 2024–2026 agents went from demos to everyday tools: Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, Google's Jules and many others now carry out multi-step software tasks. The research question has moved from "can an agent do this at all?" to "how long a task can it complete reliably, and how do we know?"

Measuring task length: the "time horizon"

In March 2025 the research group METR published Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks (Kwa, West, Becker, Deng and others). Its idea is simple. Time how long skilled humans take to do a set of software and reasoning tasks. Then find, for each AI model, the task length at which it succeeds half the time. They call that the model's time horizon.

Their headline finding: the frontier time horizon has been doubling approximately every seven months since 2019, and the trend may have accelerated in 2024. That is a published finding with data, not a prediction. Turning it into a prediction ("so by year X agents will do month-long projects") requires assuming the trend continues, which is a separate claim.

Why "50% success" and why it matters for you

The 50% point is used because it can be estimated reliably from limited data: plot success against task length and fit a curve. But 50% is a coin flip. For work you depend on, you care about the 80% or 95% point, which is much shorter. The paper also reports an 80% horizon, and it is considerably lower than the 50% one.

Two more caveats a tester will spot. First, the tasks are well-specified, self-contained and automatically checkable; real work is often vague and needs judgement. Second, "time a human takes" depends on which humans; the authors used skilled professionals without prior context on each task. None of this makes the trend wrong. It tells you exactly what was measured.

What researchers are working on

  • Reliability over many steps. A 99%-reliable step repeated 100 times succeeds end to end only about 37% of the time (0.99100). Compounding error is why long-task reliability is hard (Chapter 107).
  • Memory and context. Long tasks outgrow a context window. Compaction, notes files and retrieval are today's workarounds (Chapter 20).
  • Computer use. Agents that drive a screen like a person are general but slower and less reliable than API-based tools (Chapter 112).
  • Shared standards. MCP (donated to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation in December 2025) and AGENTS.md let tools and instructions work across vendors.
  • Evaluation. Benchmarks such as Terminal-Bench and SWE-bench Pro test agents on real tasks; OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified in February 2026, citing contamination and flawed tasks (Chapter 24).
Real-world example

A QA lead reads that "agents can now complete multi-hour tasks". In her team, Claude Code reliably writes a Playwright test from a clear story in ten minutes, but a vague "improve our regression suite" request drifts. Both are consistent with the research: well-specified tasks with automatic checks are where agents are strongest. Her move is to make work well-specified and checkable, which is good QA practice anyway.

140.3Reasoning and thinking time

Reasoning models spend extra tokens thinking before they answer (Chapter 19). The line runs from chain-of-thought prompting (2022) to OpenAI's o1 (2024), then DeepSeek-R1 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (2025). DeepSeek's R1 paper showed that reinforcement learning alone (R1-Zero) could teach a model to reason, and published the method openly.

The established lesson is that test-time compute is a second dial alongside model size: a model can do better on hard problems by thinking longer. Current Claude models think adaptively, controlled by an effort level.

Open questions

QuestionWhy it's open
Does reasoning transfer beyond maths and code?Training uses automatic verifiers (does the answer match? do the tests pass?). Many real tasks, such as judging a requirement's ambiguity, have no automatic checker.
Is the written reasoning the real reasoning?Studies show a model's stated chain of thought does not always reflect what drove its answer. This matters for anyone who reads the thinking to check the work.
How far does thinking time scale?More thinking helps up to a point, then gains flatten, and cost and latency keep rising.
Novel problemsARC-AGI-3 (March 2026) uses interactive puzzles. At launch, humans solved all of them and the best AI system about 0.37%.
Practical consequence

Thinking is billed as output tokens and adds latency. "More effort" is not automatically better. Measure on your own tasks, as in Chapter 36.

140.4Interpretability: looking inside

A neural network's knowledge is spread across billions of numbers. Interpretability research tries to find out what those numbers represent and how they produce an answer. The branch that studies the actual internal mechanisms is often called mechanistic interpretability.

  1. Oct 2023Towards Monosemanticity. Anthropic uses dictionary learning to pull out interpretable "features" from a small model.
  2. May 2024Scaling Monosemanticity. Millions of features found in Claude 3 Sonnet, including concepts like the Golden Gate Bridge. Turning that feature up produced "Golden Gate Claude".
  3. Mar 2025Tracing the thoughts of a large language model. Circuit tracing in Claude 3.5 Haiku shows steps such as planning a rhyme before writing the line.
  4. May 2025Circuit-tracing tools open-sourced (date from a search summary), so other researchers can apply the method.
  5. Oct 2025Signs of introspection. Anthropic reports limited, unreliable evidence that models can sometimes notice concepts injected into their own activations.
In everyday life

Early interpretability is like early brain imaging. You can see that a region lights up when someone thinks about faces, which is real knowledge, but you are far from reading a full thought. Researchers can explain some behaviours of some models on some prompts. They can't yet audit a whole frontier model.

Why it matters: if you can see why a model produced an answer, you can find hidden failure modes, check that safety training worked, and catch deception. Interpretability is one of the few safety approaches that does not rely on the model's own description of itself.

Opinion · Interpretability researchers at Anthropic

Anthropic's researchers present interpretability as a path towards something like an "MRI for AI": a way to inspect models for problems before deployment. They describe it as early-stage work with large gaps, not a finished safety tool. Others in the field question whether full mechanistic understanding of very large models is achievable in time to matter, and favour behavioural testing and control methods instead.

140.5Multimodality, world models and robotics

Multimodal models handle more than text (Chapter 21). Products already differ widely. Claude reads images and PDFs but doesn't generate images or accept video; Gemini 4 Argon documents text, image, audio, video and PDF inputs. Research is pushing towards models that take in and produce every kind of data in one system.

World models

A world model is a system that learns how an environment behaves, so it can predict what happens next if something acts. Google DeepMind's Genie models, for example, generate interactive environments. Language models arguably contain partial world models learned from text; how complete these are is debated.

Robotics

Robots have long lagged language AI. Recall Moravec's paradox: walking and folding laundry are hard for machines, chess is easy. A recent idea is the vision-language-action model, which reuses a pre-trained vision-language model and adds the ability to output robot actions.

  • RT-2 (Google DeepMind, 2023) showed that knowledge learned from web images and text could help a robot follow new instructions.
  • π0 (Physical Intelligence, October 2024) trained one policy on data from several robot types, so it could follow language instructions and learn new dexterous skills by fine-tuning.

The main bottleneck is data. The internet supplies trillions of tokens of text, but there is no equivalent pile of robot experience. Researchers are trying simulation, teleoperation (people driving robots to record demonstrations) and pooling data across robot types.

For QA teams

Multimodal research is already useful at work: screenshot reading, visual comparison and document understanding (Chapter 123). Robotics matters to you only if your product includes hardware; there, simulation-based testing is a growing field.

140.6Efficiency: more for less

Frontier training runs cost enormous sums, and data centres use large and growing amounts of electricity (the IEA estimated about 415 TWh in 2024, projected about 945 TWh by 2030; Chapter 25). So a lot of research aims to get the same capability for less.

TechniquePlain ideaWhere in this book
Mixture of ExpertsOnly part of the model runs for each token, so a huge model costs like a smaller one per token (Mistral Large 4: about 1T parameters, 52B active)Ch. 30, Ch. 139
DistillationA small model learns to imitate a large oneCh. 139
QuantisationStore numbers with fewer bits; much smaller, slightly less accurateCh. 137
Attention efficiencyCheaper ways to handle long contexts, such as DeepSeek's Multi-head Latent AttentionCh. 16
Small, capable modelsCheap tiers (Claude Haiku 5.5 costs $0.50 per million output tokens) handle bulk workCh. 40

Efficiency gains have a known side effect: when something gets cheaper, people tend to use more of it. So cheaper tokens don't automatically mean lower total spending or energy use. Economists call this the rebound effect (or Jevons paradox).

140.7Safety research

Safety research asks how to make increasingly capable systems behave as intended, and how to know when they don't. Chapter 38 covers Anthropic's programme in detail; here is the wider map.

AreaQuestionExample work
Alignment trainingHow do we give models good values and behaviour?RLHF, Constitutional AI, Claude's 2026 constitution
Stress testsDo models misbehave under pressure in contrived setups?Alignment faking (Anthropic with Redwood Research, Dec 2024); agentic misalignment across 16 models (Jun 2025)
Scalable oversightHow do humans supervise systems that are better than them at a task?AI-assisted review, debate, checking work with tools
Chain-of-thought monitoringCan we read a model's reasoning to catch bad intent?Korbak and 40 co-authors (Jul 2025) call it "a new and fragile opportunity"
Dangerous capabilities and policyWhen does a model need stronger safeguards?Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (v3.4, Jul 2026); ASL-3 activated May 2025
Model welfareCould models have experiences that matter morally?Anthropic's research programme (Apr 2025); explicitly an open question

The chain-of-thought paper makes a point testers will appreciate. Today, many models reason in readable language, which lets humans and monitors look for problems. The authors warn that this could be lost if training pressures push reasoning into forms people can't read, so they urge developers to preserve it while it lasts.

Read the caveats

Stress-test results such as "models attempted blackmail in a simulated scenario" come from deliberately artificial setups designed to find worst cases. They are evidence that a behaviour is possible, not of how often it happens in normal use. Equally, the absence of a dramatic result in normal use is not proof of safety.

140.8Open questions and competing views

These questions have no settled answer. Each matters for planning.

Open questionWhat would help settle it
Will scaling plus reasoning plus agents keep delivering steady gains?Whether trends like the time horizon hold, bend or break over the next few years
Can models learn continuously on the job?Today weights are frozen after training (Chapter 12); continual learning without forgetting old skills is unsolved at scale
Do language models need grounding in the physical world?Progress (or not) of world models and robotics on tasks text can't teach
Can hallucination be driven close to zero?Calibration and abstention measured on hard, fresh questions (Chapter 22)
Can we verify a very capable model is safe?Interpretability maturing; evals that keep up with capabilities
What happens to jobs and productivity?Careful measurement over years, such as the Anthropic Economic Index (from Feb 2025)
Opinion · Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and others expecting rapid change

In "Machines of Loving Grace" (October 2024), Dario Amodei wrote that powerful AI could compress the progress biologists would have made over the next 50–100 years into 5–10 years, and that such systems might arrive within a few years. People with this view tend to cite steady capability trends, reasoning models and agents, and argue for urgent safety and policy work.

Opinion · Yann LeCun and others who expect new ideas to be needed

Yann LeCun, a Turing Award winner and long-time head of Meta's FAIR, has argued for years that language models alone will not reach human-level intelligence, and that systems which learn world models from sensory data are needed. Researchers in this camp expect current methods to plateau and new architectures to drive the next leap.

Opinion · Many economists and adoption researchers

A common view among people who study technology adoption is that even fast capability gains turn into economic change slowly, because organisations, regulation, trust and infrastructure take years to adapt. They compare AI to electricity: transformative, but over decades.

Opinion · This book's authors

For a QA team, planning does not depend on which camp is right. Capable, cheaper models and better agents are very likely over the next year or two; the size of the jump is not. Build habits that pay off in every scenario: well-specified tasks, automatic checks, evals, human approval for consequential actions, and portable setups.

140.9What it means for you

Likely to keep paying off

Clear requirements and acceptance criteria. Automated, trustworthy tests. Your own eval sets. Good context files (CLAUDE.md, skills). Security habits around tools and tokens. The judgement to decide what "correct" means.

Likely to change often

Which model is best, prices, product names, exact commands and settings, benchmark rankings. Treat them as dependencies with expiry dates and re-verify (Chapter 39).

A sensible routine: once a quarter, rerun your eval set on the newest models, read one or two primary sources (a paper or system card, not just the headlines), and update your team's decision record.

Stress-test a forecast25 min · Python 3 and claude.ai (any plan)
  1. Save this script as horizon.py. It projects an agent's time horizon under different doubling assumptions. Every number in it is illustrative.
    horizon.py
    # horizon.py - how much a forecast depends on its assumptions.
# All numbers are ILLUSTRATIVE, not measurements.
import sys

start_hours = float(sys.argv[1]) if len(sys.argv) > 1 else 2.0   # task length an agent manages today (illustrative)
years = [1, 2, 3, 5]
doubling_months = [4, 7, 12, 24]                                   # different assumptions

print(f"Starting horizon: {start_hours:g} hours (illustrative)")
print("doubling | " + " | ".join(f"{y} yr" for y in years))
for d in doubling_months:
    row = []
    for y in years:
        h = start_hours * 2 ** (12 * y / d)
        row.append(f"{h:,.0f} h" if h >= 10 else f"{h:.1f} h")
    print(f"{d:>4} mo  | " + " | ".join(row))

Common misunderstandings
"A trend line is a prediction."
A trend describes the past. Extending it is an extra assumption that needs its own evidence.
"Interpretability means we can now read a model's mind."
Researchers can explain some mechanisms on some prompts. Full audits of frontier models are not yet possible.
"The model's visible reasoning shows exactly why it answered."
Stated reasoning is useful but not always faithful to the internal computation.
"If experts disagree, nobody knows anything."
There is a lot of established knowledge. The disagreement is mostly about timing and the limits of current methods.
"Cheaper models will cut our AI bill."
Often usage grows to fill the savings. Track total spend, not just price per token.

140.10Review

Key takeaways
  • Sort research news into established results, published findings, vendor claims and predictions, and weigh each accordingly.
  • METR (2025) found the frontier agent time horizon doubling about every seven months since 2019; extending it is an assumption.
  • Reasoning via test-time compute is established; its reach beyond verifiable tasks and the faithfulness of stated reasoning are open.
  • Interpretability can explain some mechanisms (features, circuit tracing) but can't yet audit a whole frontier model.
  • Vision-language-action models carry language-model ideas into robotics; the main bottleneck is robot data.
  • Efficiency research (MoE, distillation, quantisation) lowers cost per token, but total use often grows.
  • Safety research spans alignment training, stress tests, scalable oversight, chain-of-thought monitoring, scaling policies and model welfare.
  • Plan with no-regret habits: clear specs, automatic checks, evals, approvals and regular re-verification.
Check yourself

What does METR's "time horizon" measure?

It links model success rates to how long the same tasks take skilled humans.

A vendor blog says its new model is "the best at agentic coding". How should you treat this?

Vendor claims use chosen benchmarks and settings. Shortlist with them; decide with your own evals.

Why is a model's written chain of thought useful for safety, according to Korbak et al. (2025)?

The authors call it "a new and fragile opportunity" that training choices could erode.

What is the main bottleneck for applying language-model methods to robots?

Simulation, teleoperation and pooling data across robot types are attempts to close that gap.

Which belongs in an Opinion box?

It's a prediction held by some researchers, such as Yann LeCun, and disputed by others.

Teach it

"AI research is moving on several fronts: agents doing longer tasks, models thinking longer, researchers starting to see inside models, robots borrowing language-model ideas, and a lot of safety work. When you read about it, sort each statement: is it a replicated result, one group's finding, a vendor's claim, or someone's prediction? Predictions depend heavily on their assumptions. A small change in how fast you assume things double changes a five-year forecast tenfold. So plan with habits that help whatever happens: clear specs, good tests, our own evals, and human approval for anything that matters."
