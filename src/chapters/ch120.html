The cheapest bug is the one you prevent by asking a good question before development starts. AI is excellent at that: give Claude a user story and it will list ambiguities, missing rules and risky areas in seconds. It's also a tireless partner for test design. It can apply boundary values, decision tables and pairwise combinations systematically, and never gets bored. What it can't do is know your product, your users or your risk appetite. So the pattern in this chapter is always the same: Claude drafts, you decide.

120.1Shift left: AI in refinement

"Shift left" means finding problems earlier: in requirements rather than in testing, and in testing rather than in production. Refinement (also called grooming or backlog refinement) is where a team discusses a story before building it. Many teams hold a "three amigos" session: business (product owner), development and testing look at the story together.

A tester's job in that meeting is to ask the awkward questions. Claude can help you prepare a much longer, sharper list of them in a few minutes.

Prompt: refinement preparation
You are a senior QA engineer preparing for a three-amigos session.
Here is the user story and its acceptance criteria:

<story>
As a shopper, I want to apply a discount code at checkout
so that I pay less.
AC1: A valid code reduces the order total.
AC2: An invalid code shows an error.
AC3: Only one code per order.
</story>

1. List every ambiguity as a question for the product owner.
2. List missing acceptance criteria (rules you would expect but can't see).
3. Mark any statement that can't be tested as written, and suggest
   a testable rewording.
4. List risks: data, money, security, accessibility, integrations.
Do not invent answers. Where the story is silent, ask.

A typical answer will include questions like these:

AmbiguityIs the discount applied before or after tax? Before or after delivery charges?Different answers give different totals: a money bug

In everyday life

It's like asking a well-read friend to check a holiday booking before you pay. They don't know your family, but they'll ask "does the hotel price include breakfast?" and "what if the flight is delayed past check-in?" You still decide; you just decide with fewer surprises.

Real-world example

A QA engineer brings Claude's ten questions to refinement. The product owner answers six on the spot, two become new acceptance criteria, and one ("what if the code expires while the shopper is mid-checkout?") becomes a separate story. That's three defects that will never be written, found in fifteen minutes.

120.3Risk-based test planning

You can never test everything, so you test the riskiest things most. Risk is usually scored as likelihood (how likely is it to break?) × impact (how bad is it if it does?).

Claude's scores are a starting point. It doesn't know that last quarter's biggest incident was a rounding bug, or that this market is regulated. Adjust the scores with what you know, and write down why. That written reasoning is what makes the plan defensible in a review.

120.4Classic techniques, applied by AI

Test design techniques are systematic ways of choosing a small number of tests that find a large share of bugs. They're tedious to do by hand, which is exactly why AI helps. Name the technique in your prompt; you'll get far better tests than "write test cases".

Equivalence partitioning and boundary values

Equivalence partitioning splits inputs into groups the system should treat the same way. Boundary value analysis then tests the edges of each group, because that's where off-by-one bugs live.

Prompt
Rule: a discount code needs a minimum basket of £20.00 and works up to £500.00.
Apply equivalence partitioning and boundary value analysis.
Show the partitions first, then a table of test values with the expected result.
Prices are in pounds with 2 decimal places.
PartitionTest valuesExpected
Below minimum (< £20.00)£0.01, £19.99Code rejected, "Minimum spend £20" message
Valid (£20.00–£500.00)£20.00, £20.01, £250.00, £499.99, £500.00Code accepted
Above maximum (> £500.00)£500.01Code rejected (needs confirming: is this rule real?)
Invalid input£0.00, empty basketCode field disabled or rejected

Notice the note on £500.01. A good model flags when the rule implies something nobody said. That's a refinement question, not a test case.

Decision tables

When several conditions combine, a decision table lists every combination and the expected outcome. Ask Claude to build the full table, then to collapse rows that can't happen.

A dash means "doesn't matter here". Ask Claude to justify each dash; sometimes it reveals a rule the order of checks depends on ("which error wins if the code is both expired and below minimum?").

State transitions

Many features move between states: an order is draft → submitted → paid → shipped → refunded. Ask Claude for a state-transition table that includes the invalid transitions (refunding a draft, shipping an unpaid order). Invalid transitions are where serious bugs hide, and they're the ones people forget.

Pairwise (combinatorial) testing

Suppose checkout must work on 4 browsers, 3 payment methods, 2 delivery options and 2 account types. Every combination is 4 × 3 × 2 × 2 = 48 tests. Pairwise testing covers every pair of values at least once, which catches most interaction bugs in a dozen or so tests.

Check the maths

Claude can produce a pairwise set, but it can make mistakes. For anything that matters, ask it to also list every pair and show which test covers it, or generate the set with a dedicated pairwise tool and use Claude to explain and review it. Never trust a combinatorial claim you haven't checked.

Error guessing

Error guessing is the experienced tester's instinct: "what usually breaks here?" Claude has read a great deal about how software fails, so it's a good brainstorming partner. Ask: "List 20 ways a discount-code field typically fails in production, ordered by how common they are." You'll get double-submit, whitespace, case sensitivity, Unicode lookalike characters, race conditions with two tabs, back-button resubmits and more. Keep the ones that apply.

Why naming the technique works so well

Language models respond to the vocabulary in your prompt. "Write tests" pulls toward the most common kind of test text the model has seen, which is usually happy-path examples. "Apply boundary value analysis to this rule" pulls toward the far more structured material written by testers: textbooks, ISTQB syllabus examples, test design articles. You are steering which part of the model's knowledge does the work. The same applies to "state transition table including invalid transitions" and "pairwise". Precise words get precise work.

120.5Gherkin and BDD

Behaviour-driven development (BDD) writes expected behaviour as scenarios that business people can read. The format is Gherkin: Given a starting situation, When something happens, Then this should be the result.

Gherkin drafted by Claude, then edited by the tester
Feature: Discount codes at checkout

  Scenario: Valid code at the minimum spend
    Given my basket subtotal is £20.00
    When I apply the code "SAVE10"
    Then the discount line shows -£2.00
    And the order total is £18.00

  Scenario Outline: Code rejected below the minimum spend
    Given my basket subtotal is <subtotal>
    When I apply the code "SAVE10"
    Then I see the message "Spend £20 or more to use this code"

    Examples:
      | subtotal |
      | £0.01    |
      | £19.99   |

Ask Claude to review Gherkin as well as write it. Useful review questions:

  • Does each scenario test exactly one behaviour?
  • Are the steps written in business language, not UI clicks ("I apply the code", not "I click button #apply")?
  • Is every Then a specific, checkable result?
  • Are any scenarios duplicates of each other in disguise?

120.6Traceability and coverage gaps

A traceability matrix links each requirement to the tests that cover it. It answers two questions every QA lead gets asked: "Is everything tested?" and "What does this change affect?"

Prompt: coverage gap analysis
Here are the acceptance criteria (AC1-AC7) and our current test cases (TC-01 to TC-23).
1. Build a traceability matrix: rows = ACs, columns = test IDs, mark covering tests.
2. List any AC with no test, or with only happy-path tests.
3. List any test that maps to no AC (possibly obsolete, or a missing AC).
4. Suggest the minimum new tests to close the gaps.

Point 3 is often the most valuable. A test with no matching requirement is either testing something that was removed, or something nobody wrote down. Both are worth a conversation.

Real-world example

Before a release, a QA lead exports 180 test cases from the test management tool as CSV, plus the 40 acceptance criteria for the release. Claude builds the matrix in under a minute and finds 4 criteria with only happy-path coverage and 11 tests mapping to a feature that was cut. The lead spends an hour checking Claude's mapping on a sample of 20 rows (it got 19 right), then closes the gaps. The same analysis by hand would take a day.

120.7Reviewing AI-generated tests

Treat AI-generated tests like a pull request from a capable new colleague: probably good, definitely needing review. Use this checklist.

CheckQuestion to ask
GroundedDoes every expected result come from the requirements, or did the model assume it? Ask: "For each expected result, quote the requirement it comes from."
SpecificCould a different tester run it and get the same pass or fail? "Error is shown" fails this; "'Code not recognised' appears below the field" passes.
Negative casesAre there tests for invalid input, wrong states, permissions and failures, not just the happy path?
BoundariesAre the edges of every range covered?
DuplicatesDo two tests check the same thing with different wording?

Common misunderstandings and pitfalls
Generic tests
"Verify the page loads" and "verify the user can log in" for every story. Fix: give real context (screens, rules, examples of your team's tests) and name the techniques you want.
Hallucinated requirements
The model invents a rule that sounds sensible ("codes expire after 30 days") and writes tests for it. Fix: the "quote the requirement or say ASSUMED" prompt.
Too many tests
AI will happily write 200 tests. More isn't better; maintained, meaningful coverage is. Ask it to merge duplicates and rank by risk.
"The AI did the test design"
It drafted it. You own the risk decisions, the priorities and the sign-off.
Skipping the conversation
The refinement questions are only valuable if someone answers them. Take them to the three-amigos session; don't just file them.

120.8Review

Key takeaways
  • The biggest win is before development: use Claude to find ambiguities, missing rules and untestable statements for refinement.
  • Give rich context (a Project, Jira through MCP, or repo files), not a bare story.
  • Name the technique: equivalence partitioning, boundary values, decision tables, state transitions, pairwise, error guessing.
  • Use AI for traceability matrices and coverage gap analysis, and spot-check its mapping.
  • Review every generated test: grounded, specific, negative cases, boundaries, no duplicates, real product terms.
  • Claude drafts; you own risk, priority and sign-off.
Check yourself

What's the most valuable output of AI in refinement?

Preventing a defect is cheaper than finding one. Questions answered in refinement remove bugs before they're written.

A minimum spend is £20.00. Which set best applies boundary value analysis?

Test just below, on and just above the boundary.

How do you catch hallucinated requirements in AI-generated tests?

Grounding each result in a quoted requirement exposes anything the model made up.

Why use pairwise testing for 4 browsers × 3 payment methods × 2 delivery options × 2 account types?

Most interaction bugs involve two factors, so covering every pair gives good value for much less effort.

A test in your suite maps to no acceptance criterion. What does that suggest?

Orphan tests point to either obsolete coverage or missing requirements.

Assignment

For your next sprint, use Claude to prepare for refinement on two stories. Track three numbers: questions raised, questions that changed the story, and defects later found in those stories compared with similar stories you prepared the old way. After two sprints, share the comparison with your team. This becomes evidence for the business case in Chapter 126.

Teach it

"Use Claude before development, not just after. Give it the story and context, and ask for ambiguities, missing rules and risks to take to refinement. Then name the technique you want (boundaries, decision tables, state transitions, pairwise) and you'll get proper test design, not generic tests. Always ask it to quote the requirement behind each expected result, and review everything. Claude drafts; we decide."
