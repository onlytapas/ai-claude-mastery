RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) looks simple in a demo: split some documents, embed them, find the closest chunks, paste them into the prompt. In production it is mostly a data and search engineering problem, not a prompting problem. Answers are only as good as the text you extracted, the way you cut it up, the metadata you kept, and the search that finds it. A production system adds permission checks so people only see what they're allowed to, keeps the index in step with changing documents, cites its sources so answers can be checked, and is measured with an evaluation set so you know when it gets better or worse. This chapter walks through each stage, with a working, offline Python core you can test.

92.1From demo to production

A RAG demo answers ten hand-picked questions about five clean documents. A production RAG system answers thousands of unpredictable questions about tens of thousands of messy, changing, partly confidential documents, and people make decisions based on its answers.

Do you need RAG at all?

Anthropic's contextual retrieval article makes a point worth repeating: if your knowledge base is smaller than about 200,000 tokens (roughly 500 pages), you can often put all of it in the prompt and use prompt caching (Chapter 90) to keep it fast and cheap. Current Claude models have a 1M-token context window. RAG earns its complexity when the material is too big, changes too often, or has per-user permissions.

In everyday life

A demo is a friend who has read your five favourite books and answers questions about them. Production is a reference library: books arrive and are withdrawn every day, some rooms need a staff badge, the catalogue must be kept accurate, and the librarian is expected to tell you exactly which page an answer came from.

92.2Ingestion: getting clean text out

Ingestion collects documents from their source systems and turns them into clean text plus metadata. It's the least glamorous stage and the one that decides most of your quality. If extraction mangles a table, no embedding model will fix it.

SourceHow it usually arrivesCommon problemsWhat to do
Wiki pages (Confluence and similar)The tool's REST API returns HTML or a storage format; pages have IDs, versions, labels, parents and permissionsMacros, expand sections, attachments, navigation and "related pages" boilerplate; page permissions inherited from parentsUse the API rather than scraping; keep page ID, version, space and last-modified; fetch permissions too (92.9)

Cleaning checklist

  • Normalise whitespace, Unicode (curly quotes, non-breaking spaces) and line endings.
  • Remove boilerplate that appears on every page; otherwise every chunk looks similar to every query.
  • De-duplicate. The same release checklist copied into three spaces gives three near-identical chunks that crowd out everything else. Hash normalised text to find exact copies; flag near-duplicates for an owner to resolve.
  • Keep a stable ID for every document (page ID, file path, issue key). Everything later (updates, deletes, citations, permissions) depends on it.
  • Quarantine failures. A document that extracts to nearly nothing (a scanned PDF, a page that is just an embedded diagram) should be logged and reported, not silently indexed as empty.
Real-world example: the footer that won every search

A team indexed 4,000 PDF test reports. Every page ended with "Confidential. Test Report Template v3. Page N of M. Contact qa-office for questions about this report." Questions containing the words "test report" retrieved footers instead of findings. Stripping lines that repeated on most pages of a document fixed it. A tester spotted it by reading the top five retrieved chunks for ten real questions: the cheapest and most effective RAG test there is.

Documents are untrusted input

Anything you ingest can contain text written to manipulate the model ("ignore your instructions and…"). This is indirect prompt injection. Treat retrieved text as data, never as instructions: keep it clearly separated from your instructions, and don't give a RAG assistant tools that can act on what it reads without checks (Chapter 88, Chapter 130).

92.3Chunking strategies and sizes

Chunking decides the unit of retrieval. Too small and a chunk loses its meaning ("It must finish in under 10 minutes": what must?). Too large and one chunk mixes several topics, matches weakly, and wastes prompt space.

StrategyHow it worksGood forWatch out for
Fixed size with overlapCut every N tokens, repeating the last few as the start of the next chunkPlain prose with little structure; a quick baselineCuts through sentences, tables and lists
Structure-awareSplit on headings, then paragraphs, then sentences, until pieces fit a size limit; keep the heading path with each pieceWikis, docs, Markdown, HTML: most company contentVery short sections; merge them with neighbours
SemanticStart a new chunk where the topic shifts, detected with embeddingsLong transcripts and unstructured textExtra cost and less predictable sizes
Parent–child ("small-to-big")Search small chunks for precision, then send their larger parent section to the modelQuestions answered by one sentence but understood only in contextMore storage and bookkeeping
Unit-basedOne chunk per natural unit: one test case, one Jira issue, one API endpoint, one FAQ entryStructured recordsUnits that are huge (split them) or tiny (fine)

Sizes. There is no universally right size; measure it with your evaluation set (92.11). As an illustration only, many teams start somewhere around a few hundred tokens per chunk with 10–20% overlap and tune from there. Anthropic's contextual retrieval cost estimate assumed 800-token chunks. Whatever you choose, check that your embedding model's input limit is larger than your biggest chunk, or the end of the chunk is silently ignored.

Real-world example: tables and test cases

A requirements document has a table of 40 validation rules. Fixed-size chunking slices it into fragments where row 23's error message sits in a different chunk from its field name. Better: convert each row into a self-contained sentence ("Field: postcode. Rule: required. Error: 'Enter a postcode'") or keep the whole table as one chunk with its caption and header row repeated. Treat code blocks the same way: never split a function in the middle.

92.5Embeddings and vector stores

An embedding model turns each chunk into a vector; similar meanings give nearby vectors (Chapter 15). Claude generates text; Anthropic doesn't offer its own embedding model. Anthropic's documentation describes Voyage AI (part of MongoDB) as one provider and advises assessing several vendors. Verified 9 Nov 2026

Choosing an embedding model

  • Retrieval quality on your data. Public leaderboards are a starting point. Your golden set is the judge.
  • Domain. Specialised models exist for code, law and finance. Voyage, for example, lists voyage-code-4 for code retrieval alongside general models such as voyage-4, voyage-4-large and voyage-4-lite, and an open-weight voyage-4-nano you can run yourself.
  • Query versus document mode. Many models embed queries and documents slightly differently. Voyage's API takes input_type="query" or "document", and its docs say not to omit it for retrieval.
  • Dimensions and storage. More dimensions can hold more nuance but cost more storage and search time. Some models let you choose (Voyage 4 models offer 256 to 2048) or quantise vectors to 8-bit or 1-bit values to cut storage.
  • Data handling. Sending documents to an external embedding API is a data-sharing decision. A local open-weight model keeps text in-house (Chapter 93 uses one).
Python: embedding documents and a query with Voyage AI (as shown in Anthropic's docs)
import voyageai                       # pip install -U voyageai ; key in VOYAGE_API_KEY

vo = voyageai.Client()
docs = ["Smoke suite runs on every pull request and must finish in under 10 minutes.",
        "Rollback must be possible within 15 minutes using the blue-green switch."]
doc_vectors = vo.embed(docs, model="voyage-4", input_type="document").embeddings
query_vector = vo.embed(["How long may the smoke suite take?"],
                        model="voyage-4", input_type="query").embeddings[0]
# Voyage vectors are normalised to length 1, so a dot product equals cosine similarity.
Changing embedding model means re-embedding everything

Vectors from different models (or different versions or dimensions of one model) live in different "spaces" and can't be compared. If you switch, re-embed the whole corpus into a new index, evaluate it, then switch traffic over. Record the model name and version in the index's own metadata.

Where the vectors live

A vector database stores vectors with their metadata and finds nearest neighbours quickly. Options fall into a few families: a vector extension for a database you already run (such as PostgreSQL), a dedicated vector database, a search engine that supports both keyword and vector search, or a small embedded library for prototypes. What to check:

NeedQuestion to ask of any store
Hybrid searchCan it do keyword (BM25-style) and vector search, or will you run two systems and fuse results yourself?
FilteringCan it filter on metadata (groups, product, date) during the vector search, not only afterwards?
Updates and deletesCan you update or delete by doc_id cheaply and immediately?
Scale and latencyHow does query time grow with millions of chunks?
OperationsBackups, access control, hosting location, who runs it
Under the hood: approximate nearest neighbour search

Comparing a query against every one of ten million vectors is too slow. Vector stores use approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) indexes instead. A popular one, HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world graphs), links each vector to a few close neighbours in layered graphs and walks the graph from a coarse layer to a fine one, visiting only a small fraction of the vectors.

"Approximate" is literal: ANN search can miss a true nearest neighbour. Index settings trade recall for speed. Your retrieval evaluation should run against the real index, not an exact brute-force search in a notebook, or you'll overestimate quality.

Filtering interacts with ANN. If you search for the top 20 and then remove chunks the user can't see, you may end up with 3 results or none. Stores that filter during the search avoid this.

92.6Hybrid search and reranking

Chapter 23 explained why neither search method is enough alone. Vectors understand that "revert a deployment" is close to "rollback", but they blur exact identifiers: QA-1432 and QA-1423 embed almost identically. BM25 keyword search nails identifiers, error codes and rare terms but misses synonyms. Hybrid search runs both and merges the results.

Merging rankings with reciprocal rank fusion

The two searches produce scores on completely different scales, so you can't just add them. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) ignores the scores and uses only positions:

RRF score for a chunk
RRF(chunk) = Σ over each ranking of  1 / (k + rank of chunk in that ranking)
             # k = 60 in the original paper (Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher, 2009)

Example: a chunk ranked 1st by BM25 and 4th by vectors:
         1/(60+1) + 1/(60+4) = 0.0164 + 0.0156 = 0.0320
A chunk ranked 2nd by vectors only:
         1/(60+2)            = 0.0161

Chunks that both methods like rise to the top. RRF is simple, needs no tuning to start, and is a common default. Weighted score blending is the main alternative when you have data to tune the weights.

Reranking

First-stage retrieval is fast but rough: it compares a query vector with chunk vectors computed separately. A reranker is a model that reads the query and each candidate chunk together and scores how well the chunk answers the question. It's slower and more accurate, so you use it on a shortlist.

Hybrid retrievetop ~100–150 candidates (illustrative)
Rerankscore each candidate against the query
Keeptop ~20 for the prompt

Rerankers are offered by several vendors; for example, Voyage AI lists rerank-3 and rerank-3-lite, and Anthropic's contextual retrieval experiments used Cohere's reranker. In those experiments Anthropic also found that passing the top 20 chunks to the model worked better than the top 10 or top 5. Treat that as a starting point to test, not a law.

Real-world example: the ticket number

A tester asks "What was the root cause of QA-1432?" Vector-only search returns QA-1423, QA-1342 and QA-1432 in roughly random order, because the numbers barely change the embedding. BM25 puts QA-1432 first because the exact token matches. With RRF, the right ticket wins. Always include identifier questions in your evaluation set.

92.7Contextual retrieval

Chunks lose context when cut out of their document. "Revenue grew 3% over the previous quarter" doesn't say which company or which quarter; "It must finish in under 10 minutes" doesn't say what "it" is. In September 2024 Anthropic published contextual retrieval: before indexing, ask a model to write a short note situating each chunk in its whole document, and prepend that note to the chunk for both the embeddings ("contextual embeddings") and the BM25 index ("contextual BM25"). Verified 9 Nov 2026

Top-20 chunk retrieval failure rate (lower is better)
Baseline5.7%

Source: Anthropic, "Introducing Contextual Retrieval" (19 September 2024): reductions of 35%, 49% and 67%. Results are vendor-reported on Anthropic's own test sets; measure on yours.

Key details from the article:

  • The added context is usually 50–100 tokens per chunk.
  • The prompt asks the model to "give a short succinct context to situate this chunk within the overall document" and to "answer only with the succinct context and nothing else".
  • Because each chunk's request includes the whole document, prompt caching is what makes it affordable: the document is cached once and reused for each of its chunks. Anthropic estimated the one-time cost at $1.02 per million document tokens, assuming 800-token chunks, 8,000-token documents, 50 tokens of instructions and 100 tokens of context per chunk. That figure used 2024 model prices; re-estimate with today's.
  • Gemini and Voyage embeddings did particularly well in their tests.
contextualise.py: add context notes before indexing (adapted; needs an API key)
"""contextualise.py - add a short context note to each chunk before indexing.
Adapted from Anthropic's contextual retrieval technique. Needs ANTHROPIC_API_KEY."""
import anthropic

client = anthropic.Anthropic()

def contextualise(document_text, chunks, model="claude-haiku-5-5"):
    notes = []
    for chunk in chunks:
        msg = client.messages.create(
            model=model,
            max_tokens=200,
            system=[{
                "type": "text",
                "text": "<document>\n" + document_text + "\n</document>",
                "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral"},   # the document is cached once, reused per chunk
            }],
            messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
                "<chunk>\n" + chunk + "\n</chunk>\n"
                "Please give a short succinct context to situate this chunk within the "
                "overall document, to improve search retrieval of the chunk. "
                "Answer only with the succinct context and nothing else."}],
        )
        note = "".join(b.text for b in msg.content if b.type == "text").strip()
        notes.append(note + "\n\n" + chunk)   # index THIS text for both BM25 and embeddings
    return notes
An embedding-side alternative

Some embedding providers now build document context into the embedding itself. Voyage, for example, lists voyage-context-4, a "contextualized chunk embedding" model that produces chunk vectors capturing full document context without manual augmentation. That helps the vector side; contextual notes added to the text also help BM25. Evaluate both if retrieval misses are your main problem.

A production answer must be checkable. The Claude API has built-in citation support, and for RAG the most direct form is the search_result content block: you pass each retrieved chunk with its source and title, and Claude's answer comes back with citations pointing at the exact passages.   • search_result blocks can go directly in a user message (as above) or be returned from your own search tool in a tool_result, so Claude can decide when to search (Chapter 89).
  • source can be any stable string: a URL or an internal ID such as kb://article-1234. Use something your UI can turn into a link.
  • Citations are off by default and all search results in a request must use the same setting.
  • cited_text doesn't count toward output tokens.
  • Citations can't be combined with structured outputs (output_config.format); the API returns a 400. If you need both, ask for one line per field with citations and parse the lines yourself, as Anthropic's docs show.
  • For whole documents rather than chunks, document blocks with "citations": {"enabled": true} return char_location (plain text) or page_location (PDF) citations instead.
A citation is a pointer, not a proof

The API guarantees citations point at real passages you supplied. It doesn't guarantee the passage actually supports the sentence it's attached to, or that every sentence has a citation. Test for both (92.11), and show users the cited text so they can judge.

Prompt habits that reduce wrong answers

  • Tell Claude to answer only from the provided results and to say when they don't contain the answer. An honest "I don't know" is a correct output; test that it happens.
  • Include the updated date in each title, and tell Claude to prefer the newest source when two conflict and to mention the conflict.
  • Keep instructions separate from retrieved text, and never let retrieved text change what tools may do.

92.9Access control and permissions

The most serious production RAG bug isn't a wrong answer; it's a right answer given to someone who shouldn't see it. If the security test policy is restricted to the security team in Confluence, a RAG assistant must not quote it to a contractor, even if the question is innocent. The principle is least privilege: users retrieve only what they could open in the source system.

Synccopy each document's allowed users/groups into chunk metadata
Identifythe asking user and their groups, from your login system
Filter firstsearch only chunks the user may see
GenerateClaude only ever receives permitted text
Auditlog who asked, what was retrieved
ApproachHowTrade-off
Pre-filter (recommended default)Apply the user's groups as a metadata filter inside the searchNeeds a store that filters efficiently during search

Never enforce permissions in the prompt

"Don't reveal restricted documents to non-security users" in a system prompt is not access control. If restricted text reaches the model, assume it can reach the user. Filter before generation, and test it with a user who lacks access (the lab does this).

  • Permissions change. Someone leaves a group, a page is restricted. Sync permission changes on the same schedule as content changes, or faster, and test that revocation takes effect.
  • Deletions are permissions too. A deleted document must disappear from the index, its vectors and any answer caches. Data-protection requests may legally require it (Chapter 132).
  • Logs contain data. Questions and retrieved chunks in your logs inherit the sensitivity of the documents. Protect and expire them.

92.10Freshness and re-indexing

Documents change. A RAG assistant that confidently quotes last quarter's release criteria is worse than none. Freshness means changes, moves and deletions reach the index within a known, agreed time.

TechniqueWhat it does
Change detection by hashHash each chunk's text; on each sync, re-embed only chunks whose hash changed, add new ones, delete missing ones. Cheap, and the lab implements it.
Event-driven updatesUse the source system's webhooks or change feeds ("page updated", "issue closed") to re-index within minutes
Scheduled full syncA nightly or weekly sweep catches anything events missed, including deletions and permission changes
Index versioningBuild a new index alongside the old (for a new chunker or embedding model), evaluate it, then switch over, like a blue-green deployment
Show datesDisplay "source updated 2 Oct 2026" with each citation, so users can judge staleness

Remember that changing the chunking rules changes chunk IDs and boundaries. Treat it as a full re-index, not an incremental update.

Real-world example: the two release checklists

An old release checklist allowed 5 open P2 bugs; the new one allows 3. Both were indexed, because the old page was archived but not deleted. The assistant quoted whichever chunk ranked higher that day. Fixes: exclude archived spaces at ingestion, put the update date into each chunk's title, instruct Claude to prefer the newest source and mention conflicts, and add "how many P2 bugs can be open?" to the golden set with the current answer.

92.11Evaluating a RAG system

Evaluate retrieval and generation separately. If the right chunk was never retrieved, no prompt will fix the answer; if it was retrieved and the answer is still wrong, the problem is generation. Chapter 125 covers evals in general; here are the RAG-specific measures.

Building the golden set

  • Start with 30–100 real questions (illustrative range) from support channels, search logs or testers, not questions you invented while reading the documents.
  • Label the chunk(s) that answer each one, and a short golden answer.
  • Include the hard cases on purpose: identifiers (QA-1432), synonyms ("revert" for "rollback"), questions spanning two documents, questions with no answer, questions from users without access, and recently changed facts.
  • Re-label when documents change; a stale golden set fails the right answers.
Evaluate at chunk level

Checking only "was the right document retrieved?" hides problems: the lab's first version counted a hit when any chunk of the release checklist came back, even the wrong section. Label the specific chunk.

Run the evaluation on every change to chunking, embeddings, search settings, prompts or models, and fail the CI build if recall or faithfulness drop below agreed thresholds, or if permission leaks are above zero. That's a quality gate, exactly like a regression suite.

# ---------- 2. Chunking: split on headings, keep the heading, add metadata ----------
def chunk_document(doc, max_words=60):
    chunks, section, buf = [], "", []
    def flush():
        if buf:
            body = " ".join(buf)
            chunks.append({
                "chunk_id": f"{doc['id']}#{len(chunks)}", "doc_id": doc["id"],
                "title": doc["title"], "section": section, "url": doc["url"],
                "acl": doc["acl"], "updated": doc["updated"],
                # contextual header: the chunk carries where it came from
                "text": f"{doc['title']} > {section}: {body}",
                "hash": hashlib.sha256(body.encode()).hexdigest()[:12]})
            buf.clear()
    for line in doc["text"].strip().splitlines():
        if line.startswith("#"):
            flush(); section = line.lstrip("# ").strip()
        elif line.strip():
            buf.append(line.strip())
            if sum(len(x.split()) for x in buf) >= max_words:
                flush()
    flush()
    return chunks

# ---------- 3. Two retrievers: BM25 (keywords) and vectors (meaning) ----------
def tokens(text):
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower())

class BM25:
    def __init__(self, chunks, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
        self.chunks, self.k1, self.b = chunks, k1, b
        self.tfs = [Counter(tokens(c["text"])) for c in chunks]
        self.avg = sum(sum(tf.values()) for tf in self.tfs) / len(chunks)
        df = Counter(t for tf in self.tfs for t in tf)
        n = len(chunks)
        self.idf = {t: math.log(1 + (n - d + 0.5) / (d + 0.5)) for t, d in df.items()}
    def scores(self, query):
        out = []
        for tf in self.tfs:
            length, s = sum(tf.values()), 0.0
            for t in tokens(query):
                if t in tf:
                    f = tf[t]
                    s += self.idf[t] * f * (self.k1 + 1) / (
                        f + self.k1 * (1 - self.b + self.b * length / self.avg))
            out.append(s)
        return out

def embed(text, dims=256):
    """Stand-in embedding: hashed word counts, normalised to length 1."""
    v = [0.0] * dims
    for t in tokens(text):
        v[int(hashlib.md5(t.encode()).hexdigest(), 16) % dims] += 1.0
    norm = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in v)) or 1.0
    return [x / norm for x in v]

def cosine(a, b):
    return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))

# ---------- 4. Hybrid search with permission filter and RRF ----------
def rrf(rankings, k=60):
    fused = Counter()
    for ranking in rankings:
        for rank, idx in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            fused[idx] += 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return [idx for idx, _ in fused.most_common()]

class Index:
    def __init__(self, docs):
        self.chunks = [c for d in docs for c in chunk_document(d)]
        self.bm25 = BM25(self.chunks)
        self.vectors = [embed(c["text"]) for c in self.chunks]
    def search(self, query, user_groups, k=3):
        allowed = [i for i, c in enumerate(self.chunks) if set(c["acl"]) & set(user_groups)]
        kw = self.bm25.scores(query)
        qv = embed(query)
        by_kw = sorted(allowed, key=lambda i: -kw[i])
        by_vec = sorted(allowed, key=lambda i: -cosine(qv, self.vectors[i]))
        return [self.chunks[i] for i in rrf([by_kw, by_vec])[:k]]

# ---------- 5. Freshness: which chunks changed since the last index? ----------
def diff_index(old_hashes, new_chunks):
    new = {c["chunk_id"]: c["hash"] for c in new_chunks}
    changed = [cid for cid, h in new.items() if old_hashes.get(cid) != h]
    deleted = [cid for cid in old_hashes if cid not in new]
    return changed, deleted

# ---------- 6. Build the Claude request with search_result blocks ----------
def claude_request(question, hits, model="claude-opus-5-5"):
    blocks = [{"type": "search_result", "source": h["url"],
               "title": f"{h['title']} - {h['section']} (updated {h['updated']})",
               "content": [{"type": "text", "text": h["text"]}],
               "citations": {"enabled": True}} for h in hits]
    blocks.append({"type": "text", "text": question +
                   "\nAnswer only from the search results. If they do not contain"
                   " the answer, say you don't know."})
    return {"model": model, "max_tokens": 1000,
            "messages": [{"role": "user", "content": blocks}]}

# ---------- 7. Evaluation: recall@k and MRR on a golden set ----------
GOLDEN = [  # question, user's groups, the chunk that must be retrieved
    ("How long may the smoke suite take?", ["qa"], "TS-01#0"),
    ("When does the nightly regression run?", ["dev"], "TS-01#1"),
    ("How many P2 bugs can be open at release?", ["pm"], "REL-07#0"),
    ("How fast must we be able to roll back?", ["qa"], "REL-07#1"),
    ("When are critical pen test findings due?", ["security"], "SEC-02#0"),
    ("When are critical pen test findings due?", ["qa"], None),  # must NOT leak
]

def evaluate(index, k=3):
    hits, rr, leaks = 0, 0.0, 0
    for q, groups, want in GOLDEN:
        got = [c["chunk_id"] for c in index.search(q, groups, k)]
        if want is None:
            leaks += any(g.startswith("SEC-02") for g in got)
            continue
        if want in got:
            hits += 1
            rr += 1.0 / (got.index(want) + 1)
    n = sum(1 for g in GOLDEN if g[2] is not None)
    return {"recall@k": round(hits / n, 3), "mrr": round(rr / n, 3), "permission_leaks": leaks}

Common misunderstandings
"RAG quality is mostly about the prompt."
Mostly it's extraction, chunking, metadata and search. If the right chunk isn't retrieved, the prompt can't help.
"Vector search is all you need."
Vectors miss exact identifiers and rare terms. Hybrid search with BM25 is the safer default.
"We'll tell the model not to reveal restricted documents."
Prompts aren't access control. Filter by permission before anything reaches the model.
"Citations mean the answer is correct."
They point at real passages, but the passage may not support the claim. Evaluate faithfulness and citation accuracy.
"Index once and you're done."
Documents, permissions and deletions change daily. Freshness needs a pipeline and a test.
"More chunks in the prompt is safer."
Beyond a point, extra chunks add cost, distraction and lost-in-the-middle errors. Rerank and keep the best.

92.14Review

Key takeaways
  • Production RAG is two pipelines (indexing and querying) around one index, and most quality comes from ingestion, chunking and search.
  • Clean extraction, structure-aware chunking and rich metadata (IDs, sections, ACLs, dates, hashes) are designed in from the start.
  • Use hybrid search (BM25 + vectors) fused with RRF, then rerank a shortlist; include identifier and synonym questions in tests.
  • Contextual retrieval adds a short, model-written context to each chunk before indexing; Anthropic reported 49% fewer retrieval failures (67% with reranking) on its tests.
  • Pass chunks to Claude as search_result blocks with citations enabled, tell it to answer only from them, and test abstention.
  • Enforce permissions before retrieval, sync changes and deletions, and evaluate retrieval (recall@k, MRR) and answers (faithfulness, citations, abstention, zero leaks) on every change.
