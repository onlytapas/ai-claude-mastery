A neural network is built from one very simple part repeated many times. An artificial neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up, adds a bias, and passes the total through an activation function, a small rule such as "if negative, output zero". Neurons are arranged in layers, each feeding the next. Running an input through every layer to get an output is the forward pass. The weights and biases are the network's parameters: the numbers training adjusts. Claude is, at heart, this same idea at enormous scale.

11.1Inspired by the brain, but not a brain

Your brain contains tens of billions of nerve cells, called neurons. Each one receives signals from others, and if the combined signal is strong enough, it "fires" and sends a signal on. In 1943 Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts proposed a mathematical cartoon of this, and in 1958 Frank Rosenblatt built the perceptron, a machine that learned its own weights (Chapter 3).

The artificial neuron kept only the bare idea: combine weighted inputs, then decide how strongly to respond. Everything else about real neurons (chemistry, timing, the way they grow and rewire) was left out.

Keep the analogy loose

"Neural network" is a historical name. Modern artificial networks are arithmetic, designed by engineers for what works on computer chips, not by copying biology. Claims like "this AI works just like the human brain" are marketing, not science.

11.2One neuron, worked by hand

An artificial neuron does three things:

1. WeighMultiply each input by its own weight
2. SumAdd the results together, then add the bias. Call the total z
3. ActivatePass z through the activation function to get the output

Let's make it concrete with a made-up QA example. Imagine a neuron that scores how risky a code change is. (The example and all its numbers are illustrative; in real life training would find the weights, as Chapter 12 explains.)

InputMeaningValueWeightWhy that weight
x1Does the change touch checkout code? (1 = yes, 0 = no)1.0w1 = 0.8Positive: touching checkout raises risk
x2Test coverage of the changed code (0.5 = 50%)0.5w2 = −0.4Negative: more coverage lowers risk
biasA baseline that applies whatever the inputs—b = 0.1Every change carries some risk

Step 1 and 2, the weighted sum:

By hand
z = (x1 × w1) + (x2 × w2) + b
  = (1.0 × 0.8) + (0.5 × −0.4) + 0.1
  =     0.8     +     −0.2     + 0.1
  = 0.7

Step 3, activation. What the neuron outputs depends on which activation function it uses. With the two most common ones:

  • ReLU (rectified linear unit): "if z is negative, output 0; otherwise output z". ReLU(0.7) = 0.7.
  • Sigmoid: squashes any number into the range 0 to 1, so it can be read like a probability. sigmoid(0.7) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−0.7) ≈ 0.668, roughly "67% risky".
In everyday life

Deciding whether to take an umbrella. Inputs: dark clouds (yes), forecast rain chance (30%), whether you're cycling (yes). You care about some inputs more than others; those are weights. You're naturally cautious, or naturally relaxed; that's the bias. Then you make a decision rather than just holding a number in your head; that's the activation. A neuron is that habit written as arithmetic.

What the weights and bias mean

  • A large positive weight means "this input pushes the output up a lot".
  • A negative weight means "this input pushes the output down".
  • A weight near zero means "ignore this input".
  • The bias shifts the whole sum up or down, setting how easily the neuron activates even when inputs are small.

In a trained network nobody chooses these numbers by hand, and individual weights rarely mean anything as tidy as "checkout risk". Understanding what learned weights represent is a research field of its own, called interpretability (Chapter 38).

11.3Activation functions: the small rule that makes it all work

Why not skip the activation and just output z? Because then the network could only ever learn straight-line relationships, no matter how many layers you stacked. A weighted sum of weighted sums is still just one weighted sum. The activation function adds a bend, and stacking many bends lets a network fit curved, complicated patterns. The technical word for "bendy" is non-linear.

Why ReLU won

Training adjusts each weight according to how much changing it would change the error (Chapter 12). That depends on the slope of the activation function. Sigmoid and tanh flatten out at both ends, so for large positive or negative z their slope is almost zero. In a deep network those tiny slopes get multiplied together layer after layer and the learning signal fades away: the "vanishing gradient" problem.

ReLU's slope is exactly 1 for any positive z, so the signal passes through undiminished. It's also extremely cheap to compute: a single comparison. Research around 2010–2011 (Nair and Hinton; Glorot, Bordes and Bengio) showed that it made deep networks much easier to train, and AlexNet used it in 2012 (Chapter 5).

ReLU has a weakness: a neuron whose z is always negative outputs 0 forever and stops learning (a "dead" neuron). Smoother variants such as GELU are common in Transformer language models. The principle is the same: a cheap non-linear bend after each weighted sum.

11.4From neurons to layers

One neuron can only draw one straight dividing line through its inputs. Power comes from many neurons working together. A layer is a group of neurons that all read the same inputs, each with its own weights and bias, so each one picks up on a different pattern. The outputs of one layer become the inputs of the next.

Input layerThe raw numbers: pixel brightness, word codes, feature values. No calculation here.
Hidden layersNeurons computing new features from the previous layer. "Hidden" because you never see their values directly.
Output layerThe answer: a category score, a number, or a probability for every possible next token.

When every neuron in a layer is connected to every output of the previous layer, it's called a fully connected (or "dense") layer. A network with more than one hidden layer is called deep, which is where deep learning gets its name.

Real-world example: recognising a handwritten digit

A classic beginner network reads 28 × 28 pixel images of handwritten digits: 784 inputs, one per pixel. It might have one hidden layer of 128 neurons and an output layer of 10 neurons, one for each digit 0–9. Early hidden neurons tend to respond to simple strokes and edges; the output neuron with the highest score is the prediction. Nobody programmed "a 7 has a horizontal bar and a diagonal"; training found weights that respond to such patterns.

In everyday life

A bug triage team works in stages. First-line testers each check one thing: does it crash, is it in checkout, does it affect many users. Leads combine those reports into "probably a payments outage" or "cosmetic issue". The release manager combines the leads' views into one decision. Each stage builds a more abstract judgement from the one before. Layers do the same with numbers.

11.5A tiny network that one neuron can't replace

Here is a famous small problem called XOR ("exclusive or"): output 1 if exactly one of two inputs is 1, otherwise 0. QA version: alert when exactly one of two mirrored servers is down; if both are up all is well, and if both are down another alarm already fires.

x1x2Wanted output
000
011
101
110

A single neuron cannot do this. Draw the four points on a grid and you'll find no single straight line that puts (0,1) and (1,0) on one side and (0,0) and (1,1) on the other. Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert highlighted limits like this in their 1969 book Perceptrons, which contributed to the first loss of confidence in neural networks (Chapter 4). The fix is a hidden layer.

The network

Two inputs, a hidden layer of two ReLU neurons, and one output neuron with no activation (it just outputs its sum). These weights are a well-known hand-made solution, used in Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville's Deep Learning textbook.

NeuronWeightsBiasActivationIn words
Hidden h11, 10ReLU"How many inputs are on?"
Hidden h21, 1−1ReLU"Are both on?" (only positive when both are 1)
Output y1, −20none"Count, minus a penalty of 2 if both are on"

The forward pass, by hand

Inputh1 = ReLU(x1 + x2)h2 = ReLU(x1 + x2 − 1)y = h1 − 2·h2Correct?
(0, 0)ReLU(0) = 0ReLU(−1) = 00 − 0 = 0Yes
(0, 1)ReLU(1) = 1ReLU(0) = 01 − 0 = 1Yes
(1, 0)ReLU(1) = 1ReLU(0) = 01 − 0 = 1Yes
(1, 1)ReLU(2) = 2ReLU(1) = 12 − 2 = 0Yes

Look at what the hidden layer did. It turned the raw inputs into two new, more useful features ("how many" and "both"), and the output layer combined them with a simple subtraction. That is the core trick of deep learning: hidden layers learn useful intermediate features, so the final decision becomes easy. In a large language model the intermediate features are far richer (grammar, topic, tone, who "it" refers to), but the mechanism is the same.

Try it without the activation

Remove ReLU from the hidden layer and recompute (0, 0): h2 becomes −1 instead of 0, and y = 0 − 2 × (−1) = 2. Wrong. Without the bend, the network collapses into a single straight-line function and can't solve XOR. The lab lets you watch this happen.

Large language models aren't built only from fully connected layers. They're Transformers, which also contain attention layers and embedding tables (Chapter 16). But much of their parameter count sits in fully connected "feed-forward" layers made of exactly the neurons in this chapter, and the counting principle is the same.

Total versus active parameters

Some recent models use a design called mixture of experts, where only part of the network runs for each token. Their announcements may give two figures: total parameters and "active" parameters per token. Read such numbers carefully when comparing models (Chapter 33), and remember that parameter count alone doesn't tell you how good a model is.

Under the hood: layers are matrix multiplications

Writing out every neuron separately is clear but slow. In practice, a layer's weights are stored as a grid of numbers, a matrix, with one row per neuron and one column per input. The inputs form a list, a vector. The whole layer's weighted sums are then one operation: matrix times vector, plus a bias vector. Then the activation is applied to every element.

The XOR hidden layer as a matrix (input (1, 1))
  W           x        b         z            ReLU(z)
[ 1  1 ]   [ 1 ]   [  0 ]    [ 2 ]          [ 2 ]
[ 1  1 ] × [ 1 ] + [ -1 ] =  [ 1 ]   →      [ 1 ]

Matrix multiplication is thousands of independent multiply-and-add operations that can all happen at once. That's exactly what GPUs are built for, which is why the rise of GPUs and the rise of deep learning went together (Chapter 5, Chapter 25). Libraries such as NumPy and PyTorch do this efficiently; the lab deliberately doesn't use them so you can see every step.

11.7The forward pass, and what it means for Claude

Feeding an input through every layer, in order, to produce an output is called the forward pass. It's all that happens during inference: no weights change, the numbers just flow through.

InputYour prompt, converted into token IDs and then into numbers (Chapter 14)
Many layersAttention and feed-forward layers, each refining the numbers
Output layerA score for every token in the vocabulary, turned into probabilities
Pick a tokenOne token is chosen and appended to the text
RepeatA new forward pass for each next token

So when Claude writes a 500-token answer, it runs roughly 500 forward passes, one per token (with engineering tricks that avoid recomputing earlier work). That's why longer answers take longer and cost more: each output token is a trip through the whole network.

Training adds a second direction. After a forward pass on a training example, the network's output is compared with the right answer, and a backward pass works out how to nudge every weight to reduce the error. That's backpropagation and gradient descent, the subject of Chapter 12.

Real-world example: why the same prompt can give different answers

A tester runs the same prompt twice and gets two different test plans. The forward pass itself is deterministic arithmetic, but the final step picks a token from a probability distribution, and that choice can involve randomness. Small differences early in the answer then send later forward passes down different paths. This is why AI outputs are called non-deterministic and why AI features need evals rather than single exact-match assertions (Chapter 125). Chapter 17 explains sampling.

  2. Create a file called tiny_net.py with this complete code:
    tiny_net.py
    # tiny_net.py - a neural network forward pass in plain Python (no libraries)
import math

def relu(z):
    return max(0.0, z)

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))

def neuron(inputs, weights, bias, activation):
    # weighted sum: multiply each input by its weight, add them up, add the bias
    z = sum(x * w for x, w in zip(inputs, weights)) + bias
    return activation(z)

def layer(inputs, weight_rows, biases, activation):
    # a layer is several neurons that all read the same inputs
    return [neuron(inputs, w, b, activation) for w, b in zip(weight_rows, biases)]

def identity(z):
    return z

# Part 1: one neuron, worked by hand in the chapter
z_inputs, z_weights, z_bias = [1.0, 0.5], [0.8, -0.4], 0.1
print("One neuron:")
print("  weighted sum z =", round(sum(x * w for x, w in zip(z_inputs, z_weights)) + z_bias, 3))
print("  ReLU output    =", round(neuron(z_inputs, z_weights, z_bias, relu), 3))
print("  sigmoid output =", round(neuron(z_inputs, z_weights, z_bias, sigmoid), 3))

# Part 2: a 2-2-1 network that computes XOR ("exactly one input is on")
hidden_weights = [[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0]]
hidden_biases = [0.0, -1.0]
output_weights = [[1.0, -2.0]]
output_biases = [0.0]

print("XOR network:")
for x in [[0, 0], [0, 1], [1, 0], [1, 1]]:
    h = layer(x, hidden_weights, hidden_biases, relu)
    y = layer(h, output_weights, output_biases, identity)
    print(f"  input {x} -> hidden {h} -> output {y[0]}")

# Part 3: count parameters (weights + biases) for any layer sizes
def count_parameters(sizes):
    total = 0
    for n_in, n_out in zip(sizes, sizes[1:]):
        total += n_in * n_out + n_out
    return total

print("Parameters in 2-2-1 network:", count_parameters([2, 2, 1]))
print("Parameters in 784-128-10 network:", count_parameters([784, 128, 10]))

Troubleshooting

  • python3: command not found: on Windows use python or py; otherwise install Python 3 from python.org.
  • IndentationError: re-copy the code and use spaces only.
  • NameError: name 'identity' is not defined after editing: make sure the def identity function is still above Part 1.
  • Numbers like 0.30000000000000004: normal floating-point rounding in any language; the code rounds where it matters.
Common misunderstandings
"Artificial neurons work like brain cells."
They borrow one idea: combine weighted inputs and respond. Everything else is engineering arithmetic.
"Activation functions are optional extras."
Without them, any number of layers collapses into one straight-line function. They're what makes depth useful.
"Each neuron stores a fact."
Knowledge is spread across many weights, and a single neuron often responds to several unrelated things. Interpretability research tries to untangle this (Chapter 38).
"More parameters always means a better model."
Data quality, training method and design matter as much. Smaller recent models often beat larger older ones.
"The model updates its weights while it answers me."
Answering is forward passes only. Weights change only during training.

11.8Review

Key takeaways
  • A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and applies an activation function.
  • Weights set how much each input matters (and in which direction); the bias shifts the threshold.
  • Activation functions such as ReLU and sigmoid add the non-linear bend that lets stacked layers learn complex patterns.
  • Layers of neurons build increasingly useful features; hidden layers are what let a network solve XOR and far harder problems.
  • Parameters are all the weights and biases. A dense layer has inputs × neurons + neurons of them.
  • The forward pass is inference. An LLM runs one forward pass per generated token; training adds a backward pass to adjust weights.
Check yourself

A neuron has inputs (2, 1), weights (0.5, −1) and bias 0.5. What is its ReLU output?

z = 2×0.5 + 1×(−1) + 0.5 = 1 − 1 + 0.5 = 0.5. ReLU of a positive number is the number itself.

Why do neural networks need activation functions?

A weighted sum of weighted sums is still one weighted sum. The non-linear bend is what makes depth worthwhile.

How many parameters does a fully connected layer with 10 inputs and 4 neurons have?

10 × 4 = 40 weights, plus one bias for each of the 4 neurons.

What does the sigmoid function do to a weighted sum?

Sigmoid gives 0.5 at z = 0 and approaches 0 or 1 at the extremes. Setting negatives to zero is ReLU; rounding to 0 or 1 is the step function.

Claude writes a 300-token answer. Roughly how many forward passes did that take?

Each new token needs a pass through the network. Inference is forward passes; training adds backward passes.

Teach it

"A neural network is one tiny calculation repeated millions of times. Each neuron multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them, adds a bias, then applies a simple rule like 'if it's negative, make it zero'. That rule matters: without it, stacking layers gains nothing. Neurons sit in layers, and each layer turns the previous layer's numbers into more useful features, which is how a two-layer network solves a problem one neuron can't. All the weights and biases together are the parameters; Claude has billions. When Claude answers, it just runs numbers forward through those layers, once per word-piece it writes. Learning the weights is a separate step called training."
