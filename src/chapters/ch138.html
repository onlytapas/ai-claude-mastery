Training a large language model means showing it trillions of words and adjusting billions of numbers after each batch. No single chip has enough memory or speed for that, so the work is split across thousands of GPUs or TPUs that must stay in step, connected by very fast networks. How to split it (by data, by layer, or by slicing each layer) is an engineering craft of its own. Deciding how big a model to build and how much data to feed it is guided by scaling laws: smooth, predictable relationships between compute, model size, data and quality. Those laws are why labs can plan a training run costing many millions before they start it.

138.1Why training is so much bigger than using

Chapter 25 looked at inference: running a finished model to answer you. Training is a different scale of problem. Inference reads the weights once per token. Training has to, for every token in a dataset of trillions:

  1. run the model forwards to make a prediction (the forward pass),
  2. work out how wrong it was and how each weight contributed (backpropagation),
  3. nudge every weight a little (gradient descent).

A widely used approximation from Kaplan et al. (2020) puts the total training work at about 6 × N × D floating-point operations (FLOPs), where N is the number of parameters and D the number of training tokens. Roughly 2 of those 6 are the forward pass and 4 the backward pass.

Real-world example: a sense of scale

Meta reported training Llama 3.1 405B on "over 15 trillion tokens" using "over 16 thousand H100 GPUs". Plugging N = 405 billion and D = 15 trillion into 6 × N × D gives about 3.6 × 1025 FLOPs. If you could do one calculation per second, that would take about 80 million times the age of the universe. Even at the speed of thousands of GPUs, it takes weeks to months.

In everyday life

Inference is a chef cooking one meal from a finished recipe. Training is writing the recipe book from scratch by cooking millions of test dishes, tasting each one, and adjusting every recipe slightly after each tasting. You don't do that in one kitchen with one chef.

138.2Where the memory goes

Running a model at 16-bit precision needs about 2 bytes per parameter (Chapter 32). Training needs far more, because you must also keep the gradients (the "which way to nudge" for each weight) and the optimiser's running statistics.

What's storedBytes per parameter (typical mixed-precision setup)Why
Weights in 16-bit2Used for the fast forward and backward passes
Gradients in 16-bit2One "nudge direction" per weight
Master copy of weights in 32-bit4Small updates would be lost in 16-bit rounding
Adam optimiser: two running averages in 32-bit8Adam tracks momentum and variance for every weight
Total, before activations16Eight times the 2 bytes needed just to run it

This accounting comes from the ZeRO paper (Rajbhandari et al., 2019) and is a standard starting point. On top of it come activations: the intermediate results of the forward pass, kept so the backward pass can use them. They grow with batch size and sequence length and can be as large as everything else combined.

So a 70-billion-parameter model needs about 70 × 16 = 1,120 GB just for weights, gradients and optimiser state. One NVIDIA H100 has 80 GB of memory. Even before activations, that's at least 14 GPUs, and that's only to hold one copy of the training state.

Under the hood: mixed precision and activation recomputation

Mixed-precision training does the heavy matrix maths in 16-bit formats (or 8-bit on newer hardware), which is faster and uses less memory, while keeping a 32-bit master copy of the weights so that tiny updates aren't rounded away. The format bfloat16 is popular because it keeps the same range of values as 32-bit numbers while giving up some precision.

Activation recomputation (also called gradient checkpointing) trades compute for memory: instead of storing every intermediate result, the system stores a few and recomputes the rest during the backward pass. It typically adds a fraction more compute but can cut activation memory dramatically. Real training runs combine this with the parallelism techniques below until everything fits.

138.3Splitting the work: three kinds of parallelism

There are three classic ways to divide training across many chips. Real systems combine all three, often called "3D parallelism".

In everyday life

Imagine marking 100,000 exam scripts. Data parallelism: give each of 100 teachers their own copy of the marking scheme and 1,000 scripts; they compare notes at the end of each day. Pipeline parallelism: one teacher marks question 1, passes the script to the next who marks question 2, and so on, like an assembly line. Tensor parallelism: one enormous question is split so that four teachers each mark a quarter of it at the same moment and immediately combine their scores.

TypeWhat is splitWhat must be sharedMain limitationLandmark paper
Data parallelismThe training data: each chip has a full copy of the model and processes different examplesGradients, averaged across all chips after every stepEach chip must hold the whole model and its training stateStandard technique; ZeRO (Rajbhandari et al., 2019) removes the duplicated memory
Pipeline parallelismThe layers: chip 1 holds layers 1–20, chip 2 layers 21–40, and so onActivations passed from stage to stage"Bubbles": stages sit idle while the pipeline fills and drainsGPipe (Huang et al., 2018)
Tensor parallelismEach layer's big matrices, sliced across chipsPartial results, exchanged inside every layerNeeds extremely fast links, so usually kept within one serverMegatron-LM (Shoeybi et al., 2019)

Two more appear in modern systems. Sharded data parallelism (the ZeRO family, and PyTorch's FSDP) keeps data parallelism but splits the optimiser state, gradients and even weights across chips instead of copying them, fetching pieces only when needed. Expert parallelism places the different experts of a mixture-of-experts model on different chips and routes each token to the chips holding its chosen experts.

Under the hood: what the papers reported
  • Megatron-LM (2019) showed "a simple, efficient intra-layer model parallel approach" and trained transformer models of up to 8.3 billion parameters on 512 GPUs, sustaining 15.1 petaFLOPs with 76% scaling efficiency.
  • GPipe (2018) introduced a batch-splitting pipelining algorithm: a batch is split into smaller micro-batches so different stages work on different micro-batches at once, shrinking the idle bubble. It reported almost linear speed-up and trained a 6-billion-parameter, 128-layer Transformer for translation across over 100 languages.
  • ZeRO (2019) "eliminates memory redundancies in data- and model-parallel training", reported training models over 100 billion parameters on 400 GPUs, and could train models up to 13 billion parameters without any model parallelism.

The communication pattern behind data parallelism is called all-reduce: every chip contributes its gradients and every chip ends up with the average. Its cost grows with model size, not with the number of examples, which is why network speed matters so much.

138.4Clusters: thousands of chips acting as one

A training cluster is a building, or part of one, full of servers, each with several accelerators, linked by networks designed for this one job. It is closer to a single giant computer than to an office network.

ChipOne GPU or TPU with its own fast memory (an H100 SXM has 80 GB at 3.35 TB/s)
ServerSeveral chips joined by very fast links inside the box (NVLink on NVIDIA systems): ideal for tensor parallelism
ClusterThousands of servers joined by a high-speed network fabric: used for pipeline and data parallelism
StorageTraining data streams in; checkpoints stream out

The hardware families (NVIDIA GPUs, Google TPUs, AWS Trainium) and the energy and cooling story are in Chapter 25. What changes at training scale is coordination:

  • The slowest chip sets the pace. In data parallelism, every chip waits for the gradient average at each step. One slow or faulty chip slows thousands.
  • Failures are routine. With tens of thousands of components running for weeks, hardware faults, network hiccups and software bugs are expected, not exceptional.
  • Checkpoints are the safety net. The full training state is saved regularly so a failed run can restart from the last checkpoint instead of from zero.
  • Monitoring is constant. Engineers watch the loss curve for sudden spikes or stalls, which can signal bad data, numerical instability or a hardware fault.
Real-world example: it's a QA problem

A large training run looks a lot like a long-running production system under test. Teams track a "health dashboard" (loss, throughput per chip, failed nodes, time lost to restarts), run small-scale rehearsals before the big run, and keep evaluation suites that run on checkpoints along the way, much like a regression suite running on every build. If you've ever triaged a flaky nightly test run, you understand the daily life of a training-infrastructure team.

138.5Scaling laws: predicting quality before you train

Plain idea first. If you train the same kind of model several times at small sizes, and plot how good each one gets against how much compute it used, the points fall on a smooth curve. That curve keeps going in the same way when you go much bigger. So you can run cheap small experiments, draw the line, and predict roughly how good an expensive large model will be before you spend the money. That predictable relationship is a scaling law.

In everyday life

A runner who logs training hours and race times can predict, roughly, what extra training will buy: big improvements at first, smaller ones later, but in a steady pattern. Scaling laws are that training log for AI models, measured across many orders of magnitude.

  1. Jan 2020Kaplan et al., "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (OpenAI). Loss "scales as a power-law with model size, dataset size, and the amount of compute used for training", across a very wide range. Architectural details like width or depth mattered little. "Larger models are significantly more sample-efficient", so the paper concluded that the compute-efficient approach is to train very large models on a relatively modest amount of data and stop well before convergence.
  2. 2020–2021Very large models follow. Labs built models with hundreds of billions of parameters trained on a few hundred billion tokens, consistent with the "make the model bigger" reading of Kaplan.
  3. Mar 2022Hoffmann et al., "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (DeepMind), the "Chinchilla paper". After over 400 training runs it found that current large models were "significantly undertrained" and that "the model size and the number of training tokens should be scaled equally". Chinchilla, at 70 billion parameters trained on about four times more data than the 280-billion-parameter Gopher with the same compute, outperformed Gopher, GPT-3 and others, reaching 67.5% average accuracy on MMLU.
  4. 2023 onwardTraining past "optimal". Many open models are trained on far more tokens per parameter than Chinchilla suggests. Llama 3.1 405B used over 15 trillion tokens, about 37 per parameter.

From the Chinchilla results, people commonly derive a rule of thumb of about 20 training tokens per parameter for compute-optimal training (70 billion parameters × 20 = 1.4 trillion tokens). It's a rule of thumb, not a law of nature, and it depends on the data and the setup.

Kaplan (2020) in one line

With more compute, grow the model much faster than the data.

Chinchilla (2022) in one line

With more compute, grow the model and the data at the same rate.

Under the hood: power laws, and why the two papers disagreed

A power law has the form loss ≈ a × C−b, where C is compute and b a small positive number. Take logarithms of both sides and it becomes a straight line: log(loss) = log(a) − b × log(C). That's why scaling-law plots use log-log axes and why the results look like straight lines. The lab below fits exactly such a line.

The two papers reached different allocations partly because of experimental choices. The Chinchilla authors point out that Kaplan's runs used the same number of training tokens and learning-rate schedule across model sizes, which tends to understate how much smaller models gain from longer training. Chinchilla tuned the schedule to each run's length and used three different estimation methods, which agreed with one another. Differences in how parameters were counted and in the scale of the experiments also contributed.

Two caveats matter. First, scaling laws predict the loss (how well the model predicts the next token), not specific abilities; some skills appear to improve gradually and others more abruptly on particular benchmarks. Second, "compute-optimal" means optimal for training cost. If a model will answer billions of requests, a smaller model trained for longer can be cheaper overall because every answer costs less to serve. That's a common explanation for why today's open models are trained far beyond 20 tokens per parameter.

Opinion · debated across the field

Whether scaling pre-training will keep paying off is openly argued. Some researchers and lab leaders expect continued gains from more compute; others point to limits on high-quality text and to rising costs, and argue that the next gains come from other axes, such as reinforcement learning after pre-training and spending more compute at answer time (test-time compute, Chapter 19). Treat any confident forecast either way as an opinion. Chapter 140 looks at research frontiers.

138.6Energy, cost and what it means for you

All this compute turns into electricity and heat. A rough way to think about a run's energy is: number of chips × power per chip (plus cooling and networking) × hours. With thousands of chips running for weeks, the total is large, but exact energy figures for specific frontier models are rarely published, so treat any number you see quoted for a closed model with care. Chapter 25 gives the International Energy Agency's estimates for data centres overall.

For a QA engineer or manager, three practical lessons follow. First, model releases come in generations because each training run is a huge, planned project, not a nightly build. Second, the "knowledge cutoff" (Chapter 20) exists because the training data is frozen when the run starts. Third, when a vendor says a new model is "better at the same price", one reason can be a smaller, longer-trained model that's cheaper to serve, which links directly to Chapter 139.

scale_calc.py
# scale_calc.py - back-of-envelope training estimates. Rules of thumb, not a simulator.
import math

TOKENS_PER_PARAM = 20      # Chinchilla-style rule of thumb for compute-optimal training
BYTES_PER_PARAM = 16       # mixed-precision Adam: weights, gradients, optimiser state
GPU_MEMORY_GB = 80         # one H100 SXM has 80 GB
GPU_FLOPS = 400e12         # ILLUSTRATIVE effective speed per GPU (FLOP per second)
NUM_GPUS = 1024            # ILLUSTRATIVE cluster size

def estimate(params, tokens=None):
    tokens = tokens or params * TOKENS_PER_PARAM
    flops = 6 * params * tokens                       # about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token
    hours = flops / (GPU_FLOPS * NUM_GPUS) / 3600
    state_gb = params * BYTES_PER_PARAM / 1e9
    min_gpus = math.ceil(state_gb / GPU_MEMORY_GB)    # ignores activations: a lower bound
    return tokens, flops, hours, state_gb, min_gpus

print(f"{'model':<14}{'tokens':>10}{'FLOPs':>11}{'hours':>10}{'state GB':>10}{'min GPUs':>10}")
for name, n, d in [("1B", 1e9, None), ("8B", 8e9, None), ("70B", 70e9, None),
                   ("405B @ 15T", 405e9, 15e12)]:
    tokens, flops, hours, state_gb, min_gpus = estimate(n, d)
    print(f"{name:<14}{tokens/1e12:>9.2f}T{flops:>11.1e}{hours:>10,.1f}{state_gb:>10,.0f}{min_gpus:>10}")

powerlaw.py
# powerlaw.py - fit a straight line on a log-log plot, the way scaling-law papers do.
import math

# ILLUSTRATIVE (made-up) results: compute used (FLOPs) and the loss each run reached
runs = [(1e18, 4.20), (1e19, 3.62), (1e20, 3.11), (1e21, 2.68), (1e22, 2.31)]

xs = [math.log10(c) for c, _ in runs]
ys = [math.log10(l) for _, l in runs]
n = len(runs)
mx, my = sum(xs) / n, sum(ys) / n
slope = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys)) / sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
intercept = my - slope * mx

print(f"loss ~= {10 ** intercept:.1f} * C^({slope:.3f})")
for c in (1e23, 1e24):
    print(f"predicted loss at {c:.0e} FLOPs: {10 ** (intercept + slope * math.log10(c)):.2f}")

Troubleshooting
  • SyntaxError on the f-strings: you're on Python 2 or a very old Python 3. Run python3 --version; you need 3.6 or newer.
  • Numbers differ slightly: check you copied the constants exactly; the outputs above come from the scripts as printed.
  • "Why is the H100 speed only 400 TFLOP/s?": it's a deliberately round, illustrative figure for real-world sustained throughput, not NVIDIA's peak specification.
Common misunderstandings
"Scaling laws say bigger models are always better."
They describe how loss falls with compute, data and size together. Chinchilla showed a smaller model with more data can beat a bigger one at the same compute.
"Training just needs the same memory as running the model."
Training needs roughly eight times more for weights, gradients and optimiser state alone, before activations.
"More GPUs means proportionally faster training."
Communication, pipeline bubbles and stragglers mean efficiency drops as clusters grow. Papers report scaling efficiency precisely because it's below 100%.
"20 tokens per parameter is the right amount for every model."
It's a rule of thumb for minimising training compute. Models meant to serve many users are often trained much longer.
"Scaling laws predict what a model will be able to do."
They predict the loss. Specific abilities still have to be measured with evaluations.

138.7Review

Key takeaways
  • Training compute is roughly 6 × parameters × tokens FLOPs; frontier runs reach the order of 1025 and beyond.
  • Training needs about 16 bytes per parameter before activations, so even a 70B model's state spans many 80 GB GPUs.
  • Data parallelism splits examples, pipeline parallelism splits layers, tensor parallelism splits each layer's matrices; real systems combine them, plus sharding (ZeRO/FSDP) and expert parallelism.
  • Clusters are tightly coupled machines where failures are routine, so checkpoints and monitoring are essential.
  • Kaplan et al. (2020) showed loss follows power laws in size, data and compute; Chinchilla (2022) showed size and data should grow together, about 20 tokens per parameter as a rule of thumb.
  • Labs often train smaller models for longer than "compute-optimal" because they're cheaper to serve.
