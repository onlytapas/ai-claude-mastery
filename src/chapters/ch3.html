Before you start: Chapter 2 introduces Turing and Shannon.

Between 1943 and the mid-1960s, a small group of researchers turned the question "can machines think?" into a scientific field. Turing proposed a practical test. Others described artificial neurons. In 1956 a summer workshop at Dartmouth College gave the field its name. Early programs proved maths theorems, played games and followed logical rules, and their creators predicted human-level AI within a generation. They were far too optimistic, but they laid foundations still in use today.

3.1The first artificial neuron (1943)

In 1943 the neuroscientist Warren McCulloch and the logician Walter Pitts published a simple mathematical model of a brain cell. Their "neuron" receives several on/off signals, adds them up, and fires (outputs 1) if the total passes a threshold. They showed that networks of these simple units could, in principle, compute logical functions.

In everyday life

Picture a committee that approves a project only if at least three of its five members vote yes. Each member is an input; the threshold is three. That's a McCulloch–Pitts neuron. Connect many committees, each feeding its decision into others, and you get a network that can make complicated decisions out of simple votes.

This idea lay mostly dormant for decades, but it's the ancestor of the neural networks inside every modern AI model (Chapter 11).

3.2Turing asks: can machines think? (1950)

In 1950 Alan Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in the philosophy journal Mind. It opens: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" He then sets that question aside, because "think" is too vague to argue about, and replaces it with a game.

The imitation game

A judgetypes questions and reads answers on a screen, without seeing who answers
A personanswers honestly, trying to help the judge
A machineanswers too, trying to seem human
The verdictIf the judge can't reliably tell which is which, the machine passes

This became known as the Turing Test. Its strength is that it's practical: it judges behaviour, which you can observe, rather than inner experience, which you can't.

The paper also answers objections, several of which you still hear today:

ObjectionThe argumentTuring's reply, briefly
Lady Lovelace's objectionMachines only do what they're told; they can't originate anything (Chapter 2)Machines can surprise their makers, and a learning machine could go beyond its programming
ConsciousnessA machine can't feel or truly know what it's sayingWe can't directly verify that other people feel either; we judge by behaviour
"Machines can't do X"They'll never be kind, creative, fall in love, enjoy strawberries…These claims are usually based on the limited machines people have seen so far
Mathematical limitsSome problems no machine can solveThat's true, but there's no proof humans escape those limits either

Most strikingly, Turing suggested building a "child machine" and educating it, rather than trying to program adult intelligence directly: "Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child's?" That is essentially the idea behind machine learning, decades before it worked.

Real-world example: is the Turing Test passed?

Modern chatbots can hold conversations many people can't distinguish from a person's, at least for short chats, and researchers have run formal Turing-style tests with large language models. But most researchers no longer treat the test as the measure of intelligence: a system can imitate human conversation well while still failing at reliability, reasoning or honesty. AI is now usually judged with many specific tests instead (Chapter 24).

3.3Dartmouth 1956: AI gets its name

In 1955 four researchers wrote a proposal for a summer research project at Dartmouth College in New Hampshire:

JMJohn McCarthyDartmouth mathematician who coined the term "artificial intelligence" and later created the LISP programming language.
MMMarvin MinskyHarvard researcher who later co-founded MIT's AI lab.
NRNathaniel RochesterIBM engineer who designed IBM's first commercial scientific computer.
CSClaude ShannonBell Labs, the founder of information theory (Chapter 2).

Their proposal made a bold claim that set the tone for the field:

From the 1955 proposal

"The study is to proceed on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it."

The workshop ran over the summer of 1956. It produced no single breakthrough, but it brought the key people together and gave the field a name and an ambition. Most histories date the start of AI as a field to this event.

3.4Symbolic AI: intelligence as rules and symbols

The dominant approach for the next thirty years is now called symbolic AI, sometimes nicknamed "good old-fashioned AI". The idea: represent knowledge as symbols (words, facts, logical statements) and write rules for manipulating them. Intelligence would come from searching through possibilities and applying logic.

Symbolic approach

A person writes the rules: "IF temperature > 38°C AND cough THEN suspect infection." The computer applies them precisely.

Learning approach (later)

The computer is shown thousands of patient records with diagnoses and finds the patterns itself (Chapter 5).

In everyday life

Symbolic AI is like a cook who follows recipes perfectly but can't cook anything without a recipe. A learning system is more like a cook who has eaten and cooked thousands of meals and has a feel for what works, but can't always explain why.

3.5The first AI programs

  1. 1952–59Arthur Samuel's checkers program at IBM improved by playing against itself. In 1959 Samuel used the phrase "machine learning" for this kind of self-improvement.
  2. 1955–56Logic Theorist, by Allen Newell, Herbert Simon and Cliff Shaw, proved theorems from Principia Mathematica. It's often called the first AI program and was shown around the time of Dartmouth.
  3. 1957–59General Problem Solver (Newell and Simon) tried to solve any problem that could be described as goals and steps.
  4. 1958Perceptron. Frank Rosenblatt built a machine that learned to recognise simple patterns, a learning version of the 1943 neuron. The press predicted machines that would walk, talk and be conscious.
  5. 1958LISP, created by John McCarthy, becomes the main language of AI research for decades.
  6. 1964–66ELIZA, Joseph Weizenbaum's chatbot, imitates a therapist with simple word patterns (Chapter 6).
  7. 1966–72Shakey, at the Stanford Research Institute, is the first mobile robot to reason about its own actions: it could plan a route and push boxes.
How the Perceptron learned

The Perceptron took inputs (for example, light sensors arranged in a grid looking at a printed letter), multiplied each by a weight, added them up, and output "yes" if the total passed a threshold. The new part was learning. When it got an example wrong, it nudged the weights: increase the weights of inputs that should have pushed towards the right answer, decrease the others. Repeat over many examples, and the weights settle on values that classify correctly.

The Perceptron learning rule, in plain pseudo-code
for each training example:
    guess = 1 if (w1*x1 + w2*x2 + ... + bias) > 0 else 0
    error = correct_answer - guess          # 0 if right, +1 or -1 if wrong
    for each weight i:
        w[i] = w[i] + learning_rate * error * x[i]
    bias = bias + learning_rate * error

This "guess, measure the error, adjust slightly, repeat" loop is the ancestor of how every modern neural network is trained (Chapter 12). Its weakness, which you'll meet in Chapter 4, is that a single layer of these units can only learn very simple patterns.

3.6The age of optimism

Early results were exciting, and well-known researchers made bold predictions:

Predictions that didn't come true
  • Herbert Simon (1965): "machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work a man can do."
  • Marvin Minsky (1967): "Within a generation… the problem of creating 'artificial intelligence' will substantially be solved."

Why were such brilliant people so wrong? Three reasons:

  1. Toy problems. Early programs worked in tiny, tidy worlds (a chessboard, a few blocks on a table). Real problems are vastly larger and messier.
  2. Hidden common sense. Everyday tasks rely on huge amounts of knowledge we don't notice we have (Moravec's paradox, Chapter 1).
  3. Not enough computing power or data. The computers of the 1960s had less memory than a modern greeting card that plays a song.
Real-world lesson

When you hear confident predictions about AI today, remember 1965. Experts are often right about the direction and wrong about the timing. Good practice is to test claims against what systems can actually do now.

Common misunderstandings
"Passing the Turing Test means a machine is intelligent."
Turing proposed it as a practical test, not a proof. Systems can imitate conversation while lacking other abilities.
"Early AI was a failure."
It overpromised, but produced lasting tools: search algorithms, LISP, and the ideas behind neural networks and machine learning.
"Neural networks are a recent invention."
The first artificial neuron model dates from 1943 and the Perceptron from 1958.

3.7Review

Key takeaways
  • McCulloch and Pitts (1943) described the first artificial neuron.
  • Turing (1950) replaced "can machines think?" with a practical imitation game and anticipated machine learning.
  • The 1956 Dartmouth workshop (McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, Shannon) named and launched the field.
  • Symbolic AI represented knowledge as rules and symbols; early programs proved theorems and played games.
  • Rosenblatt's Perceptron (1958) learned from examples by adjusting weights.
  • Researchers predicted human-level AI within decades; they badly underestimated real-world complexity.
