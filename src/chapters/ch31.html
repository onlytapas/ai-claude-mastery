Before you start: you should know what a token is (Chapter 14). It helps, but isn't required, to have read about reasoning and thinking time (Chapter 19) and the AI companies (Chapter 26).

No single AI model is best at everything. A bigger, slower, more expensive model is usually more capable; a smaller one is faster and far cheaper. So every major company sells a family: a few sizes (tiers), sometimes with a "thinking" mode, plus specialised models for jobs like coding, images or search. Each model also has a version and, eventually, a retirement date. Choosing well means matching the model to the job, the speed you need and the budget you have.

31.1One company, many models

Open the model picker in Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini and you'll see several names. New users often ask: "Why not just give me the best one?" The answer is that "best" depends on what you're doing. Every model is a trade-off between three things:

Capability

How well it handles hard, unfamiliar or long tasks: reasoning through a tricky bug, reviewing a 200-page contract, running a long agentic job.

Speed and cost

How quickly the answer arrives (latency) and how much each call costs. Both rise with model size and with how much "thinking" the model does.

You can't max out all of these at once. A model that thinks hard about every question is slow and expensive. A model that answers in a blink is cheap but makes more mistakes on hard problems. So companies build a range and let you choose.

In everyday life

A hospital has consultants, junior doctors and nurses. You don't send every patient to the most senior consultant: they're expensive, booked up, and wasted on a sprained ankle. You also wouldn't send a complex heart case to a first-week trainee. AI model tiers work the same way. Route easy, high-volume work to the fast, cheap tier; save the top tier for the cases that need it.

There are four main reasons the menu keeps growing:

31.2Sizes and tiers

A model's "size" usually means its number of parameters: the adjustable numbers learned during training. More parameters let a model store more knowledge and more subtle patterns, but every answer then needs more computation, which means more chips, more electricity, more time and a higher price per token.

Companies rarely publish exact parameter counts for their closed models. Instead they give each size a name: a tier. Anthropic's current tiers make a clear worked example.

Real-world example

A QA team uses AI in three places. A bot labels every incoming Jira ticket as "bug", "feature request" or "question": thousands of short, easy calls, so a Haiku-class model fits. Testers ask for test cases from a user story a few dozen times a day: a mid tier like Sonnet works well. Once a sprint, an agent investigates a flaky end-to-end suite across a large repository: that long, tricky job justifies Opus. Same company, three tiers, three jobs.

How companies make small models good: distillation and Mixture of Experts

Two techniques explain why today's small and mid-sized models are far better than you'd expect from their size.

Distillation. A large "teacher" model produces answers (and sometimes its full probability for each next token), and a smaller "student" model is trained to imitate them. The student learns from a cleaner, richer signal than raw internet text, so it gets much of the teacher's skill at a fraction of the running cost. This is one reason the cheapest tier of a new generation can match the middle tier of the previous one.

Mixture of Experts (MoE). Instead of one giant block that runs fully for every token, the model contains many "expert" sub-networks and a small router that picks a few of them per token. So a model can have a huge total parameter count but a much smaller number of active parameters per token. Published examples: Mistral Large 4 is described as about 1 trillion parameters with about 52 billion active; Microsoft's MAI-Thinking-1 as about 1 trillion total with about 35 billion active. Total size drives how much knowledge fits; active size drives speed and cost per token.

Because of both techniques, "bigger is better" is only a rough rule. A well-distilled or MoE model can beat an older, denser model several times its size.

31.3Fast answers versus reasoning

The second axis is not size but how long the model thinks. A reasoning model writes out intermediate working before giving its final answer. That extra "thinking time" improves results on maths, debugging, planning and multi-step tasks, but costs more tokens and takes longer. Chapter 19 explains how this works.

In 2024–2025 many companies sold reasoning models as separate products (OpenAI's o1 line was the best-known early example). The trend since then has been to merge the two: one model that decides how much to think, with a setting you can turn up or down. Anthropic called Claude 3.7 Sonnet (February 2025) "the first hybrid reasoning model on the market". Today's Claude models use adaptive thinking, and you steer the amount of effort with an effort level rather than picking a different model.

Quick question"What does HTTP 409 mean?"
Low effortShort or no thinking; fast, cheap answer
Hard problem"Why does this test fail only on CI, only on Tuesdays?"
High effortLonger reasoning; slower, more tokens, better odds
Two dials, not one

Tier and effort are separate choices. Haiku at high effort is still a small model; Opus at low effort is still a large one. For a hard task, a larger model at moderate effort often beats a small model at maximum effort. For a simple task, extra effort on a big model mostly burns money.

In everyday life

A good general practitioner can treat most things, but the hospital still has an X-ray machine and a blood-test lab. Those machines don't diagnose; they do one narrow job very well and hand results to the doctor. Embedding models are like the lab: fast, cheap, narrow, and feeding the general model.

31.5Versions, snapshots, aliases and retirement

Models also multiply over time. Each generation arrives while older ones are still in use, because businesses need time to test and switch. Three ideas matter here.

Snapshots. A model snapshot is one fixed, frozen version of a model. If you call the same snapshot next month, you get the same model (though, being non-deterministic, not necessarily word-for-word the same answer). Older Claude IDs put the snapshot date in the name, for example claude-3-5-sonnet-20240620. From the Claude 4.6 generation onward, Anthropic's IDs have no date (for example claude-sonnet-5-5) but are still pinned snapshots.

Aliases. An alias is a friendly name that may point to whatever the vendor currently considers the latest version. For the current Claude models, Anthropic lists the API ID and the alias as the same string. Other vendors use moving aliases such as "latest". Before you rely on a name, check whether it is pinned or moving.

Deprecation and retirement. A deprecated model still works but has a published end date; a retired model stops answering. Anthropic publishes these dates:

  1. Nov 2024Claude 1.x and Instant 1.x retired (6 Nov 2024).
  2. Jul 2025Claude 2.0, 2.1 and Claude 3 Sonnet retired (21 Jul 2025).
  3. Oct 2025Claude 3.5 Sonnet retired, both versions (28 Oct 2025).
  4. Feb 2026Claude 3.7 Sonnet and Claude 3.5 Haiku retired (19 Feb 2026).
  5. Jun 2026Claude Opus 4 and Sonnet 4 retired (15 Jun 2026).
  6. Nov 2026Claude Sonnet 4.5 retires 30 Nov 2026. Deprecated now; any pipeline still calling it must move.
  7. 2027Current models: Anthropic states Fable 5.1, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 will not retire before Sep–Oct 2027.
Why testers should care

A model change is a dependency upgrade. When your team's test-generation script, triage bot or AI feature moves from one model to another, its behaviour changes: wording, format, what it refuses, how often it calls tools. Treat it like upgrading a library. Pin the model ID in configuration, keep a small set of golden answers, rerun them before switching, and put the retirement date in the team calendar. Chapter 36 builds a harness for exactly this, and Chapter 125 covers evals.

Why a new model can cost more for the same text

Price per token isn't the whole story, because different models can split the same text into different numbers of tokens. Anthropic's documentation notes that Claude 4.7 and later use a newer tokenizer that produces "approximately 30% more tokens for the same text". So when comparing an older model with a newer one, compare the cost of a real task (measured in your own test run), not just the price list. Chapter 14 explains tokenizers.

31.6Pricing tiers in practice

API prices are quoted per million tokens (MTok), separately for input (what you send) and output (what the model writes). Output costs more, typically four to five times the input price. Here is the output price across Claude's current tiers:

Claude API list prices, verified 9 November 2026 (platform.claude.com pricing page). Haiku 5.5 price applies to prompts up to 100K tokens; above that it is $2.50. Cloud platforms such as Bedrock and Vertex set their own prices.

That is a 100-fold spread between the cheapest and most expensive tier. Three more levers change the bill:

  • Batch processing. If you can wait (most batches finish within an hour), Anthropic's Message Batches API is 50% off. Good for overnight jobs like re-labelling a backlog.
  • Prompt caching. If many calls share the same long prefix (instructions, a style guide, a codebase summary), cached reads cost a small fraction of the normal input price (for example 0.05× on Opus 5.5). Chapter 89 covers caching.
  • Subscriptions versus API. In the Claude apps you pay a monthly plan with usage limits rather than per token; which models you can pick depends on the plan. Chapter 42 covers plans.
Real-world example (illustrative numbers)

A team triages 20,000 tickets a month. Each call sends about 1,500 tokens and receives about 200. That's 30 million input tokens and 4 million output tokens. At list prices: Haiku 5.5 about $5, Sonnet 5.5 about $100, Opus 5.5 about $200, Fable 5.1 about $500 a month. If Haiku labels tickets accurately enough (which you must test), the top tier would cost 100 times more for no benefit. The lab below lets you rerun this with your own numbers.

31.7A simple way to choose

Choosing a model is covered properly in Chapter 35. For now, this flow captures the idea:

1. Define the jobInput, output, volume, how fast, how bad is a mistake
2. Pick the kindGeneral chat, embedding, image, speech?
3. Start mid-to-highProve the task works at all (Anthropic suggests Opus 5.5)
4. Try cheaperTest the same cases on a smaller tier or lower effort
5. Pin and recordPin the model ID; note the retirement date; rerun tests on change
Opinion · widely held among AI engineering practitioners

Many practitioners argue that most production systems end up using two or more tiers: a cheap model for routing, classification and extraction, and a capable model only for the steps that need it. Anthropic's own positioning of Haiku for "classification, extraction, routing" points the same way. Whether that suits your team depends on your volumes and how much a wrong answer costs.

# cost.py - compare monthly cost of one job across Claude tiers
# Prices: USD per million tokens (MTok), Claude API list prices, verified 9 Nov 2026.
PRICES = {
    "claude-haiku-5-5":  (0.10, 0.50),   # prompts up to 100K tokens
    "claude-sonnet-5-5": (2.00, 10.00),
    "claude-opus-5-5":   (4.00, 20.00),
    "claude-fable-5-1":  (10.00, 50.00),
}

# Illustrative workload: edit these numbers for your own job
calls_per_month = 20_000      # e.g. Jira tickets triaged
input_tokens    = 1_500       # prompt + ticket text, per call
output_tokens   = 200         # the model's answer, per call
use_batch       = False       # Batch API is 50% off

total_in  = calls_per_month * input_tokens / 1_000_000
total_out = calls_per_month * output_tokens / 1_000_000
print(f"Workload: {calls_per_month:,} calls, {total_in:.1f} MTok in, {total_out:.1f} MTok out")
print(f"{'model':<20}{'cost/month':>12}")
for model, (p_in, p_out) in PRICES.items():
    cost = total_in * p_in + total_out * p_out
    if use_batch:
        cost *= 0.5
    print(f"{model:<20}{'$' + format(cost, ',.2f'):>12}")

Common misunderstandings
"The most expensive model is always the right choice."
For simple, high-volume work a small model is often just as accurate and many times cheaper and faster. Test before you assume.
"A newer version is a drop-in replacement."
Behaviour, output format, token counts and refusals can all change. Treat a model switch like a library upgrade and rerun your checks.
"Reasoning models are a different product I have to switch to."
Current Claude models think adaptively; you adjust effort rather than change model. Some other vendors still split them, so check each one.
"Claude can make embeddings for my search system."
Anthropic doesn't offer an embedding model. You pair Claude with an embedding provider such as Voyage AI.
"Bigger parameter count means a better model."
Only roughly. Distillation, Mixture of Experts and training quality mean smaller or sparser models can beat older, larger ones.

31.8Review

Key takeaways
  • Every model trades capability against speed and cost, so companies sell families of models.
  • Tiers are sizes: Claude's current line runs Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1, with a 100-fold spread in output price.
  • Thinking time is a second dial; current Claude models think adaptively and you set an effort level.
  • Specialised models (embeddings, image, speech, coding-tuned) do narrow jobs; Anthropic has no embedding model of its own.
  • Pin model IDs, watch deprecation dates (Claude Sonnet 4.5 retires 30 Nov 2026) and retest when you switch.
  • Batch processing (50% off) and prompt caching can change the cost picture as much as the tier.
Check yourself

Why do AI companies offer several model tiers instead of one?

A bigger model is more capable but slower and pricier; a smaller one is fast and cheap. Tiers let you match the model to the job.

Your team needs to label 50,000 short support tickets overnight. Which choice is most sensible to try first?

Classification at high volume is what small tiers are for, and batch processing halves the cost when you can wait. Testing a sample tells you whether accuracy is good enough.

What does it mean when a model is "deprecated"?

Deprecated means "still running, end date announced". Retired means it no longer answers.

You want semantic search over 10,000 old bug reports and Claude to summarise the matches. What do you need?

Anthropic's docs state it does not offer its own embedding model; a separate embedding provider handles the similarity search.

In a Mixture of Experts model, what are "active parameters"?

A router picks a few experts per token, so only part of the model runs each time. That keeps cost per token down while total size stays large.
