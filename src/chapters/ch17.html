Pre-training is the long, expensive first stage of building a language model. The model reads a vast amount of text and, at every position, tries to predict the next token. Each time it's wrong, its billions of weights are nudged slightly so it would do better next time. Repeated trillions of times, this one simple task forces the model to absorb grammar, facts, coding patterns and styles of reasoning. The result is a base model: brilliant at continuing text, but not yet a helpful assistant. That comes in post-training (Chapter 18).

17.1One task: predict the next token

Every large language model you have used, Claude included, starts life the same way. Show it some text, hide what comes next, and ask it to guess. That's the whole pre-training task, called next-token prediction.

In everyday life

Imagine learning a language only by reading millions of books with the next word always covered by your thumb. You guess, lift your thumb, see the real word, and adjust your instincts. At first your guesses are random. After a lifetime of this you'd know grammar, the plots of famous stories, how recipes are written and what usually happens in a bug report, without anyone ever teaching you a rule.

The beauty of this task is that the text labels itself. No one has to annotate anything: the "right answer" is simply the next token in the document. Learning from labels that come from the data itself is called self-supervised learning (Chapter 10), and it is why models can learn from trillions of tokens rather than the thousands of examples a human team could label.

One sentence gives many training examples at once. From "the login test failed on chrome" the model learns:

The model seesIt must predict
thelogin
the logintest
the login testfailed
the login test failedon
the login test failed onchrome

Thanks to the causal mask from Chapter 16, all five predictions are computed in a single pass: each position can only see the tokens before it, so none of them can cheat. A training document of 4,000 tokens gives about 4,000 predictions in one go.

17.2Measuring wrongness: the loss

To improve, the model needs a number saying how wrong it was. The standard measure is cross-entropy loss: look at the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next, and take minus its natural logarithm.

Probability the model gave the real next tokenLoss (−ln p)In plain words
0.990.01Almost certain and right: tiny penalty
0.600.51Fairly confident and right: small penalty
0.053.00Thought it unlikely: big penalty
0.00000113.8Ruled it out: huge penalty

Training tries to make the average loss over all positions as low as possible. Notice the shape: being confidently wrong is punished far more than being unsure. So the model learns to spread probability sensibly when the future is genuinely uncertain ("the test… failed / passed / timed out") and to be sharp when it isn't ("the capital of France is…").

Under the hood: perplexity

Researchers often report perplexity, which is e raised to the average loss. A perplexity of 10 means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing fairly between 10 tokens. Lower is better. Perplexity is only comparable between models that use the same tokenizer and the same test text, because different tokenizers cut text into different pieces (Chapter 14).

17.3One training step, repeated millions of times

Pre-training is gradient descent (Chapter 12) on a gigantic scale. Each step looks like this:

1 BatchTake a batch of text sequences, often millions of tokens in total
2 Forward passRun them through the Transformer; get next-token probabilities at every position
3 LossAverage −ln p of the real next tokens
4 BackpropagationWork out how each weight should move to lower the loss
5 UpdateNudge every weight a tiny amount; repeat

A frontier model repeats this for weeks or months on thousands of chips working together. During the run, engineers watch the loss curve fall, save regular snapshots of the weights (checkpoints) in case hardware fails, and test the snapshots on held-out text and on benchmarks (Chapter 24). Usually the model sees each piece of data only about once, or a small number of times, which reduces pure memorisation.

Real-world example

Think of it as the biggest regression run you can imagine. The "tests" are trillions of next-token predictions, the "pass rate" is the loss, and every failing test slightly rewrites the code (the weights). Like a long CI pipeline, the expensive part is running it, and a bad configuration discovered late wastes a lot of money, so teams run small trial versions first.

17.4The data: what goes in

A model can only learn patterns that are in its data, so the training set (the dataset) is one of the most closely guarded parts of any model. Labs rarely publish full details. The typical ingredients:

SourceWhat it adds
Public web pages (often from crawls such as the non-profit Common Crawl, or the lab's own crawler)Breadth: everyday language, news, forums, how-to pages
Code (for example public repositories)Programming languages, and the habit of precise, step-by-step structure
Books and long-form writingLong, coherent text and narrative
Scientific papers, reference works such as WikipediaDense facts and technical reasoning
Many languagesMultilingual ability
Licensed and synthetic dataSpecific domains; data generated by other models to fill gaps (synthetic data)

Raw web text is mostly junk, so a large part of the work is cleaning: removing duplicates, spam, boilerplate and low-quality pages, filtering harmful content, trying to strip personal information, and respecting sites that opt out of crawling. The final proportions of each source, the data mixture, are a key design decision. One of the few published examples is GPT-3:

GPT-3 training mixture (share of training examples drawn from each source)

Contamination

If benchmark questions or your own test set leaked onto the web before the crawl, the model may have seen the answers. That inflates scores without improving skill. It is one reason to build private evaluations from your own data (Chapter 36, Chapter 125).

Opinion · public debate

Whether training on copyrighted or personal data without explicit permission is fair is contested by authors, artists, publishers, privacy advocates and AI companies, and is being tested in courts in several countries. Chapter 13 covers the arguments on each side. Treat strong claims either way as positions, not settled law.

17.5Scale: parameters, tokens and compute

Three numbers describe the size of a pre-training run:

Parameters (N)

How many adjustable weights the model has: its capacity to store patterns. GPT-3 had 175 billion.

Training tokens (D)

How much text it reads. GPT-3 read about 300 billion tokens.

The third is compute: the total arithmetic done, counted in FLOPs (floating-point operations, single multiplications or additions). A widely used rule of thumb for Transformers is:

Training compute, rule of thumb
compute ≈ 6 × N × D   # about 2 for the forward pass, 4 for the backward pass, per parameter per token

GPT-3: 6 × 175,000,000,000 × 300,000,000,000 ≈ 3.15 × 1023 FLOPs

The GPT-3 paper reports 3.14 × 1023, so the rule works well. To picture it: a laptop doing a trillion operations a second would need around ten thousand years.

Scaling laws

In 2020, researchers at OpenAI (Kaplan et al.) showed that loss falls smoothly and predictably as you increase parameters, data and compute together, following simple curves called scaling laws. Labs use them to predict how a giant model will do from small trial runs. In 2022 DeepMind's "Chinchilla" paper (Hoffmann et al.) showed many models were too big for the data they were given: for a fixed compute budget, it's better to use roughly 20 training tokens per parameter. Their 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, beat their own 280-billion-parameter Gopher.

Training tokens, as published by each developer
GPT-3 (2020)
Chinchilla (2022)1.4T
Llama 2 (2023)2T
Llama 3 (2024)15T+
GPT-3: 0.3T. Sources: Brown et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022; Touvron et al. 2023; Meta, April 2024. Anthropic does not publish these figures for Claude.

The trend since then has been to train well past the Chinchilla ratio (Llama 3's 8-billion-parameter model read over 15 trillion tokens). The reason is practical: a smaller model trained longer costs more to train but less to run for millions of users, and running costs (Chapter 25) eventually dominate.

Under the hood: why a smooth loss curve still surprises people

Scaling laws predict the average loss well. They don't predict when a particular skill appears, such as multi-digit arithmetic or following a tricky instruction. Some skills seem to switch on suddenly as models grow, which led to talk of "emergent abilities" (Wei et al., 2022). Later work (Schaeffer et al., 2023) argued that some of that suddenness comes from how skills are scored: pass/fail metrics jump even when the underlying ability improves gradually. Both papers are worth reading as an example of how measurement choices shape conclusions, a lesson any tester will recognise.

17.6What the model learns

Nobody programs in grammar or geography. They appear because they reduce the loss. To predict text well across the whole internet, a model is pushed to pick up:

What it learnsBecause it helps predict…
Spelling, grammar, styleEvery sentence it reads
Facts about the world"The capital of Japan is …", "HTTP 404 means …"
Code and formats (JSON, SQL, Gherkin)The next line of a program or a feature file
Patterns of reasoningThe next step of a worked solution or a proof
Learning from examples in the promptDocuments where a pattern repeats ("Input… Output… Input… Output…")

The last row surprised researchers. GPT-3 showed that a large pre-trained model can do a new task just from a few examples in the prompt, without any change to its weights: "in-context learning". It is why few-shot prompting works (Chapter 44).

It learns the bad too

The model also learns whatever is common in its data: stereotypes, popular misconceptions, outdated advice, insecure code patterns. Pre-training rewards plausible continuations, not true ones. That gap is the root of hallucinations (Chapter 22) and one big reason for post-training.

17.7What it costs, roughly

Pre-training cost is mostly chips × time × electricity, plus the people and the failed experiments along the way. A worked estimate, with illustrative numbers:

Back-of-envelope cost of a GPT-3-sized run (illustrative)
compute needed            3.15 × 10^23 FLOPs
useful speed per chip     3 × 10^14 FLOPs per second   # illustrative modern accelerator
chip-seconds              3.15e23 / 3e14 ≈ 1.05 × 10^9 seconds
chip-hours                ≈ 290,000
at $2 per chip-hour       ≈ $580,000                     # illustrative price

That is for a 2020-sized model on today's hardware. Frontier runs in 2026 use vastly more compute, and companies rarely publish what they spend. Independent estimates for the largest runs reach hundreds of millions of US dollars, but treat any specific figure as an estimate, not data. The point to remember: pre-training is so expensive that it happens rarely, and everything after it (post-training, prompting, tools) is comparatively cheap.

17.8From probabilities to text: sampling and temperature

Training produces a model that outputs probabilities. Using it to write text (inference) requires choosing a token at each step. This choice is called decoding, and there are several strategies:

StrategyHow it choosesEffect
Greedy decodingAlways the single most likely tokenPredictable, but tends to get repetitive and dull in long text
SamplingDraw at random, weighted by the probabilitiesVaried and natural; two runs can differ
TemperatureDivide the logits by T before softmaxT below 1 sharpens towards the top choice; above 1 flattens towards randomness
Top-kSample only from the k most likely tokensCuts off the long tail of odd choices
Top-p (nucleus)Sample from the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to pLike top-k, but the cut-off adapts to how confident the model is

What changed in Claude's API

Many tutorials tell you to set temperature, top_p or top_k. Anthropic's API version 2026-10-21 removed all three: sending them now returns a 400 error (Chapter 86). You steer Claude's output with instructions, examples and structured outputs instead. Other providers' APIs may still offer these settings; check their current documentation.

Real-world example

A QA team generating test data from the same prompt every night sees slightly different wording each run. That's sampling at work, not a bug in their pipeline. If they need stable outputs to compare, they should check structure and meaning (schema checks, rubric grading) rather than exact strings, and run each eval more than once (Chapter 125).

17.9Base models and chat models

The output of pre-training is a base model. It continues text. It doesn't follow instructions in the way you expect, because on the internet an instruction is often followed by more instructions, not by an answer.

Base model

Prompt: "Write three test cases for the password reset page."

Plausible continuation: "Write three test cases for the login page. Write three test cases for the signup page. …", as if continuing a list of homework exercises.

Chat (post-trained) model

Same prompt. Answer: three numbered test cases with steps and expected results, in a helpful tone, perhaps asking whether you want negative cases too.

The knowledge in both is largely the same; post-training changes the behaviour: following instructions, taking turns in a conversation, refusing harmful requests, admitting uncertainty. Some open-weight families publish both versions (for example Meta's Llama "base" and "instruct" models). Anthropic offers Claude to the public only as post-trained models. Chapter 18 explains how the transformation is done.

17.10The knowledge cutoff

Pre-training data is collected up to some date. After that, the model has seen nothing. That date is its knowledge cutoff. Anthropic lists two dates per model: a training data cutoff (how recent the data goes) and a reliable knowledge cutoff (up to when its knowledge is most complete and reliable; the last months before a cutoff are thinly covered, because the web hasn't finished writing about them yet).

Current Claude modelsReliable knowledge cutoffTraining data cutoff
Fable 5.1, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5June 2026June 2026

What this means for QA work:

  • A library version, API change or CVE released after the cutoff is unknown to the model unless you provide it: paste the changelog, or use a tool such as web search or documentation via MCP.
  • Near the cutoff, knowledge is patchy. Ask the model to state its uncertainty, and verify version-specific claims.
  • The model doesn't know today's date unless the app tells it. Claude Code puts the current date into its context, and chat apps generally do the same; a raw API call only knows the date if you include it.

  1. Save this as tiny_lm.py. It is a "bigram" model: it only looks at the previous token, and "training" is just counting. Real models learn far richer patterns with a Transformer, but the objective, the loss and the sampling are the same ideas.
    tiny_lm.py
    # tiny_lm.py - a toy "pre-trained" language model: count, measure loss, generate
import math
import random
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

corpus = """
the login test failed on chrome .
the login test passed on firefox .
the checkout test failed on safari .
the checkout test timed out on chrome .
the search test passed on chrome .
the login page failed to load .
the checkout page timed out .
""".split()

# 1. "Pre-training": count which token follows which (a bigram model)
follows = defaultdict(Counter)
for prev, nxt in zip(corpus, corpus[1:]):
    follows[prev][nxt] += 1

def probs(prev):
    counts = follows[prev]
    total = sum(counts.values())
    return {tok: c / total for tok, c in counts.items()}

print("After 'test' the model predicts:")
for tok, p in sorted(probs("test").items(), key=lambda x: -x[1]):
    print(f"  {tok:8} {p:.2f}")

# 2. Cross-entropy loss: how surprised is the model by real text?
def loss(text):
    toks = text.split()
    losses = [-math.log(probs(a).get(b, 1e-6)) for a, b in zip(toks, toks[1:])]
    return sum(losses) / len(losses)

print()
print(f"loss on 'the login test failed on chrome .'  = {loss('the login test failed on chrome .'):.2f}")
print(f"loss on 'the chrome test login on failed .'  = {loss('the chrome test login on failed .'):.2f}")

# 3. Temperature reshapes the probabilities before a token is drawn
def with_temperature(prev, temperature):
    p = probs(prev)
    w = {t: math.exp(math.log(q) / temperature) for t, q in p.items()}
    total = sum(w.values())
    return {t: v / total for t, v in w.items()}

print()
print("After 'on', at different temperatures:")
for t in (0.2, 1.0, 3.0):
    shown = ", ".join(f"{tok} {q:.2f}" for tok, q in with_temperature("on", t).items())
    print(f"  T={t}: {shown}")

# 4. Generate whole sentences by sampling one token at a time
def generate(temperature, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    out = ["the"]
    while out[-1] != "." and len(out) < 12:
        p = with_temperature(out[-1], temperature)
        out.append(rng.choices(list(p), weights=list(p.values()))[0])
    return " ".join(out)

print()
for t in (0.2, 3.0):
    print(f"Sentences at temperature {t}:")
    for seed in (1, 2, 3, 4):
        print("  ", generate(t, seed))

Common misunderstandings
"The model stores the internet and looks things up."
It stores patterns in its weights, compressed far below the size of its training data. It can reproduce some text it saw many times, but it doesn't keep a searchable copy.
"The model learns from my chats as I use it."
Using a model doesn't change its weights. Whether your conversations are used to train future models depends on the provider's terms and your settings (Chapter 41).
"Lower temperature means more accurate."
It means more predictable. A confident wrong answer stays wrong at any temperature.
"Bigger is always better."
For a fixed budget, more data for a smaller model can beat a bigger model (Chinchilla). And bigger models cost more to run.

17.11Review

Key takeaways
  • Pre-training = predict the next token across trillions of tokens; the text labels itself (self-supervised).
  • Cross-entropy loss (−ln p of the real next token) punishes confident mistakes hardest.
  • Data is mixed from web, code, books, papers and more, then heavily filtered; mixtures are rarely published.
  • Scale = parameters (N), tokens (D) and compute (≈ 6ND FLOPs); scaling laws make results predictable; Chinchilla suggested about 20 tokens per parameter.
  • Decoding turns probabilities into text: greedy, sampling, temperature, top-k, top-p. Anthropic's API no longer accepts temperature, top_p or top_k (version 2026-10-21).
  • A base model continues text; post-training makes it an assistant. Current Claude models have a reliable knowledge cutoff of June 2026.
Check yourself

Why is pre-training called self-supervised?

Every position in a document provides its own label: the token that actually came next.

The model gave the real next token a probability of 0.05. What is the cross-entropy loss for that position?

Loss = −ln(0.05) ≈ 3.0. A probability of 0.6 would give about 0.51.

Using compute ≈ 6 × N × D, roughly how many FLOPs does a 10-billion-parameter model trained on 200 billion tokens need?

6 × 1010 × 2 × 1011 = 12 × 1021 = 1.2 × 1022.

What does raising the temperature above 1 do?

Dividing the logits by a larger T shrinks the gaps between them before softmax.

Your team asks Claude about a framework release from last month (November 2026). What is the most likely problem?

Weights don't update as the world changes. Give the model the facts it can't have seen.

Teach it

"Pre-training is where a language model learns almost everything it knows. It reads trillions of tokens of text and keeps guessing the next one. Each wrong guess nudges its weights. To guess well across the whole internet it has to pick up grammar, facts, code and reasoning patterns, but also the internet's mistakes. It's hugely expensive, so it happens rarely, and the knowledge stops at a cutoff date. The result can continue any text, but it isn't a helpful assistant until post-training. When it writes, it picks each next token from a set of probabilities, which is why answers vary a little each run."
