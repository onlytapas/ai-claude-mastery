Before you start: this is an expert, optional chapter. You should know how a network learns (Chapter 12), what post-training and supervised fine-tuning are (Chapter 18), RAG (Chapter 23) and evals (Chapter 125). Most readers will never need to fine-tune a model; reading 135.1 and 135.2 is enough to make that call. The lab needs only Python 3.

Fine-tuning means training an existing model a little more on your own examples, so its weights change and it behaves differently without being told in every prompt. It's good at teaching a consistent style, format or narrow skill, especially to a smaller, cheaper model. It's bad at teaching facts that change, and it brings ongoing costs: data, evaluation, hosting and retraining when the base model is retired. Today's strong models, good prompts and retrieval solve most problems first. And for Claude specifically, fine-tuning options are very limited, so check carefully before you plan around them.

136.1What fine-tuning is, and isn't

In Chapter 18 you saw that every chat model is already fine-tuned: a pre-trained base model is post-trained on examples of good conversations. Fine-tuning for your own use is the same idea, at a much smaller scale. You give the model hundreds or thousands of input-and-desired-output examples, and a training job nudges its weights so that it produces outputs like yours.

In everyday life

Prompting is like giving a skilled contractor detailed written instructions each morning. RAG is like giving them the right reference binder for each job. Fine-tuning is like sending them on a training course: after it, they do things your way without being told, but the course costs money, takes time, and they'll need another course if your way changes, or if you hire a new contractor.

Fine-tuning is good forFine-tuning is poor for
A consistent output format or house style across millions of callsTeaching facts, especially ones that change (use RAG)
A narrow, repetitive task, such as classifying bug reports into your categoriesTasks where you have only a few dozen examples
Making a small model good enough at one task, to cut cost and latencyMaking a model "smarter" in general
Replacing a very long prompt full of examples that you send on every requestAnything you could fix by writing clearer instructions
Teaching tone or preferences that are hard to describe but easy to demonstrateGuaranteeing safety: fine-tuning can weaken a model's safety behaviour

136.2Try these first: the customisation ladder

Climb this ladder one rung at a time, and only go up when you have measured that the rung below isn't enough. Each rung up costs more and is harder to change.

1. Better promptClear instructions, format, constraints (Ch 44)
2. Few-shot examplesShow 3–20 examples in the prompt (Ch 45)
3. Retrieval (RAG)Supply the right documents per request (Ch 92)
4. Tools and structured outputLet code do exact work; force a schema (Ch 89)
5. Model choice and routingA different tier or model for this task (Ch 35)
6. Fine-tuningOnly with data, an eval set and a measured gap

Two modern developments make fine-tuning less necessary than it used to be. Context windows are large enough to hold many examples, and prompt caching (Chapter 89) makes sending the same long set of examples on every request far cheaper. Together they cover much of what people once fine-tuned for.

Real-world example

A QA team wanted to auto-classify incoming bug reports into five components. Their first thought was fine-tuning. Instead they wrote a clear prompt with the five definitions and 15 cached examples, and measured it on 300 already-labelled historical bugs. Accuracy was close to what two humans agreed on with each other, so there was nothing left for fine-tuning to win. (Illustrative scenario.) If they later need to classify millions of reports a day at minimum cost, fine-tuning a small open-weight model becomes worth evaluating, and the 300 labelled bugs are already their test set.

Opinion · widely held among AI engineers

Many practitioners advise treating fine-tuning as an optimisation for a working system, not a way to make a system work. Get it working with prompting and retrieval on a large model first; then, if cost or latency matters, consider fine-tuning a smaller model to match it. Some teams with very specialised data or strict latency needs disagree and fine-tune earlier. Both positions exist; the deciding factor should be your measured results.

136.3The methods

There are several ways to fine-tune. They differ in what training signal you provide and how much of the model changes.

MethodYou providePlain idea
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)Prompts with the ideal response"Answer like this." The most common method.
Preference tuning, e.g. DPOA prompt with a better and a worse response"Prefer this one over that one." Good for tone and quality where there's no single right answer.
Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT)Prompts plus a grader or reward function that scores answers"Try, get scored, improve." Fits tasks where answers can be checked automatically.
DistillationPrompts; a large "teacher" model writes the answersA small "student" model learns to imitate the big one on your task.
Continued pre-trainingLots of unlabelled domain textMore next-token training on, say, your industry's documents. Rarely offered or needed today.

Full fine-tuning versus parameter-efficient fine-tuning

Full fine-tuning updates every weight in the model. For a model with billions of parameters that needs a lot of GPU memory, and each fine-tuned version is a complete copy of the model to store and serve.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) freezes the original weights and trains a small number of new ones. The most popular PEFT method is LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation, Hu et al., 2021). Instead of changing a big weight matrix directly, LoRA learns a small "correction" made of two thin matrices, which is added on top. The result is a small adapter file. You can keep one base model in memory and swap adapters for different tasks.

Under the hood: why LoRA is so small

A layer in a Transformer (Chapter 16) multiplies its input by a weight matrix W, say 4,096 × 4,096: about 16.8 million numbers. Full fine-tuning learns a change ΔW of the same size. LoRA's insight is that the useful change for one task usually has a simple structure, so it can be approximated as the product of two thin matrices: ΔW ≈ A × B, where A is 4,096 × r and B is r × 4,096. The rank r is small, often 4 to 64. With r = 8, A and B together hold 2 × 4,096 × 8 = 65,536 numbers: about 0.4% of the full matrix. The lab's lora_params.py computes this for several ranks.

QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) goes further: it stores the frozen base model in 4-bit quantised form while training LoRA adapters on top, so fairly large open-weight models can be fine-tuned on a single GPU. On the open-source side, Hugging Face's peft and trl libraries are common starting points (Chapter 137 covers running models locally).

The settings you'll meet

Training jobs expose a few hyperparameters: the number of epochs (passes through the data), the learning rate (often as a multiplier) and the batch size. Start with the platform's defaults. More epochs or a higher learning rate make the model fit your data more tightly, which risks overfitting and catastrophic forgetting: getting better at your task while getting worse at everything else.

136.4Preparing the data

Data quality decides the result far more than any setting. A model fine-tuned on inconsistent examples learns to be inconsistent. Most platforms take JSONL: one JSON object per line, each a short conversation ending with the response you want.

train.jsonl — one example per line (shown wrapped here)
{"messages": [
  {"role": "system", "content": "Classify the bug report into one of: UI, API, Performance, Data, Security. Reply with the label only."},
  {"role": "user", "content": "POST /orders returns 500 when the body is empty"},
  {"role": "assistant", "content": "API"}
]}

The exact field names differ by platform, so always follow the provider's format page. The checklist doesn't change:

  • Consistent labels. If two experts would label an example differently, fix the guideline first. Measure how often humans agree; that's roughly the ceiling for the model.
  • Representative. Real inputs, in their real messiness: typos, long and short, all categories, edge cases.
  • Balanced enough. A category with five examples will be learned poorly.
  • Clean. Remove duplicates, broken rows and examples where the "ideal" answer is actually wrong.
  • Safe. Remove personal data, secrets and anything under restriction (Chapter 132). A model can memorise and repeat training data.
  • Split before you start. Separate training, validation and test sets, and never let test examples leak into training. Keep the test set locked away until the end.

136.5Evaluating honestly

A fine-tuned model is a new model. Evaluate it like one, with the methods from Chapter 125 and Chapter 36.

BaselineBest prompt-only result on the test set
TrainWatch training and validation loss
CompareSame test set, same grader, several runs
Check side effectsGeneral ability, safety, refusals
DecideIs the gain worth the ongoing cost?
  • Compare against the best prompt, not a lazy one. A fine-tuned model beating a one-line prompt proves little.
  • Watch both loss curves. If training loss keeps falling while validation loss rises, the model is memorising rather than learning: stop earlier or use fewer epochs.
  • Test beyond the task. Run a small general eval and your safety and bias checks (Chapter 135). Researchers have shown that fine-tuning, even on harmless-looking data, can weaken a model's safety behaviour (Qi et al., 2023).
  • Count total cost per correct answer, including hosting, not only accuracy.

136.7The real costs

The training run is often the smallest cost. A fuller picture:

CostWhat drives it
DataCollecting, cleaning, labelling and reviewing hundreds or thousands of examples: usually the biggest cost, in people's time
TrainingTraining tokens × epochs, per run; you'll rarely get it right first time, so plan for several runs
EvaluationBuilding and running the test set, plus general and safety checks, after every run
HostingSome platforms charge for storing the custom model and for dedicated capacity to serve it, whether or not you use it
MaintenanceRetraining when your categories, style or data change, and when the base model is retired
OpportunityWhile you maintain a custom model, new general models keep improving; a prompt can move to a new model in an afternoon

A rough sizing formula for the training run: examples × average tokens per example × epochs = training tokens. For instance, 2,000 examples × 500 tokens × 3 epochs = 3 million training tokens (illustrative numbers). Multiply by the platform's current per-token training price; prices vary by model and change often, so check the provider's pricing page.

Lab: prepare and check a fine-tuning dataset30 min · your computer, Python 3, no cloud account
  1. This lab does the part of fine-tuning that matters most and costs nothing: preparing the data. Make a folder (mkdir ft-lab && cd ft-lab) and save this as prep_data.py:
    prep_data.py
    """prep_data.py - check and split a chat-format fine-tuning dataset (JSONL), offline.

  python3 prep_data.py make-sample     writes sample.jsonl (illustrative, with planted problems)
  python3 prep_data.py check FILE      validates, removes bad rows, splits train/validation/test
"""
import json
import random
import re
import sys
from collections import Counter

LABELS = ["UI", "API", "Performance", "Data", "Security"]
EMAIL = re.compile(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.]+")
CARD = re.compile(r"\b(?:\d[ -]?){13,16}\b")

def example(bug, label):
    return {"messages": [
        {"role": "system", "content": "Classify the bug report into one of: " + ", ".join(LABELS) + ". Reply with the label only."},
        {"role": "user", "content": bug},
        {"role": "assistant", "content": label},
    ]}

def make_sample():
    rows = []
    templates = {
        "UI": ["Button '{x}' overlaps the footer on small screens", "The {x} dropdown text is cut off in Firefox"],
        "API": ["POST /{x} returns 500 when the body is empty", "GET /{x} ignores the page parameter"],
        "Performance": ["The {x} page takes 9 seconds to load", "Search for {x} times out under load"],
        "Data": ["Totals on the {x} report don't match the database", "Duplicate {x} records after import"],
        "Security": ["User can view another user's {x} by changing the ID", "Session for {x} doesn't expire after logout"],
    }
    words = ["orders", "invoices", "profile", "basket", "reports", "settings", "users", "payments"]
    for label, temps in templates.items():
        for t in temps:
            for w in words:
                rows.append(example(t.format(x=w), label))
    rows.append(rows[0])                                                   # planted: duplicate
    rows.append(example("Email jane.doe@example.com about the refund page bug", "UI"))  # planted: personal data
    rows.append(example("Login page is slow", "Slow"))                     # planted: unknown label
    rows.append({"messages": [{"role": "user", "content": "No answer here"}]})         # planted: no assistant turn
    with open("sample.jsonl", "w", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        for r in rows:
            f.write(json.dumps(r) + "\n")
    print(f"Wrote sample.jsonl with {len(rows)} rows (illustrative data, 4 planted problems).")

def problems_in(row):
    msgs = row.get("messages")
    if not isinstance(msgs, list) or not msgs:
        return ["no messages list"]
    if msgs[-1].get("role") != "assistant":
        return ["last message is not from the assistant"]
    issues = []
    text = " ".join(m.get("content", "") for m in msgs)
    if EMAIL.search(text) or CARD.search(text):
        issues.append("possible personal data")
    if msgs[-1]["content"] not in LABELS:
        issues.append(f"unknown label '{msgs[-1]['content']}'")
    return issues

def check(path):
    good, seen, dropped = [], set(), Counter()
    with open(path, encoding="utf-8") as f:
        for n, line in enumerate(f, 1):
            try:
                row = json.loads(line)
            except json.JSONDecodeError:
                dropped["invalid JSON"] += 1
                print(f"  line {n}: invalid JSON")
                continue
            key = json.dumps(row, sort_keys=True)
            if key in seen:
                dropped["duplicate"] += 1
                print(f"  line {n}: duplicate")
                continue
            seen.add(key)
            issues = problems_in(row)
            if issues:
                for i in issues:
                    dropped[i.split(" '")[0]] += 1
                print(f"  line {n}: {'; '.join(issues)}")
                continue
            good.append(row)
    print(f"\nKept {len(good)} rows, dropped {sum(dropped.values())}: {dict(dropped)}")
    print("Label balance:", dict(Counter(r["messages"][-1]["content"] for r in good)))

    random.seed(42)                       # fixed seed: the same split every run
    by_label = {}
    for r in good:
        by_label.setdefault(r["messages"][-1]["content"], []).append(r)
    splits = {"train": [], "validation": [], "test": []}
    for rows in by_label.values():        # stratified: each label split 70/15/15
        random.shuffle(rows)
        a, b = int(len(rows) * 0.7), int(len(rows) * 0.85)
        splits["train"] += rows[:a]
        splits["validation"] += rows[a:b]
        splits["test"] += rows[b:]
    for name, rows in splits.items():
        with open(f"{name}.jsonl", "w", encoding="utf-8") as f:
            for r in rows:
                f.write(json.dumps(r) + "\n")
        print(f"  {name}.jsonl: {len(rows)} rows")

    chars = sum(len(m["content"]) for r in splits["train"] for m in r["messages"])
    tokens = chars / 4                    # rough rule of thumb for English: about 4 characters per token
    print(f"\nRough training size: ~{tokens:,.0f} tokens per epoch, ~{tokens * 3:,.0f} for 3 epochs (estimate only)")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    if len(sys.argv) >= 2 and sys.argv[1] == "make-sample":
        make_sample()
    elif len(sys.argv) == 3 and sys.argv[1] == "check":
        check(sys.argv[2])
    else:
        print(__doc__)
    The file is saved. It uses only Python's standard library.

  5. See why LoRA is cheap. Save this as lora_params.py and run python3 lora_params.py:
    lora_params.py
    """lora_params.py - how many parameters does LoRA train, compared with full fine-tuning?"""
d_in, d_out = 4096, 4096          # one weight matrix in a mid-sized model (illustrative)
full = d_in * d_out
print(f"Full fine-tuning of one {d_in}x{d_out} matrix: {full:,} parameters")
for r in (4, 8, 16, 64):
    lora = r * (d_in + d_out)     # A is d_in x r, B is r x d_out
    print(f"LoRA rank {r:>2}: {lora:>9,} parameters ({lora / full:.2%} of full)")

Common misunderstandings
"Fine-tuning is how you teach the model about our product."
Knowledge that changes belongs in retrieval (RAG). Fine-tuning teaches behaviour, format and narrow skills, and facts learned this way go stale.
"We can fine-tune the latest Claude model."
As of 9 November 2026, no self-service fine-tuning for current Claude models is documented by Anthropic or the major clouds. Bedrock lists only Claude 3 Haiku.
"More epochs means a better model."
More epochs often means overfitting and forgetting. Watch validation loss and test on held-out data.
"Once it's trained, we're done."
Custom models need re-evaluation and retraining when data changes or the base model retires, plus hosting costs in the meantime.
"Fine-tuning keeps the model's safety behaviour intact."
It can weaken it. Re-run safety and bias checks on every fine-tuned version.

136.8Review

Key takeaways
  • Fine-tuning changes a model's weights using your examples; it's good for style, format and narrow tasks, and poor for changing facts.
  • Climb the ladder first: prompt, few-shot, RAG, tools and structured output, model choice. Fine-tune only with data, an eval set and a measured gap.
  • LoRA and other PEFT methods train a tiny fraction of parameters as a swappable adapter; QLoRA adds quantisation to fit on smaller GPUs.
  • Data quality, clean splits and honest comparison against the best prompt decide whether fine-tuning was worth it.
  • As of 9 November 2026: no self-service Claude fine-tuning on the Claude API; Bedrock lists only Claude 3 Haiku; Gemini, Foundry and open-weight models offer more options; OpenAI is winding its platform down.
  • The biggest costs are data, evaluation, hosting and retraining, and every custom model is tied to a base model that will retire.
