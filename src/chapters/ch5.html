Instead of telling computers the rules, researchers started showing them examples and letting them work out the rules themselves. That is machine learning. For years it worked best with simple methods, because neural networks with many layers were hard to train and computers were slow. Around 2012 three things came together: a big labelled image collection (ImageNet), fast graphics chips (GPUs) and better training techniques. Neural networks suddenly beat everything else. That moment started the deep learning era that leads directly to Claude.

5.1From rules to examples

Traditional programming

Rules + data → answers. A person writes "IF the email contains 'winner' AND a link THEN spam."

Machine learning

Data + answers → rules. Show the computer 100,000 emails already marked spam or not, and it works out which patterns predict spam.

In everyday life

Nobody teaches a child the rules for recognising a dog. There's no checklist of ear shapes and tail lengths. Instead the child sees many dogs, hears "dog" each time, and learns to recognise new ones, even breeds they've never seen. Machine learning works the same way: examples first, patterns learned.

Real-world example: spam filters

In 2002 the programmer Paul Graham published "A Plan for Spam", describing a filter that learned word probabilities from your own spam and genuine mail. It worked far better than hand-written rules and adapted as spammers changed tactics. Learned spam filters soon became standard, and they're still how your email is filtered today, now with much more advanced models.

5.2Backpropagation: how deep networks learn (1986)

Chapter 4 ended with a problem: networks with several layers could solve harder problems, but nobody had a practical way to train them. In 1986 David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams published a paper in Nature that popularised backpropagation (the core idea had appeared earlier, including in Paul Werbos's work in 1974).

In plain words, backpropagation answers this question: the network got the answer wrong; which of its millions of internal settings should change, and by how much? It works backwards from the error, layer by layer, sharing out the "blame" so every connection can be adjusted a little. Repeat over many examples and the network gradually improves.

In everyday life

A restaurant serves a dish that a customer says is too salty. The head chef traces back: was it the sauce, the stock or the seasoning at the end? Each cook adjusts slightly in proportion to how much they contributed. After many dishes and much feedback, the kitchen gets it right without anyone writing a rule for "the right amount of salt". That tracing-back of blame is backpropagation. Chapter 12 explains it properly.

With backpropagation, multi-layer networks could learn. In 1989 Yann LeCun and colleagues at Bell Labs used it to train a network that read handwritten digits; versions of it were later used to read the numbers on bank cheques. But in the 1990s and 2000s neural networks still struggled on harder problems, and other methods often worked better.

5.3The statistical years (1990s–2000s)

During this period, machine learning became a mainstream engineering tool, mostly using methods simpler than deep neural networks: decision trees, "support vector machines" and statistical models. They powered search engines, recommendations, credit scoring and fraud detection. Several famous moments came from this era:

  1. 1997Deep Blue beats Garry Kasparov. IBM's chess computer defeated the world champion in a six-game match. It won mainly through enormous search speed and expert-tuned evaluation, not learning, which is why some said it "wasn't really AI" (the AI effect, Chapter 1).
  2. 1997LSTM, by Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber: a kind of network that can remember information across long sequences. It later powered speech recognition and translation (Chapter 6).
  3. 2006–09The Netflix Prize offered US$1 million to anyone who could improve its film recommendations by 10%. The winning team combined many learning models, showing how valuable learned predictions had become.
  4. 2011IBM Watson wins Jeopardy! on US television against two champions, combining search, language processing and statistics.

5.4ImageNet: data at scale (2009)

The Stanford professor Fei-Fei Li believed AI was limited less by clever algorithms than by a lack of data. Her team built ImageNet: millions of photos, each labelled by people (often through Amazon's Mechanical Turk crowdsourcing service) with what it showed, across thousands of categories. It was presented in 2009.

From 2010 an annual competition used a subset of ImageNet: 1.2 million training images in 1,000 categories, from "tabby cat" to "container ship". Teams' programs had to name what was in new photos. The main score was the "top-5 error": how often the right answer wasn't among the program's five best guesses.

5.52012: the AlexNet moment

In 2012 a team from the University of Toronto entered a deep neural network called AlexNet. It was named after Alex Krizhevsky, who built it with Ilya Sutskever and their supervisor Geoffrey Hinton. It achieved a top-5 error of about 15.3%. The next best entry, using traditional methods, scored about 26.2%. In a field where teams fought over fractions of a percent, it was a landslide.

Why did it work now, when neural networks had disappointed for decades? All three ingredients from Chapter 2 arrived together:

Data1.2 million labelled images from ImageNet
ComputeTrained on two NVIDIA gaming graphics cards (GTX 580s)
MethodsA deeper network plus training tricks, such as "dropout" to stop it memorising
ResultA huge leap in accuracy, and every major lab switched to deep learning

Within a few years, deep learning had taken over image recognition, speech recognition and machine translation. Tech companies hired the researchers, often by buying their start-ups: Google acquired Hinton's small company in 2013 and the London lab DeepMind in 2014.

5.6What makes learning "deep"?

"Deep" just means many layers. Each layer transforms the information a little and passes it on. In image networks, researchers found that early layers learn to detect simple things like edges and colours; middle layers combine them into textures and shapes; later layers recognise whole objects like eyes, wheels or faces.

PixelsThe raw image: millions of coloured dots
Early layersEdges, lines, colour patches
Middle layersTextures, corners, simple shapes
Later layersParts: eyes, ears, wheels
Output"Golden retriever (91%)"

Nobody programs these features. The network discovers them during training because they help it get the answers right. Language models (Part II) work similarly with text: early layers handle word fragments, later layers handle meaning and context.

5.7The landmark years (2014–2020)

  1. 2014GANs (generative adversarial networks), by Ian Goodfellow and colleagues: two networks compete, one generating images and one spotting fakes. An early form of generative AI.
  2. 2015ResNet, from Microsoft Research, trains networks over 100 layers deep and beats the human-level benchmark often quoted for ImageNet.
  3. 2016AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol 4–1 at Go. DeepMind combined deep learning with reinforcement learning and search. Go has far more possible positions than chess and had been expected to resist computers for another decade.
  4. 2017The Transformer is introduced by Google researchers, transforming language AI (Chapter 6).
  5. 2018Turing Award (often called computing's Nobel Prize) to Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton and Yann LeCun for deep learning.
  6. 2020AlphaFold 2 (DeepMind) predicts protein shapes with accuracy close to laboratory methods, solving a 50-year-old biology problem.
Recognition

In 2024, the Nobel Prize in Physics went to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for foundational work on neural networks, and half of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of Google DeepMind for AlphaFold (shared with David Baker for protein design).

Common misunderstandings
"Deep learning means the AI thinks deeply."
"Deep" refers only to the number of layers in the network.
"Deep Blue used deep learning."
No. It relied mainly on fast search and hand-tuned evaluation. The names are a coincidence.
"Machine learning removes humans from the process."
People choose the data, label it, design the model, decide what counts as success, and check the results. Bad data gives bad models (Chapter 13).
"AlexNet was a brand-new idea."
Its design built on LeCun's networks from 1989. What changed was scale: more data, GPUs and training tricks.

5.8Review

Key takeaways
  • Machine learning flips programming around: give data and answers, get the rules.
  • Backpropagation (popularised 1986) made it practical to train multi-layer networks.
  • Through the 1990s and 2000s, simpler learning methods ran search, recommendations and spam filters.
  • ImageNet (2009) provided the data; GPUs provided the compute; AlexNet (2012) proved deep learning by winning by a landslide.
  • "Deep" means many layers, each learning more complex features automatically.
  • AlphaGo (2016), the Transformer (2017) and AlphaFold (2020) showed deep learning's reach; its pioneers won the 2018 Turing Award and 2024 Nobel Prizes.
