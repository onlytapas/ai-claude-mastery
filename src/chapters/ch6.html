Before you start: Chapter 5 (machine learning and deep learning).

Getting computers to understand and produce language took seventy years. First came hand-written grammar rules, which were brittle. Then statistics: counting which words tend to follow which. Then neural networks that turned words into numbers capturing meaning, and read sentences one word at a time. In 2017 the Transformer let models look at all the words at once and decide which ones matter to each other. That design, plus huge amounts of text, produced the language models we use today.

6.1Why language is hard

Language is full of things people handle without noticing:

ChallengeExample
Ambiguity"I saw the man with the telescope." Who has the telescope?
Context"It's cold in here" can be a request to close the window
Words with many meanings"Bank" (river or money), "bug" (insect or software fault)
Long-distance links"The test that the new developer wrote last Tuesday after the review was flaky." The verb agrees with "test", far back
Sarcasm and tone"Great, another production outage."
World knowledge"The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." What was too big?

The field that tackles this is called natural language processing, or NLP.

6.2The rule-based era (1950s–1980s)

  1. 1954Georgetown–IBM experiment. A computer translated about 60 carefully chosen Russian sentences into English using a small vocabulary and a handful of rules. Newspapers predicted machine translation would be solved within a few years.
  2. 1966ALPAC report concludes machine translation isn't working; US funding collapses (Chapter 4).
  3. 1964–66ELIZA, by Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT, the first famous chatbot.
  4. 1968–70SHRDLU, by Terry Winograd, understood typed commands about a small world of coloured blocks ("Pick up the big red block") but only within that tiny world.

ELIZA and the ELIZA effect

ELIZA's best-known script, DOCTOR, imitated a certain kind of therapist. It looked for keywords and turned your words back into questions:

An ELIZA-style exchange
You:   I am worried about my release.
ELIZA: Why are you worried about your release?
You:   My manager is angry.
ELIZA: Tell me more about your manager.

It understood nothing; it matched patterns like "I am X" → "Why are you X?". Yet people, including Weizenbaum's own secretary, confided in it and felt understood. Weizenbaum was disturbed by this and spent much of his later career warning about trusting computers too much. The tendency to assume understanding behind fluent text is now called the ELIZA effect, and it's worth remembering whenever an AI sounds empathetic.

6.3The statistical turn (late 1980s–2000s)

Instead of writing grammar rules, researchers started counting. Given large amounts of text, how often does each word follow each other word? IBM researchers led by Frederick Jelinek applied this to speech recognition and translation with striking success. A remark attributed to Jelinek captures the mood: "Every time I fire a linguist, the performance of the speech recognizer goes up."

The simplest statistical language model is an n-gram model: predict the next word from the previous one or two words, based on counts.

In everyday life

Your phone keyboard suggesting the next word is a descendant of this idea. Type "Happy" and it suggests "birthday", because it has seen that pair so many times. The problem: it only looks at the last word or two, so it can't follow the meaning of a whole sentence or paragraph.

Remember Claude Shannon from Chapter 2? In 1948–51 he ran experiments in which people guessed the next letter of English text, to measure how predictable language is. Predicting what comes next turned out to be one of the most powerful ideas in AI.

6.4Words become numbers: embeddings (2003–2013)

Counting treats every word as unrelated: "cat" and "kitten" are as different as "cat" and "carburettor". Neural approaches fixed this by representing each word as a list of numbers, an embedding, learned so that words used in similar ways get similar numbers.

  • In 2003 Yoshua Bengio and colleagues published a neural language model that learned word representations while predicting the next word.
  • In 2013 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google released word2vec, which learned good word embeddings quickly from billions of words.
Real-world example: word arithmetic

word2vec embeddings had a surprising property: you could do arithmetic with meaning. Take the numbers for "king", subtract "man", add "woman", and the closest word is "queen". Similarly "Paris − France + Italy" lands near "Rome". The model was never told about royalty or capitals; it picked up these relationships from how words are used. Chapter 15 explains how.

6.5Reading in sequence: RNNs, LSTMs and seq2seq (2014)

To understand sentences, networks needed memory. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) read text one word at a time, carrying a running summary forward. The LSTM (1997, Chapter 5) improved how long that memory lasted.

In 2014 Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le at Google showed sequence-to-sequence ("seq2seq") learning: one network reads a whole sentence into a summary, and a second network writes a translation from that summary.

EncoderReads "The cat sat on the mat" one word at a time
SummaryOne fixed list of numbers meant to hold the whole meaning
DecoderWrites "Le chat était assis sur le tapis" one word at a time

The weak point is the middle box. Squeezing a whole long sentence into one fixed summary loses information, like describing a whole film in a single sentence and then asking someone to retell it scene by scene.

6.6Attention: look back at what matters (2014)

In 2014 Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio proposed attention. Instead of relying only on the single summary, the decoder can look back at all the input words each time it writes a word, focusing on whichever are most relevant right now.

In everyday life

When a human interpreter translates a long sentence, they don't memorise it and then recite. They glance back at the original, focusing on the part they're translating at that moment. Translating "assis" (sat), their attention is on "sat". That glancing back is attention.

In 2016 Google switched Google Translate to a neural system using these ideas, with a large jump in quality. But a limitation remained: the networks still read words one after another, which was slow to train and still struggled with very long texts.

6.72017: the Transformer

In June 2017 eight researchers at Google (Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Niki Parmar, Jakob Uszkoreit, Llion Jones, Aidan Gomez, Łukasz Kaiser and Illia Polosukhin) published a paper with a confident title: "Attention Is All You Need". They removed the word-by-word reading entirely. Their new design, the Transformer, uses attention for everything: every word looks at every other word in the text at the same time.

Before: reading in order

Like reading a book one word at a time through a keyhole, keeping a running memory. Slow, and early words fade.

After: the Transformer

Like spreading the whole page out on a table and drawing lines between every pair of related words at once.

This had two huge benefits:

  1. Better understanding of context. In "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big", the word "it" can directly look at "trophy" and "suitcase" and work out which one fits.
  2. Massive speed-up in training. Because all words are processed at once, the work fits perfectly onto GPUs (Chapter 2). That meant models could be trained on far more text than ever before.

That second point turned out to be decisive. The Transformer didn't just improve translation; it made it practical to train on a large share of the internet. Chapter 16 takes you through it step by step.

6.8Immediately after: BERT and GPT (2018)

Within a year, two influential families appeared, using different halves of the Transformer:

BERT (Google, 2018)GPT (OpenAI, 2018)
UsesThe "encoder" side: reads text in both directionsThe "decoder" side: predicts the next word, left to right
Good atUnderstanding: search, classification, answering from a passageGenerating: continuing and writing text
Trained byHiding words and guessing them ("fill in the blank")Guessing the next word, over and over
LegacyImproved Google Search from 2019The line that leads to ChatGPT; Claude and most chat models use the same next-word approach

GPT stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformer: generative (it writes), pre-trained (first trained on a large general text collection), Transformer (the design). The idea of training a general model first and specialising it later is called pre-training, and it's how every major language model is built. Chapter 7 tells the story of what happened when these models were scaled up.

Why "predict the next word" teaches so much

It sounds too simple to produce intelligence. But consider what you'd need to know to predict the next word well across all kinds of text. To finish "The capital of Japan is…" you need geography. To finish a line of code, you need programming. To finish "She was furious, so she…" you need some model of human behaviour. To finish a maths proof, you need to follow the logic.

A model trained to predict the next word across trillions of words is pushed to pick up grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and styles, because each of them reduces its prediction error. That's the deep reason simple next-word prediction, at enormous scale, produces surprisingly capable systems. It also explains their weaknesses: they learn what text tends to say, not what's guaranteed to be true (Chapter 22).

Common misunderstandings
"Language models store sentences and look them up."
They learn patterns and generate new text word by word. They can reproduce text they saw many times, but they aren't a database.
"The Transformer was designed to build chatbots."
It was designed for machine translation. Its suitability for large-scale text prediction was discovered as people applied it more widely.
"Sounding understanding means understanding."
That's the ELIZA effect. Fluency is evidence of something, but not proof of human-like understanding.

6.9Review

Key takeaways
  • Language is hard because of ambiguity, context, multiple meanings and world knowledge.
  • Rule-based NLP (1950s–80s) was brittle; ELIZA showed how easily people attribute understanding to fluent text.
  • Statistical methods (from the late 1980s) counted word patterns and worked better.
  • Embeddings (2003, word2vec 2013) turned words into numbers that capture meaning.
  • Seq2seq (2014) and attention (2014) improved translation; Google Translate went neural in 2016.
  • The Transformer (2017) used attention alone, processed all words at once and trained efficiently on GPUs.
  • BERT and GPT (2018) followed; GPT's next-word approach leads to ChatGPT and Claude.
