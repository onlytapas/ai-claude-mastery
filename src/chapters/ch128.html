Giving everyone an AI licence and hoping for the best rarely works. Successful teams treat AI adoption like any other change programme: check readiness, agree the rules, run a small pilot with clear goals, set Claude up consistently, train people properly, measure honestly and then scale what works. This chapter gives you a step-by-step roadmap to take a QA team from "a few people experimenting" to "AI is a normal, well-governed part of how we test", with the same team covering more ground.

128.1The roadmap at a glance

1 · Ready?Assess people, process, data and policy
2 · Sign-offSecurity, privacy and policy agreed
3 · Pilot90 days, few use-cases, clear success criteria
4 · StandardiseShared setup, training, governance
5 · ScaleMore teams, more use-cases, continuous measurement
In everyday life

Think about how a hospital introduces a new piece of equipment. It doesn't hand it to every ward on Monday. It checks the wards are ready, gets safety approval, trials it on one ward with trained staff, writes standard procedures, then rolls it out with training and monitoring. AI in a QA team deserves the same care, because it touches your product's quality and your company's data.

128.2Step 1: readiness assessment

Spend a week answering these questions honestly. Each "no" isn't a blocker, but it becomes an early task.

AreaQuestionIf the answer is no…
PolicyDoes the company have an AI usage policy that names approved tools?Start sign-off early (119.3); it takes longest
Is it clear what data may go into AI tools (customer data, credentials, source code)?Agree data rules before the pilot
ProcessAre requirements and test cases written down somewhere AI can read (Jira, Confluence, a repo)?AI works from text; tidy the key sources for the pilot area
Do you have any measurements today (time, defects, coverage)?Start a baseline now (Chapter 126)
ToolsDo testers have access to Claude (Team or Enterprise plan) and, where needed, Claude Code?Arrange licences for pilot members only
Is there a test repository with a working way to run tests locally?Fix that first; AI can't help automate what nobody can run
PeopleIs there at least one person keen to lead and learn?Find a champion (119.5) before starting
Does management support time for learning, not just "be faster"?Agree protected learning time as part of the pilot

128.3Step 2: policy and security sign-off

Security and legal teams aren't the enemy; they're protecting the company. You'll get faster approval if you bring a clear, specific proposal rather than "can we use AI?"

Bring them

  • Which tools and plans (e.g. Claude Team or Enterprise)
  • Which use-cases, for which people
  • What data will and won't be used
  • Which MCP servers you want, and with what access (Chapter 105)
  • How outputs are reviewed before use
  • How you'll measure and report

Ask them

  • What data classes are off-limits?
  • What are the plan's data-retention and training terms? (Check the current terms for your plan; don't assume.)
  • Do they require SSO, audit logs or admin controls? (These vary by plan; see Chapter 42)
  • Who approves new MCP servers and plugins?
Real-world example: a one-paragraph proposal

"We propose a 90-day pilot with six testers on the Payments squad, using Claude (Team plan) and Claude Code. Use-cases: drafting test cases from Jira stories, triaging failed CI runs, and drafting bug reports. No production customer data will be used; test environments use synthetic data. We'll connect the Atlassian MCP server with each tester's own access and Playwright locally; no other servers. All AI outputs are reviewed by a tester before use. We'll report time, coverage and quality measures fortnightly." (Illustrative.)

128.4Step 3: choosing pilot use-cases

Pick two to four use-cases from the ideas catalogue in Chapter 119. Good pilot use-cases share four traits:

  1. Frequent. Done every week, so you get enough data.
  2. Measurable. Clear start and end, easy to time.
  3. Low risk. A mistake is caught in review, not by customers.
  4. Visible. A win people will notice.
Use-caseFrequencyMeasurableRiskGood pilot?
Test cases from user storiesHighYes: time per storyLow (reviewed)Excellent
Failed CI run triageHighYes: MTTRLowExcellent
Bug report draftingHighYes: time, bounce rateLowGood
Test data generationMediumYesMedium (data rules)Good, with data rules
Auto-approving releasesLowHardHighNo: keep humans in charge

Write success criteria before you start

Agree them with your manager in week 1, so nobody moves the goalposts later. For example (illustrative): "Test design time per story falls by at least 30% including review; requirement questions raised at refinement increase; no rise in escaped defects; no data incidents; at least four of six testers want to continue."

Champions

An AI champion is a team member who learns early and helps others. A good champion network has one champion per team of about six to ten people, a regular slot to share what works, a shared place for prompts and skills, and a direct line to whoever runs the rollout. Champions should be given time for this, not expected to do it on top of a full workload.

128.6Step 5: a shared team setup

If every tester configures Claude differently, results vary and lessons don't spread. Put the setup in the test repository so everyone who clones it gets the same behaviour. These are the building blocks; their chapters have the exact syntax.

Building blockFileWhat it does for the teamDetails
Team instructionsCLAUDE.md in the repoHow to run tests, conventions, rules such as "never weaken a failing test without asking"Chapter 77
Shared skills.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.mdRepeatable team workflows: /bug-report, /test-plan, /triageChapter 80
Shared MCP servers.mcp.json in the repoEveryone gets Jira, Playwright and GitHub connections; secrets come from each person's environment variables, never from the fileChapter 83, 93
Permission rules.claude/settings.jsonAllow safe test commands; deny reading secrets; ask before writes to JiraChapter 78
Organisation-wide policymanaged-settings.json (deployed by IT)Rules individuals can't override, including which MCP servers are allowedChapter 78, 98
A team .mcp.json with no secrets in it (shape only; see Chapters 83 and 106 for exact, verified syntax)
{
  "mcpServers": {
    "playwright": { "command": "npx", "args": ["@playwright/mcp@latest"] },
    "github": {
      "type": "http",
      "url": "https://api.githubcopilot.com/mcp/",
      "headers": { "Authorization": "Bearer ${GITHUB_PAT}" }   // each tester's own token, from their environment
    }
  }
}
Secrets never go in shared files

Tokens and API keys stay in each person's environment variables or a secret manager, never in .mcp.json, CLAUDE.md or a skill (Chapter 98). Claude Code also asks each person to approve project servers from .mcp.json before they run, which is a deliberate safety check.

Organisations on managed plans can go further. IT can deploy a managed-settings.json file that sets rules nobody can override, and control which MCP servers are allowed (for example with an approved list) or deploy a fixed set through managed-mcp.json. Chapter 105 shows the verified syntax.

128.8Step 7: governance

Governance is a short list of rules everyone knows, not a thick binder.

RuleExample wording
Human review"Every AI-generated test, bug report or analysis is reviewed by a tester before it's used or shared."
Data handling"No production customer data, credentials or personal data in prompts. Use synthetic test data."
Approved tools and servers"Only approved MCP servers and plugins. Request new ones through the QA lead; security reviews them."
Writes need approval"Claude may draft Jira issues and PRs, but a person approves anything written to shared systems."
Red tests"AI may not change a failing test's expected result without a person confirming the requirement."
Incidents"Report suspected data leaks or prompt-injection attempts to security immediately."

Many of these can be enforced, not just written down: permission rules, managed settings and MCP allow-lists (119.6). Chapter 135 covers responsible AI and governance more broadly.

128.9Step 8: scaling across teams

  1. Decide with evidence. Scale only use-cases that met their success criteria.
  2. Package the setup. Turn the pilot's skills, CLAUDE.md patterns and permission rules into a shared template repository or a plugin (Chapter 84).
  3. Grow champions first. Each new team gets a champion trained by the pilot team.
  4. Roll out in waves. Two or three teams at a time, each with a short baseline.
  5. Keep measuring. A shared dashboard of the measures from Chapter 126, reviewed monthly.
  6. Keep improving. A monthly review of the skills library: retire what nobody uses, improve what people complain about.

128.10The human side

People have real concerns about AI, and ignoring them is the fastest way to fail. Address them openly.

Concern you'll hearAn honest response
"Will AI replace me?"Be truthful about the organisation's intentions. Where the aim is "same team, more coverage", say so clearly, and show how saved time goes into work the team has wanted to do (Chapter 126).
"I'm not technical enough."Start with the Claude app and plain-language chapters; pair with a champion; terminal skills come later and are learnable (Part VII).
"I don't trust its output."Good: that's the right instinct. Review is part of the process, and testers are well suited to it.
"This is extra work on top of my job."Protect learning time during the pilot; remove something else from the plate.
"It made a mistake, so it's useless."Track mistakes, fix them with better instructions, and compare overall results with the baseline.
Opinion · the tester's role is changing, not disappearing

Many practitioners expect QA roles to shift towards test strategy, risk analysis, reviewing AI output, testing AI systems themselves and building team tooling, and away from repetitive drafting. How fast this happens, and what it means for team sizes, varies by organisation and is genuinely uncertain. Investing in people's skills is the safest bet either way.

Common misunderstandings
"Adoption means buying licences."
Licences are step one of eight. Use-cases, setup, training, governance and measurement decide the outcome.
"Security will just say no."
A specific, bounded proposal with data rules and review steps is far more likely to be approved.
"Each tester should find their own way."
Personal experimentation is useful early, but shared setup is what lets good practice spread.
"Once it's rolled out, we're done."
Tools, models and features change monthly. Keep reviewing skills, settings and measures.

128.13Review

Key takeaways
  • Treat AI adoption as a change programme: readiness, sign-off, pilot, standardise, scale.
  • Bring security a specific proposal: tools, use-cases, data rules, MCP servers and review steps.
  • Pilot two to four frequent, measurable, low-risk use-cases for 90 days with success criteria agreed up front.
  • Share one setup through the repo (CLAUDE.md, skills, .mcp.json without secrets, permission rules), with managed settings for organisation-wide rules.
  • Train with clear paths, give champions time, keep governance short and enforceable.
  • Take people's concerns seriously; frame the goal as the same team covering more risk.
  • Use the maturity model to choose realistic next steps.
Check yourself

Which makes the best first pilot use-case?

It's frequent, measurable, low-risk and visible.

Where should a tester's GitHub token go when the team shares an .mcp.json file?

Shared files must never contain secrets (Chapter 98).

When should pilot success criteria be agreed?

Agreeing them up front stops goalposts moving and keeps the evaluation honest.

What best describes level 3, "Standardised", in the maturity model?

Standardisation means shared setup and rules across teams.

Assignment

Write a complete adoption plan for your QA team (two to three pages): readiness assessment, security proposal, chosen use-cases with success criteria, a week-by-week 90-day plan, the shared setup files you'll create, a training path from this book, five governance rules and your current and target maturity level. Review it with your manager and one security contact.

Teach it

"Rolling out AI in QA works like any change programme. Check we're ready, get security to agree a specific plan, pilot a few frequent, low-risk tasks for 90 days with goals agreed up front, and set Claude up the same way for everyone through the repo, with no secrets in shared files. Train people, give champions time, keep a short set of enforceable rules, and be honest about people's concerns. Then scale what worked."
