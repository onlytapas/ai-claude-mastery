Every answer an AI gives is produced by real chips, in real buildings, using real electricity. Training a model happens once; serving it (called inference) happens billions of times, so the running cost of answers matters enormously. The model writes its reply one token at a time, and for each token the chip has to read the model's huge set of numbers from memory, so memory speed often limits how fast answers come. Providers group many users' requests together to keep chips busy, which makes each answer cheaper but can make it slower. All of this shows up as the price per token you pay, and understanding it helps you choose the right model and settings for each job.

25.1From your prompt to a data centre and back

When you press Enter in Claude, ChatGPT or any AI app, your words don't stay on your laptop. They travel over the internet to a data centre: a large building full of servers, network equipment, power supplies and cooling. There, a copy of the model is already loaded onto specialised chips, waiting for work.

1. Your appSends your prompt (and the conversation so far) as text
2. Front doorThe provider checks your account, limits and safety rules
3. SchedulerPuts your request in a queue and picks a group of chips
4. InferenceChips read the whole prompt, then generate the reply token by token
5. StreamingTokens flow back to you as they are produced

Two words are worth getting straight from the start:

  • Training is how the model is made. It happens once (per model version), takes weeks or months, and uses enormous clusters of chips. Chapter 17 covers it, and the optional Chapter 138 goes deep on training hardware.
  • Inference is using the finished model to answer. It happens every time anyone sends a prompt. This chapter is about inference, also called serving.
In everyday life

Training is like writing and printing a recipe book: expensive, slow, done once. Inference is like a restaurant kitchen cooking from that book every evening. The kitchen's rent, staff and gas bill never stop, and the busier the restaurant, the more those running costs matter. For a popular AI service, the kitchen bill (inference) can end up larger than the book (training).

25.2The chips: GPUs, TPUs and Trainium

A language model is, underneath, a very large collection of numbers (its weights or parameters). Producing each token means multiplying your input by those numbers, billions of times over. Ordinary computer processors (CPUs) are built to do a few complicated things quickly, one after another. AI needs the opposite: millions of simple multiplications at the same time.

Chip familyWho makes itPlain descriptionWho uses it (examples)
GPU (graphics processing unit)NVIDIA (main supplier), also AMDOriginally built to draw video-game graphics, which also needs many simple sums in parallel. NVIDIA's CUDA software made them usable for general computing, and so for deep learning. Recent data-centre generations: Hopper (H100), Blackwell, and Vera Rubin.Almost every AI lab and cloud
TPU (tensor processing unit)GoogleA chip Google designed specifically for the maths of neural networks. Used for Google's own Gemini models and rented out through Google Cloud.Google DeepMind; Anthropic (a deal for up to one million TPUs, Oct 2025)

Anthropic is unusual in using all three: AWS Trainium, Google TPUs, and NVIDIA GPUs through Microsoft Azure. Spreading across suppliers gives more capacity and more bargaining power, at the cost of engineering work to make the models run well on each.

Why "accelerator"?

You'll often see GPUs, TPUs and Trainium grouped together as AI accelerators: chips that accelerate the specific maths AI needs. When someone says a lab has "100,000 accelerators", they mean chips of this kind, whoever made them.

25.3Memory bandwidth: the hidden bottleneck

Here's the surprising part. For chat-style answers, the chip's calculating speed is often not what holds things back. The limit is how fast it can move the model's numbers from memory into the calculating units. That speed is called memory bandwidth.

Why? Because the model writes one token at a time, and to produce each token it needs to use (almost) all of its weights. A large model's weights take up a great deal of memory. So for every single token, the chip has to stream that whole mountain of numbers past its calculators. The calculators finish their sums quickly, then sit waiting for the next delivery.

In everyday life

Imagine a brilliant chef who can chop anything in a second, but the ingredients arrive from a basement store along one narrow staircase. However fast the chef is, dinner comes out at the speed of the staircase. Memory bandwidth is the width of that staircase. AI chips use special stacked memory called HBM (high-bandwidth memory), which is like replacing the staircase with a wide lift, but it's still often the limiting factor.

Two phases of every answer: prefill and decode

Prefill (reading your prompt)

The model processes all your input tokens at once, in parallel. This keeps the calculators busy and is relatively efficient per token. A long prompt still takes time: this phase is what you wait through before the first word appears.

Decode (writing the reply)

The model generates output tokens one after another, each depending on the last. Each step re-reads the weights from memory for just one new token per request. This is the memory-bound, slower-per-token phase.

This split explains a pattern you'll see in every price list: output tokens cost more than input tokens, typically about five times more for Claude models. Reading is done in bulk; writing is done one careful step at a time.

Under the hood: the KV cache

During decode, each new token needs to "attend" to every earlier token (Chapter 16). Recomputing everything from scratch for every new token would be hugely wasteful. Instead, the server stores intermediate results for earlier tokens, the attention "keys" and "values", in a KV cache held in chip memory.

The KV cache grows with the length of the conversation. With very long contexts (current Claude models accept up to 1 million tokens), it can take up a large share of the chip's memory. That limits how many requests fit on a chip at once, which is one reason long-context requests are more expensive to serve, and why some providers price very long prompts differently. Claude Haiku 5.5, for example, has one price for prompts up to 100K tokens and a higher one above that.

A related idea is prompt caching: if many requests start with the same long text (a system prompt, a big document, a test-suite description), the provider can keep the processed version and reuse it. Anthropic charges a small fraction of the normal input price for these "cache reads" (for example $0.20 per million tokens on Opus 5.5 instead of $4), with a surcharge to write to the cache in the first place. Chapter 89 shows how to use it.

Under the hood: other tricks providers use
  • Quantisation: storing weights with fewer bits (for example 8 instead of 16), so there's less to move from memory. It can cost a little quality if done carelessly.
  • Mixture of Experts (MoE): the model is split into many "expert" sub-networks and only some are used for each token. Several 2026 models describe themselves this way, for example Microsoft's MAI-Thinking-1 (about 35B active out of about 1T total parameters) and Mistral Large 4 (about 52B active out of about 1T). Fewer active weights per token means less to read per step.
  • Speculative decoding: a small, fast model guesses several tokens ahead and the big model checks them in one go, accepting the ones it agrees with.
  • Splitting a model across chips: a large model rarely fits on one chip, so it is spread across several connected by very fast links. Chapter 138 covers how.

Which of these any particular provider uses for any particular model is usually not published. Treat claims about a specific closed model's internals as guesses unless the provider has said so.

25.4Data centres, electricity and cooling

Thousands of accelerators running flat out produce a great deal of heat and use a great deal of electricity. That's why AI news is now full of power stations, grid connections and cooling systems, and why AI companies describe their computing deals in gigawatts (a large power station produces roughly one gigawatt).

  • Anthropic's Google Cloud deal (Oct 2025) was described as "well over a gigawatt" of capacity in 2026.
  • Its Microsoft Azure commitment (Nov 2025) is up to 1 GW.
  • OpenAI's AMD deal (Oct 2025) was for 6 GW of chips.
Global data-centre electricity use (IEA estimate and projection)

The International Energy Agency's Energy and AI report (April 2025) estimated data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5% of the world's electricity, and projected about 945 TWh by 2030 in its base case, with AI the biggest driver of growth. This covers all data centres, not just AI. Source: IEA.

Cooling matters too. Chips are cooled by air or, increasingly, by liquid pumped close to the chips. Some cooling systems use water, which is why water use near new data centres is a local political issue in several places.

Opinion · a live debate

People disagree sharply on how worrying AI's energy use is. The IEA itself notes that data centres are a modest share of total electricity demand growth to 2030, smaller than air conditioning or electric vehicles. Environmental groups and many local communities focus on the rapid local growth, grid strain and water use around specific sites. AI companies argue efficiency gains and AI-enabled science will offset the cost. These are positions held by those groups, not settled facts. As a user, the practical point is simple: smaller models and fewer wasted tokens use less energy as well as less money.

25.5Latency versus throughput, and the batching trade-off

Two measures describe how fast an AI service is, and they pull in different directions.

MeasurePlain meaningWho cares most
LatencyHow long one user waits. Often split into time to first token (how long before anything appears) and tokens per second while the reply streams.Someone chatting, a live support bot, an IDE autocomplete
ThroughputHow much total work the system gets through: tokens or requests per second across all users.The provider paying for the chips; a team processing 50,000 test logs overnight

Because decode is limited by reading weights from memory (25.3), a chip serving one user wastes most of its calculating power. If it serves 64 users at once, it reads the weights once per step and uses them for all 64. That's batching: grouping requests so one trip to memory serves many. Throughput rises dramatically. But each step now takes a little longer, so each individual user sees their tokens arrive a bit more slowly.

In everyday life

A taxi takes you straight to your door: low latency, but one passenger per trip. A bus carries 60 people along the same route: far cheaper per person and far more people moved per hour, but it stops more often and you may wait for it. AI providers run something like a very clever bus service, constantly adding and dropping passengers mid-route (this is called continuous batching).

The provider's choices about batch sizes are why the same model can be offered at different speeds and prices:

  • Anthropic's Message Batches API charges 50% less. You hand over a large set of requests and accept that results come back later (most batches finish within an hour). The provider can fit your work into quiet moments and large batches.
  • Fast mode is offered for Claude Opus 5.5 on the Claude API: the reverse trade, aiming for quicker output. Check the pricing page for its current terms before relying on it.
  • Smaller models such as Claude Haiku 5.5 have fewer weights to read per token, so they're both faster ("Fastest" in Anthropic's own comparison) and cheaper.
Real-world example

A QA team wants Claude to tag 20,000 historical bug reports by component and root-cause category for a quality dashboard. Nobody is waiting on any single answer. This is a perfect batch job: send it through the Batch API overnight and pay half price. The same team's in-IDE assistant, which a developer waits on while typing, is the opposite case: latency matters more than price, so batching delays would be unacceptable.

25.6The cost of an answer, and why prices differ

AI APIs charge per token, quoted per million tokens (often written MTok), with separate prices for input and output. These are Anthropic's list prices on the Claude API, verified 9 November 2026:

ModelInput / MTokOutput / MTokCache read / MTokAnthropic's latency label
Claude Fable 5.1$10$50$0.25Slower
Claude Opus 5.5$4$20$0.20Moderate
Claude Sonnet 5.5$2$10$0.10Fast
Claude Haiku 5.5$0.10 (prompts up to 100K tokens); $0.50 above$0.50; $2.50 above 100K$0.01 / $0.05Fastest

Batch requests are 50% off. Amazon Bedrock and Google Cloud set their own prices for the same models. Prices change: check the official pricing page before budgeting.

Why one model costs 100 times another

  1. Size. Bigger models have more weights to read for every token and need more chips to hold them. More hardware per token means more cost.
  2. Thinking. Reasoning models generate hidden "thinking" tokens before answering (Chapter 19). You usually pay for those as output tokens, so a harder effort level can multiply the real cost of an answer even at the same price per token.
  3. Context length. Long prompts use more memory (the KV cache) and more prefill work.
  4. Speed promises. Faster service usually means smaller batches and more chips per user.
  5. Business strategy. Prices are also set by competition, the cost of training (which must be recovered somehow), and how much customers will pay. A low price does not prove a model is cheap to run, and a high one does not prove it's expensive. Providers don't publish their actual serving costs.
Tokens are not words, and tokenizers differ

Price per token only compares fairly if the models count tokens the same way. Anthropic says Claude 4.7 and later use a newer tokenizer that produces "approximately 30% more tokens for the same text". When comparing providers, measure the token count of your own real prompts on each (Chapter 14), rather than comparing list prices alone.

25.7What this means for you

You don't need to buy chips to benefit from understanding them. Most of the practical advice follows directly from the sections above:

High volume and simple (classify, extract, route)Try the smallest model first, measured against an evalFewer weights per token: faster and much cheaper
Not time-sensitive (nightly reports, back-filling labels)Use the Batch APIThe provider fills its quiet capacity: 50% off
Reusing the same long instructions or documentPut the fixed part first and use prompt cachingCache reads cost a small fraction of fresh input

Subscription plans (Pro, Max, Team and so on) hide per-token prices behind usage limits, but the same physics applies: heavier models and longer contexts use up your allowance faster. Chapter 42 covers plans, and Chapter 126 covers measuring whether AI is worth what you pay.

Price a real QA job and see the batching trade-off25 min · your computer, Python 3, no API key
  1. Create a folder called serving-lab and save this as cost_of_answer.py:
    cost_of_answer.py
    # cost_of_answer.py - what does one AI job cost? (prices verified 9 Nov 2026)
# Prices are US dollars per million tokens (MTok): (input, output).
PRICES = {
    "Haiku 5.5":  (0.10, 0.50),   # prompts up to 100K tokens
    "Sonnet 5.5": (2.00, 10.00),
    "Opus 5.5":   (4.00, 20.00),
    "Fable 5.1":  (10.00, 50.00),
}
BATCH_DISCOUNT = 0.5   # Message Batches API: 50% off

def cost(model, input_tokens, output_tokens, batch=False):
    price_in, price_out = PRICES[model]
    dollars = (input_tokens * price_in + output_tokens * price_out) / 1_000_000
    return dollars * (BATCH_DISCOUNT if batch else 1)

# Illustrative workload: triage 2,000 bug reports.
# Each call sends about 1,500 tokens (instructions + report)
# and gets back about 300 tokens (labels + short reason).
JOBS, IN_TOK, OUT_TOK = 2000, 1500, 300

print(f"Workload: {JOBS} calls x {IN_TOK} in / {OUT_TOK} out tokens (illustrative)")
print(f"{'Model':<12}{'Per call':>12}{'All calls':>12}{'Batched':>12}")
for model in PRICES:
    one = cost(model, IN_TOK, OUT_TOK)
    print(f"{model:<12}{one:>12.5f}{one*JOBS:>12.2f}{cost(model, IN_TOK, OUT_TOK, True)*JOBS:>12.2f}")

share = (OUT_TOK * PRICES['Opus 5.5'][1]) / (IN_TOK * PRICES['Opus 5.5'][0] + OUT_TOK * PRICES['Opus 5.5'][1])
print(f"\nOn Opus 5.5, output is {OUT_TOK/(IN_TOK+OUT_TOK):.0%} of the tokens but {share:.0%} of the cost.")

Common misunderstandings
"Once a model is trained, answers are basically free."
Every answer uses chips, electricity and cooling. At large scale, serving can cost more in total than training did.
"A faster chip always means faster answers."
For token-by-token output, memory bandwidth is often the limit, not calculating speed.
"Input and output tokens cost the same."
Output is typically about five times the input price for Claude, because it's generated one step at a time.
"The cheapest price per token is the cheapest model."
Tokenizers count differently, reasoning models add thinking tokens, and a wrong answer has a cost of its own. Compare total cost for the same real task.
"Batching makes my answer slower, so it's bad."
It's a trade-off. For work nobody waits on, accepting slower results for half the price is the right choice.

25.8Review

Key takeaways
  • Inference (serving answers) runs on accelerators in data centres, and it happens for every single prompt.
  • GPUs (mainly NVIDIA), Google TPUs and Amazon Trainium are the main accelerator families; Anthropic uses all three.
  • Generation has two phases: prefill reads the prompt in parallel; decode writes one token at a time and is often limited by memory bandwidth.
  • Data centres consume large and growing amounts of electricity (IEA: about 415 TWh in 2024, projected about 945 TWh by 2030).
  • Latency is how long one user waits; throughput is total work done. Batching raises throughput and lowers cost but can slow each user.
  • Prices differ by model size, thinking, context length, speed and business strategy. Output costs more than input.
  • Use small models, batching, caching and concise output where they fit; pay for capability deliberately where it matters.
Check yourself

During decode (writing the reply), what most often limits speed for a large model?

Each output token needs almost all the weights, so memory bandwidth, not calculating power, is usually the bottleneck.

Why do output tokens cost more than input tokens?

Prefill handles the whole prompt at once; decode needs a full pass through the weights for every new token.

Your team needs 30,000 old test logs summarised by Monday. Nobody waits on any single summary. What's the best fit?

When latency doesn't matter, let the provider batch your work and take the discount.

What does batching do to a serving system?

One trip to memory serves many requests, so total work rises, but each step takes a little longer.

Model A is cheaper per token than model B. Which statement is safest?

"Every AI answer runs on real chips in a data centre: NVIDIA GPUs, Google TPUs or Amazon's Trainium.
