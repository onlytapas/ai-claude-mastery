Computers are good with numbers and bad with meaning. An embedding bridges the gap: it turns a word, a sentence or a whole document into a long list of numbers, chosen so that texts with similar meanings get similar lists. You can then measure how close two meanings are with simple arithmetic. That one trick powers search that understands what you mean rather than just the words you typed, finds duplicate bug reports written in different words, groups thousands of log messages into themes, and lets Claude look up the right documents before it answers.

15.1From IDs to meaning

In Chapter 14, text became token IDs: "login" might be number 4,512 and "sign" number 1,873. But those numbers are just positions in a list, like seat numbers in a theatre. Seat 4,512 isn't "more" than seat 1,873, and neighbouring seats have nothing in common. The IDs carry no meaning.

What a model needs is a representation where similar meanings are close together. That is an embedding: a vector (a list of numbers) for each piece of text, learned so that "login" and "sign in" end up near each other, and far from "invoice".

In everyday life

Think of a map. Every town has two numbers: latitude and longitude. Those numbers mean something. Towns with similar numbers are close together, and you can measure the distance between any two. An embedding is a map of meaning. Every word or sentence gets coordinates, and similar meanings are neighbours. The difference is that a real map has two dimensions, while a meaning map has hundreds or thousands.

Let's build a tiny meaning map by hand, with just three dimensions so we can picture it. We'll score some bug titles on three questions, from 0 to 1. These scores are made up (illustrative); a real model learns its own dimensions, and they rarely have neat names.

Notice something important: "Login fails with valid password" and "Cannot sign in after reset" share almost no words, yet their vectors are almost the same. That's the whole point. A keyword search for "login" would miss the second bug; a search by meaning would find it.

Imagine each vector as an arrow from the centre of a room. The login bugs point towards one corner, the payment bug towards another wall, and the performance bug towards the ceiling. Similar meaning means the arrows point in a similar direction.

15.3Cosine similarity, worked by hand

To compare two arrows, the most common measure is cosine similarity. It measures the angle between them, ignoring their length:

Cosine similarityAngle between arrowsMeaning
10° (same direction)Same meaning
around 090° (at right angles)Unrelated
−1180° (opposite)Opposite direction (rare in practice with text embeddings)

The recipe has three steps:

1. Dot productMultiply matching numbers and add them up
2. LengthsFor each vector: square each number, add, take the square root
3. DivideDot product ÷ (length A × length B)

Compare A "Login fails with valid password" [0.9, 0.1, 0.0] with B "Cannot sign in after reset" [0.8, 0.0, 0.2]:

StepCalculationResult
Dot product0.9×0.8 + 0.1×0.0 + 0.0×0.20.72
Length of A√(0.81 + 0.01 + 0)√0.82 ≈ 0.906
Length of B√(0.64 + 0 + 0.04)√0.68 ≈ 0.825
Cosine similarity0.72 ÷ (0.906 × 0.825)≈ 0.96: very similar

Now compare A with C "Card payment declined" [0.1, 0.9, 0.1]:

StepCalculationResult
Dot product0.9×0.1 + 0.1×0.9 + 0.0×0.10.18
Length of C√(0.01 + 0.81 + 0.01)√0.83 ≈ 0.911
Cosine similarity0.18 ÷ (0.906 × 0.911)≈ 0.22: not very similar
Why ignore length?

A long, wordy bug report and a short one about the same problem should count as similar. Cosine similarity cares only about direction, so length differences don't matter. Many embedding providers also normalise their vectors to length 1. Then the lengths in step 2 are both 1 and cosine similarity is just the dot product, which is faster to compute. Anthropic's embeddings guide notes that Voyage AI embeddings are normalised this way.

Other ways to measure closeness

Euclidean distance is the straight-line distance between the tips of two arrows: subtract matching numbers, square, add, take the square root. Smaller means closer. Dot product is step 1 alone; it rewards both direction and length. For vectors normalised to length 1, all three give the same ranking of results, which is why providers often say "use any of them". If vectors aren't normalised, cosine similarity is the safe default for text.

The similarity score itself has no universal scale. With one model, related texts might score 0.8 and unrelated ones 0.3; with another, the numbers cluster differently. Never copy a threshold such as "duplicates are above 0.85" from one model to another without testing it on your own data.

15.4Dimensions: why so many numbers?

Our toy map had three dimensions. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands. Each dimension is one number in the list. More dimensions give more room to separate subtle differences: tone, topic, language, formality, technical domain and thousands of other features at once.

In a learned embedding, the dimensions usually don't have tidy names like "about login". Meaning is spread across many of them together. You can't point at dimension 417 and say what it means; what matters is how whole vectors relate to each other.

In everyday life

Describing a wine with two numbers (red or white, sweet or dry) works for basic choices. A sommelier uses dozens of qualities: fruit, acidity, tannin, oak, region, age. More dimensions let you tell apart wines that the two-number description would lump together. Embeddings are the same, with far more qualities, none of which a person chose.

Trade-off

More dimensions usually means better quality but more storage and slower search. Ten million documents × 1,024 numbers × 4 bytes each is about 41 GB of vectors (illustrative arithmetic). Some providers let you choose a shorter vector, and Voyage AI supports "Matryoshka" embeddings, where the first part of a long vector works as a shorter one.

15.5How embeddings are learned

Nobody sets the numbers by hand. They are learned by training a neural network (Chapter 12) on a task where similar meanings must end up close together. The core idea comes from linguist J. R. Firth's 1957 line: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

Word embeddings (word2vec, 2013)

The network learns to predict a word from its neighbours, or the neighbours from a word. Words used in similar contexts ("login", "sign-in", "authenticate") end up with similar vectors. One vector per word, regardless of context.

Sentence embeddings (2019 onwards)

Models such as Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) are trained on pairs of texts: pull pairs that mean the same thing (a question and its answer, two paraphrases) closer together, push unrelated ones apart. This is called contrastive learning. The result is one vector for a whole sentence or paragraph.

Why "king − man + woman ≈ queen" works

If many directions in the space consistently correspond to relationships, then arithmetic can follow them. In word2vec, the arrow from "man" to "woman" turned out roughly parallel to the arrow from "king" to "queen", because those pairs appear in similar contexts differing in a similar way. So king − man + woman lands near queen. The same works, approximately, for capitals (Paris − France + Italy ≈ Rome) and verb tenses.

This is a famous result, but it's approximate and cherry-picked: many analogies don't work, and the trick usually excludes the input words from the answer. Embeddings also absorb biases in their training text. Bolukbasi and colleagues (2016) showed word2vec placed "man : computer programmer" as analogous to "woman : homemaker". The data lessons from Chapter 13 apply here too.

Real-world example

A QA team receives about 60 new bug reports a week (illustrative), many of them duplicates written by different people. They embed the title and description of every open bug. When a new one arrives, a small script compares it with all open bugs and posts a comment listing the three most similar, with scores. Triage still decides; the script just saves the search. After two weeks of checking its suggestions against human decisions, they set the threshold at the score where it was right most of the time on their data.

Limits
  • Similar is not the same as relevant or correct. "Login works on Safari" and "Login fails on Safari" can score very high, because embeddings often blur negation.
  • Exact identifiers. Embeddings are weak at exact matches like BUG-1042, error code E4031 or a specific version number. Keyword search is better there, which is why systems often combine both.
  • Model lock-in. Vectors from different models aren't compatible. Change model and you must re-embed everything.
  • Privacy. Sending text to an embedding API sends your data to that provider. Check policy before embedding customer data or internal documents.

15.8Lab: compute similarity yourself

Part A needs only Python 3. It does the hand calculation from 15.3 in code, then builds the simplest possible "embedding" from word counts to show why learned embeddings are needed. Part B (optional) uses a real, free embedding model on your laptop.

similarity.py
# similarity.py - cosine similarity by hand, then a tiny duplicate-bug finder
import math
import re
from collections import Counter

def cosine(a, b):
    dot = sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
    len_a = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a))
    len_b = math.sqrt(sum(y * y for y in b))
    return dot / (len_a * len_b)

# Part 1: hand-made 3-number "embeddings" (illustrative)
# dimensions: [about login, about payment, about performance]
toy = {
    "Login fails with valid password": [0.9, 0.1, 0.0],
    "Cannot sign in after reset":      [0.8, 0.0, 0.2],
    "Card payment declined":           [0.1, 0.9, 0.1],
    "Dashboard loads slowly":          [0.1, 0.0, 0.9],
}
query = "Login fails with valid password"
print(f"Similarity to: {query!r}")
for text, vec in toy.items():
    print(f"  {cosine(toy[query], vec):.2f}  {text}")

# Part 2: word-count vectors for real bug titles
bugs = [
    "Login button does nothing on Safari",
    "Safari: clicking the login button has no effect",
    "Unable to sign in using Safari browser",
    "Checkout total wrong when coupon applied",
    "Coupon discount not applied to checkout total",
]

def words(text):
    return re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())

vocab = sorted({w for b in bugs for w in words(b)})

def count_vector(text):
    c = Counter(words(text))
    return [c[w] for w in vocab]

vectors = [count_vector(b) for b in bugs]
print(f"\nVocabulary size (dimensions): {len(vocab)}")
print("Pairs with similarity >= 0.30:")
for i in range(len(bugs)):
    for j in range(i + 1, len(bugs)):
        s = cosine(vectors[i], vectors[j])
        if s >= 0.30:
            print(f"  {s:.2f}  #{i + 1} vs #{j + 1}")
print(f"Bug #1 vs #3 (same bug, different words): {cosine(vectors[0], vectors[2]):.2f}")
real_embeddings.py (optional, Part B)
# real_embeddings.py - the same bug titles with a free, local embedding model
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util

bugs = [
    "Login button does nothing on Safari",
    "Safari: clicking the login button has no effect",
    "Unable to sign in using Safari browser",
    "Checkout total wrong when coupon applied",
    "Coupon discount not applied to checkout total",
]

model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")   # downloads the model on first run
emb = model.encode(bugs)
print("Vector length:", len(emb[0]))

scores = util.cos_sim(emb, emb)
for i in range(len(bugs)):
    for j in range(i + 1, len(bugs)):
        print(f"  {float(scores[i][j]):.2f}  #{i + 1} vs #{j + 1}")

"No module named sentence_transformers": the install went to a different Python; use python3 -m pip install with the same python3 you run the script with, or create a virtual environment with python3 -m venv .venv and activate it first. Install is very slow or fails: the library pulls in PyTorch, which is large; Part A alone covers the chapter's ideas. Opinion · practitioners building search systems

Many engineers who build retrieval systems report that a hybrid of keyword and embedding search, followed by a "reranker" model that rescores the top results, beats either method alone. Voyage AI and others sell rerankers for this reason. Others argue that with good long-context models, simpler pipelines are often enough. The right answer depends on your documents and should be measured with your own evaluation set (Chapter 24).

Common misunderstandings
"Claude can create embeddings for me through the Claude API."
Anthropic doesn't offer an embedding model. Use a separate provider such as Voyage AI, or an open-source model.
"Each dimension means something specific, like 'about login'."
That was true only of our toy example. In learned embeddings, meaning is spread across many dimensions at once.
"A similarity of 0.8 always means 'very similar'."
Scores depend on the model. Set thresholds by testing on your own data.
"High similarity means the texts agree."
It means they're about similar things. "Test passed" and "Test failed" can be close.
"Embeddings store the text, so you can read it back."
You can't simply turn a vector back into the original words, but researchers have shown that text can sometimes be partly reconstructed from embeddings. Treat stored vectors of sensitive text as sensitive data.

15.9Review

Key takeaways
  • An embedding is a list of numbers that places text on a "map of meaning", where similar meanings are close.
  • Cosine similarity compares direction: dot product ÷ (length × length). 1 is the same direction, about 0 is unrelated.
  • Real embeddings have hundreds to thousands of dimensions; meaning is spread across them, not one per dimension.
  • Embeddings are learned from context ("the company a word keeps") and, for sentences, from pairs that should be close or far.
  • Token embeddings live inside language models; sentence embeddings come from separate embedding models you call yourself.
  • Anthropic has no embedding model of its own; its docs point to Voyage AI as one option among several.
  • Uses: semantic search, duplicate detection, clustering, classification and RAG. Weaknesses: negation, exact IDs, model lock-in, privacy.
Check yourself

Two vectors are [1, 0] and [0, 1]. What is their cosine similarity?

The dot product is 1×0 + 0×1 = 0, so the similarity is 0: the arrows are at right angles, meaning unrelated.

Why can embedding search find "Cannot sign in" when you search for "login failure"?

Learned embeddings place paraphrases close together, which keyword search can't do.

Your team builds a Claude-based assistant over internal test documents. Where do the document embeddings come from?

Anthropic does not offer its own embedding model; its documentation points to Voyage AI as one provider and suggests assessing several.

Which search is embedding similarity worst at?

Exact identifiers are a keyword-search job. Many systems combine keyword and embedding search for this reason.

You switch to a newer embedding model. What must you do with the millions of vectors already stored?

Each model has its own space. Vectors from different models can't be compared with each other.

Teach it

"An embedding turns text into a long list of numbers, like map coordinates for meaning. Texts that mean similar things get coordinates that are close, even if they use different words, so 'can't sign in' sits right next to 'login fails'. To compare two, we use cosine similarity, which checks whether the arrows point the same way: 1 is identical, near 0 is unrelated. That's how semantic search, duplicate-bug detection and RAG work. Two things to remember: Anthropic doesn't make an embedding model, so you use another provider like Voyage AI or an open-source model; and similar isn't the same as identical, because 'works' and 'fails' can look close. Always check the threshold on your own data."
