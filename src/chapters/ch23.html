A model only knows what was in its training data and what's in front of it right now. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) puts the right facts in front of it: before the model answers, a search step finds the most relevant passages from your own documents and adds them to the prompt. The model then answers from those passages and cites them. Most of the engineering is in the search: splitting documents into chunks, turning them into embeddings, storing them in a vector database, and combining meaning-based search with old-fashioned keyword search. Good retrieval makes a good answer possible; bad retrieval guarantees a bad one.

23.1Why RAG exists

Ask a general model "What's our retry policy for the orders API?" and it has three honest options: say it doesn't know, guess (a hallucination), or ask you. It can't know, because:

  • Private data was never in training. Your Confluence pages, test strategy and Jira history are yours.
  • Training has a cutoff. Anything newer than the knowledge cutoff is missing.
  • Memory of rare facts is fuzzy. Even public, rare details are recalled unreliably (Chapter 22).

The fix is to turn a closed-book exam into an open-book one. You can do that by hand by pasting a document into the chat. RAG does it automatically, at scale, choosing which few pages out of thousands to "open the book at".

In everyday life

A good reference librarian doesn't memorise every book. When you ask a question, they search the catalogue, pull the three most relevant books, open them at the right pages and answer from them, pointing at the paragraph. RAG is the librarian. The catalogue is the index, pulling books is retrieval, and pointing at the paragraph is citation.

When you don't need RAG

Context windows are now large (the current Claude models accept 1 million tokens, Chapter 20). Anthropic's own article on retrieval (September 2024) suggests that if your knowledge base is smaller than about 200,000 tokens (roughly 500 pages), you can often skip RAG and put the whole thing in the prompt, using prompt caching to keep it fast and cheaper (Chapter 89). RAG earns its complexity when the material is too big, changes often, or must respect who is allowed to see what.

ApproachBest whenWeak when
Paste it in (long context)A few documents, one-off questionsLarge or changing collections; cost per question grows with size
RAGLarge, changing collections; answers need sourcesQuestions that need the whole collection at once ("summarise every defect this year")
Tools and agentic searchLive systems (Jira, databases, a code repository) the model can query step by stepSlower; needs permissions and careful tool design (Chapter 97)
Fine-tuningTeaching a style, format or narrow skillAdding facts: they get blurred like any trained memory, and updating means retraining
RAG is not the only kind of retrieval

Coding agents such as Claude Code mostly find what they need by running search tools (text search across files, file listings, reading files) step by step, rather than querying a pre-built vector index. That is retrieval too, just driven by the model. "RAG" usually means the pipeline in this chapter, where a separate search step runs before the model answers.

23.2The pipeline

RAG has two halves. The indexing half runs ahead of time, whenever documents change. The query half runs every time someone asks a question.

1. IngestCollect documents: Confluence, PDFs, Jira, READMEs. Extract clean text and metadata (title, date, owner, permissions)
2. ChunkSplit into passages of a few hundred tokens, keeping the source with each
3. EmbedTurn each chunk into a vector with an embedding model
4. StoreSave vectors, text and metadata in a vector database and a keyword index
5. QueryThe user asks a question; embed it the same way
6. RetrieveFind the most similar chunks (often keyword + semantic), optionally rerank
7. AugmentPut the top chunks in the prompt with clear source labels
8. GenerateThe model answers only from those chunks and cites them

The name describes the second half: generation (the model writing) is augmented (improved) by retrieval (the search). The idea and the name come from a 2020 paper by Patrick Lewis and colleagues at Facebook AI Research, University College London and New York University.

23.3Chunking: cutting documents into pieces

You can't embed a 300-page manual as one vector: a single list of numbers can't hold the meaning of every page, and you want to retrieve the one relevant paragraph, not the whole manual. So documents are split into chunks.

StrategyHowTrade-off
Fixed sizeEvery N tokens (for example a few hundred), often with an overlap between neighboursSimple; can cut a sentence or table in half
Structure-awareSplit on headings, paragraphs, list items, code functionsKeeps ideas whole; needs clean input
Per recordOne chunk per Jira ticket, test case or FAQ entryNatural for structured data; long tickets may still need splitting

Chunk size is a balance. Too small, and a chunk loses its meaning ("It must finish in under 10 minutes": what must?). Too large, and one chunk mixes several topics, so its vector is a blurred average and it matches poorly. Overlap (repeating a sentence or two between neighbours) reduces the risk of cutting an idea in half.

Real-world example

A test-strategy page says under the heading "Smoke suite": "It runs on every pull request. It must finish in under 10 minutes." Split by sentence, the second chunk has no idea what "it" is. A search for "smoke test time limit" may miss it. Keeping the heading with each chunk, or prefixing a short summary of where the chunk came from, fixes this. That second fix is what Anthropic calls contextual retrieval (section 23.6).

23.4Embeddings and vector databases

From Chapter 15: an embedding model turns text into a vector, a list of perhaps a thousand numbers, arranged so that texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions. "Users get logged out after a deploy" and "sign-in problems after a release" share almost no words but land close together.

Embeddings with Claude

Claude is a text-generation model; Anthropic does not offer its own embedding model. Anthropic's documentation describes Voyage AI (now part of MongoDB) as one embeddings provider with a wide range of models, and also advises assessing a variety of vendors. Many other providers and open-weight embedding models exist. You can mix freely: one company's embeddings for retrieval, Claude for the answer.

A vector database stores those vectors alongside the original text and metadata, and answers one question very fast: "Which stored vectors are nearest to this query vector?" That's called nearest-neighbour or similarity search, and "nearest" is usually measured with cosine similarity (or the dot product, which gives the same ranking when vectors are normalised to length 1).

Query: "users get logged out after a deploy" (illustrative scores)
Sign-in problems after a deploy… expired session cookie0.82
Login timeout bug QA-1423 is fixed0.61
Release 4.2.0 shipped on 3 March0.44
Orders API returns HTTP 4290.12
Illustrative numbers, not from a real embedding model. Higher means closer in meaning; retrieval keeps the top few.
Under the hood: how a vector database searches millions of vectors fast

The simple approach compares the query with every stored vector. That's exact, and fine for thousands of chunks, but slow for hundreds of millions. Vector databases therefore use approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) indexes that trade a tiny chance of missing the true best match for huge speed-ups.

A widely used one is HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs, Malkov and Yashunin, 2016). Every vector is linked to a few of its neighbours, in several layers: the top layer has few, long-distance links (like motorways), lower layers have more, shorter links (like local streets). A search starts at the top, hops greedily towards the query, then drops a layer and refines. It visits a tiny fraction of the vectors and still usually finds the true nearest ones.

Other tricks include clustering vectors and only searching the nearest clusters, and quantisation: storing each number in fewer bits (for example 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats) to cut memory, at a small accuracy cost.

Options range from libraries you run yourself (such as FAISS), to extensions of databases you may already have (such as pgvector for PostgreSQL, or vector search in Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and MongoDB Atlas), to dedicated vector databases (such as Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Milvus and Chroma). Which one to use is a production question for Chapter 92; the concepts are the same.

23.5Keyword, semantic and hybrid search

Meaning-based search sounds strictly better than matching words. It isn't. Each has blind spots, and testers will recognise them immediately.

Keyword searchSemantic search
MatchesThe same words (after simple normalising)Similar meaning, even with different words
Classic methodBM25, the scoring formula behind many search enginesEmbeddings plus nearest-neighbour search
Great forExact identifiers: QA-1423, error codes, function names, version numbersQuestions in everyday words: "why do users get kicked out?"
Fails onSynonyms and paraphrase: "logged out" won't match "sign-in problems"Rare exact tokens: an embedding may treat QA-1423 and QA-1432 as nearly identical

Hybrid search runs both and merges the results. A simple, robust way to merge is reciprocal rank fusion (RRF): each chunk gets 1 / (k + rank) from each list it appears in (k is commonly 60), and the scores are added. Chunks that rank well in either list rise; chunks that rank well in both rise most. It needs no tuning of how scores from the two methods compare, which is why it's popular.

Under the hood: what BM25 rewards

BM25 scores a chunk for a query by adding up, for each query word found in the chunk:

  • Rarity (IDF): words that appear in few chunks count for more. "QA-1423" is rare, so it's decisive; "test" is everywhere, so it barely counts.
  • Frequency, with diminishing returns: a word appearing twice counts more than once, but the tenth mention adds little (controlled by a parameter usually called k1).
  • Length normalisation: long chunks naturally contain more words, so their scores are scaled down a little (parameter b).

The lab script implements exactly this in about ten lines.

23.6Making retrieval better: reranking and contextual retrieval

Two widely used improvements sit on top of hybrid search.

Reranking. First retrieval is fast but rough. A reranker is a slower, more accurate model that looks at the query and each candidate chunk together and scores how well the chunk answers the question. The usual pattern: retrieve many candidates (say 100–150) cheaply, rerank them, keep the best 10–20 for the prompt.

Contextual retrieval. In September 2024 Anthropic published a technique for the "lost context" problem from section 23.3. Before indexing, a model writes a short sentence or two explaining where each chunk sits in its document ("This chunk is from the smoke-suite section of the test strategy; 'it' refers to the smoke suite"), and that context is added to the chunk before embedding and before building the BM25 index. Anthropic reported on its own test sets that:

Top-20 retrieval failure rate (lower is better)
Standard embeddings5.7%
+ Contextual embeddings and contextual BM252.9%
+ Reranking1.9%
Source: Anthropic, "Introducing Contextual Retrieval" (19 September 2024): a 49% reduction, and 67% with reranking. Vendor-reported results on its own datasets; your mileage will vary.

23.8How RAG goes wrong

When a RAG system gives a wrong answer, the first question is always: did retrieval fail, or did generation fail? Chapter 125 shows how to test each half separately.

FailureSymptomTypical fix
Retrieval
Missed chunk"I don't know" when the answer existsHybrid search, better chunking, contextual retrieval
Wrong chunkConfident answer about a similar but different thing (QA-1432 vs QA-1423)Keyword search for identifiers; reranking
Stale chunkAnswer from last year's version of the policyRe-index on change; date metadata; prefer newest

Ignores sourcesAnswer from general knowledge instead of the documentsStronger grounding instructions; citations
Over-mergesCombines two chunks into a claim neither makesRequire one citation per claim; faithfulness checks
Injected instructionsA document says "ignore previous instructions…" and the model obeysTreat retrieved text as data; see prompt injection (Chapter 103)

No rows match.

Retrieval doesn't respect permissions by itself

If HR documents and engineering wikis go into the same index, a search for "salary" from an engineer will happily retrieve HR chunks. Store access rights as metadata and filter on them during retrieval, before anything reaches the prompt. Telling the model "don't reveal HR data" is not access control.

Build a tiny retriever and ground Claude with it30 min · terminal and claude.ai

This script is a complete, working miniature of steps 2 and 4–7 of the pipeline. It uses only the Python standard library. Real embedding models aren't free to call, so the "semantic" step uses a small hand-written synonym table as a stand-in: crude, but it shows exactly what semantic search adds over keywords.

minirag.py
# minirag.py - a tiny retrieval pipeline in plain Python 3 (no packages)
import math, re, sys
from collections import Counter

DOCS = {
    "release-notes.md": """Release 4.2.0 shipped on 3 March. Support for IE11 was removed.
The login timeout bug QA-1423 is fixed. Password reset emails now expire after 30 minutes.""",
    "test-strategy.md": """Smoke tests run on every pull request and must finish in under 10 minutes.
Full regression runs nightly against the staging environment.
Flaky tests are quarantined and must be fixed within one sprint.""",
    "api-guide.md": """The orders API returns HTTP 429 when a client sends too many requests.
Clients should wait and retry. Authentication uses a bearer token in the Authorization header.""",
    "oncall.md": """If production checkout fails, page the on-call engineer.
Sign-in problems after a deploy are usually caused by an expired session cookie.""",
}

STOP = {"the", "a", "an", "is", "are", "and", "or", "of", "to", "in", "on",
        "for", "when", "what", "how", "do", "does", "my", "be", "after", "by", "it"}

def words(text):
    return [w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9-]+", text.lower()) if w not in STOP]

# 1. CHUNK: one sentence per chunk, remembering its source
chunks = []
for name, text in DOCS.items():
    for sent in re.split(r"(?<=\.)\s+", text.strip()):
        chunks.append({"source": name, "text": sent})

# 2. KEYWORD SEARCH: BM25, the classic search-engine formula
N = len(chunks)
avg_len = sum(len(words(c["text"])) for c in chunks) / N
df = Counter(w for c in chunks for w in set(words(c["text"])))

def bm25(query, chunk, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
    tf = Counter(words(chunk["text"]))
    length = sum(tf.values())
    score = 0.0
    for w in words(query):
        if w not in tf:
            continue
        idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[w] + 0.5) / (df[w] + 0.5))
        score += idf * tf[w] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[w] + k1 * (1 - b + b * length / avg_len))
    return score

# 3. "SEMANTIC" SEARCH: a stand-in for embeddings. Real systems use an
#    embedding model; here a small synonym table maps words to concepts.
CONCEPTS = {
    "login": "signin", "logged": "signin", "sign-in": "signin", "signin": "signin", "log": "signin",
    "session": "signin", "password": "signin", "authentication": "signin",
    "slow": "speed", "minutes": "speed", "timeout": "speed",
    "throttled": "ratelimit", "429": "ratelimit", "too": "ratelimit", "many": "ratelimit",
    "limit": "ratelimit", "retry": "ratelimit",
    "deploy": "release", "release": "release", "shipped": "release", "4": "release",
    "broken": "failure", "fails": "failure", "bug": "failure", "problems": "failure",
    "flaky": "failure",
}

def vector(text):
    return Counter(CONCEPTS.get(w, w) for w in words(text))

def cosine(a, b):
    dot = sum(a[k] * b[k] for k in a)
    na = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in a.values()))
    nb = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in b.values()))
    return dot / (na * nb) if na and nb else 0.0

def semantic(query, chunk):
    return cosine(vector(query), vector(chunk["text"]))

# 4. HYBRID: reciprocal rank fusion combines the two ranked lists
def ranked(score_fn, query):
    scored = [(score_fn(query, c), i) for i, c in enumerate(chunks)]
    return [i for s, i in sorted(scored, reverse=True) if s > 0]   # drop zero scores

def hybrid(query, k=60):
    fused = Counter()
    for ranking in (ranked(bm25, query), ranked(semantic, query)):
        for rank, i in enumerate(ranking):
            fused[i] += 1 / (k + rank + 1)
    return [i for i, _ in fused.most_common()]

def show(title, order, top=3):
    print(f"  {title}")
    if not order:
        print("    (no matches)")
    for i in order[:top]:
        c = chunks[i]
        print(f"    [{c['source']}] {c['text'][:70]}")

query = " ".join(sys.argv[1:]) or "users get logged out after a deploy"
print(f"Query: {query}  ({N} chunks)")
show("Keyword (BM25):", ranked(bm25, query))
show("Semantic (concepts):", ranked(semantic, query))
show("Hybrid (RRF):", hybrid(query))

# 5. AUGMENT: build the prompt you would send to Claude
print("\n--- Prompt to paste into Claude ---")
print("Answer using ONLY the sources below. Cite the source name for every")
print("claim. If the sources do not contain the answer, say you don't know.\n")
for n, i in enumerate(hybrid(query)[:3], 1):
    print(f"<source id=\"{n}\" name=\"{chunks[i]['source']}\">{chunks[i]['text']}</source>")
print(f"\nQuestion: {query}")

Common misunderstandings
"RAG trains the model on our documents."
Nothing is trained. The model's weights don't change; relevant text is added to each prompt at question time.
"A vector database is a special kind of AI."
It's a database optimised for one query: find the stored vectors nearest to this one. The intelligence is in the embedding model that produced the vectors.
"Semantic search makes keyword search obsolete."
Embeddings are weak at exact identifiers like ticket numbers and error codes. Hybrid search usually beats either alone.
"With a 1M-token context window, RAG is dead."
Long context removes the need for RAG on small collections. Large or fast-changing collections, per-user permissions and cost per question still favour retrieval.
"If it cites a source, the answer is right."
A citation shows where the model looked, not that it read the passage correctly or that retrieval picked the right passage.

23.9Review

Key takeaways
  • RAG gives a model the facts at question time: retrieve relevant passages, add them to the prompt, answer from them with citations.
  • Pipeline: ingest → chunk → embed → store (indexing), then query → retrieve → augment → generate.
  • Chunking is a balance between losing context (too small) and blurring topics (too big).
  • A vector database finds the nearest vectors fast, usually with approximate indexes such as HNSW.
  • Keyword search (BM25) nails exact identifiers; semantic search handles paraphrase; hybrid with rank fusion combines them.
  • Reranking and contextual retrieval measurably reduce retrieval failures (Anthropic reported 49%, and 67% with reranking, on its datasets).
  • For small collections, putting everything in the prompt can be simpler. Permissions must be enforced at retrieval, not in the prompt.
Check yourself

What does RAG change about the model?

The weights stay the same. The model reads retrieved passages as part of its input.

A user searches for "QA-1423" and the system returns a chunk about QA-1432. Which change helps most?

Exact identifiers are where embeddings are weakest and keyword search is strongest.

Why are documents split into chunks before embedding?

Long texts average many topics into one blurred vector. Chunks keep meaning focused and retrieval precise.

What is contextual retrieval, as described by Anthropic?

The added context stops chunks like "It must finish in under 10 minutes" from losing what "it" refers to.

Engineers can retrieve HR salary documents through the company assistant. Where should this be fixed?

Prompts aren't access control. Anything retrieved can end up in the answer.

Teach it

"The model doesn't know our documents, so we look them up for it. Ahead of time, we cut our docs into small chunks and turn each into a list of numbers that captures its meaning, stored in a vector database. When someone asks a question, we find the chunks closest in meaning, plus chunks that share exact words like ticket numbers, and paste the best few into the prompt with their source names.
