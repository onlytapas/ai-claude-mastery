Every model launch comes with charts showing it beating its rivals. Those numbers are real measurements, but of a narrow thing, under chosen conditions, usually reported by the company selling the model. A model card or system card tells you what the model is for, how it was tested and what risks were found. A benchmark score tells you how it did on one fixed set of tasks. Reading them well means asking the questions a good tester asks of any test report: what exactly was tested, how, by whom, how many times, and does it resemble my use? The final answer always comes from testing on your own tasks.

33.1Where claims about models come from

When a new model is released, information arrives from several places. They differ a lot in how much you should trust them.

In everyday life

Buying a used car, you read the seller's advert, the manufacturer's spec sheet, an independent review and the MOT history, and then you take it for a test drive. The advert isn't lying, but it shows the car's best side. The test drive is the only one about your commute. Model launch posts are the advert; your own evals are the test drive.

33.2Model cards and system cards

The idea of a model card comes from a 2019 paper by Margaret Mitchell and colleagues, "Model Cards for Model Reporting". They proposed a short, standard document shipped with every model, like a nutrition label. Their suggested sections are still the backbone of most model cards today:

SectionWhat it tells youQuestions a tester asks
Model detailsWho built it, version, date, type, licenceIs this the exact version I'll use?
Intended useWhat it's for, and uses that are out of scopeIs my use case inside the intended scope?
FactorsGroups, conditions or environments that affect performanceWere languages, accents, domains like mine considered?
MetricsHow performance was measured and whyDoes the metric match what "good" means for me?
Evaluation dataWhich datasets were used for testingDo they look like my data?
Training dataWhat it learned from (often only at a high level)Could the test data have been in the training data?
Quantitative analysesResults, ideally broken down by factorAre there weak spots hidden by the average?
Ethical considerationsRisks, sensitive uses, mitigationsWhat could go wrong in my context?
Caveats and recommendationsKnown limits and adviceWhat does the maker themselves warn about?

On Hugging Face, every model repository has a model card as its front page (the README you see when you open the model). Quality varies a great deal: some are thorough; some are a one-line description and a licence.

A system card is the larger document that frontier labs such as Anthropic and OpenAI publish with major releases. "System" is a hint: it describes the model as deployed, including safeguards around it. A typical system card covers:

  • capabilities and benchmark results, with the settings used;
  • safety evaluations: harmful-request handling, over-refusal, bias, honesty and hallucination rates;
  • dangerous-capability testing (for example cyber, biological and chemical risks) and red-teaming by internal and external testers;
  • agentic safety: how the model behaves with tools, including prompt injection robustness;
  • the deployment decision, such as which safety level applied. Anthropic's Transparency Hub, for example, states that Claude Opus 5 and Claude Fable 5.1 were deployed with ASL-3 protections under its Responsible Scaling Policy (Chapter 38).
Real-world example

A QA lead is asked whether a new model can review accessibility issues in screenshots. The launch post shows a high "vision" score. The system card, read more carefully, shows the vision benchmarks were about charts and documents, and lists small-text reading as a known limit. That's not a "no", but it changes the plan: run a 30-screenshot pilot on the team's own pages before trusting it (see Chapter 21 on multimodal limits).

Read it in twenty minutes

System cards can run to well over a hundred pages. You don't need all of it. Read the summary, the section on your use (coding, agents, vision), the stated limitations, and any table where the new model is worse than the previous one. Those regressions are the most useful lines in the document.

33.3What the famous benchmarks measure

A benchmark is a fixed set of tasks with a scoring method, used to compare models. Each one measures something specific. These are among the most cited in 2026:

BenchmarkWhat it measuresStatus note (2026)
MMLUMultiple-choice knowledge across 57 subjectsLargely saturated by frontier models; harder variants such as MMLU-Pro are used instead
GPQA DiamondGraduate-level science questions written by PhD experts, hard to answer by searchingWidely quoted hardest subset
SWE-bench VerifiedFixing real GitHub issues in Python repositories so that tests pass; a human-checked 500-task subsetOpenAI stopped reporting it on 23 Feb 2026, citing contamination and flawed tasks, and recommends SWE-bench Pro
Terminal-BenchAgents completing real tasks in a command-line terminalNewer versions cited in 2026 launches
ARC-AGI-3Solving novel, game-like interactive puzzles from a few examplesAt launch (March 2026) humans solved 100% and the best AI about 0.37%
Humanity's Last ExamAbout 2,500 very hard expert-written questions across many fieldsBuilt to sit at the edge of human knowledge
Arena (formerly LMArena / Chatbot Arena)People vote blind between two chatbot answers; votes become Elo-style rankingsMeasures preference, not correctness

Notice how different these are. A model can top a knowledge quiz and be mediocre at fixing code; it can win human preference votes by sounding confident and friendly while being less accurate. No single number captures "better".

Benchmark scoring terms you'll meet
  • Accuracy / pass rate. The share of tasks answered correctly. The headline number on most charts.
  • pass@1 vs pass@k. pass@1 means the first attempt must be right. pass@k means the task counts as solved if any of k attempts is right. pass@10 is always at least as high as pass@1, so compare like with like.
  • Elo / Arena score. A rating from head-to-head votes, borrowed from chess. Differences of a few points between neighbours are often within the uncertainty.
  • Zero-shot vs few-shot. Whether examples were included in the prompt. Few-shot usually scores higher.
  • With or without tools. Scores on maths or knowledge tests can jump when the model may run code or search the web. Charts don't always make clear which was used.
  • Effort / thinking budget. Reasoning models score differently at different effort levels (Chapter 19). A launch chart may show maximum effort, while your app runs at the default.

33.4Reading a benchmark claim like a tester

Imagine a launch post says: "Model A scores 72.4% on CodeFix-500, beating Model B's 70.8%." Before you believe the ranking, ask six questions.

1. Which versions?Exact model IDs and dates, for both models
2. Which settings?Effort, tools, prompts, attempts (pass@1 or pass@k), the scaffold or harness
3. Who ran it?The vendor, the benchmark authors or an independent group?
4. How many tasks?Is the gap bigger than the noise?
5. What was left out?Benchmarks where it lost; regressions from the last version
6. Like my work?Language, domain, task length, tools

Question 4 is the one most often skipped. Scores measured on a few hundred tasks have a margin of error, just as an opinion poll does. Here is a small script that estimates it:

bench.py
# bench.py - is a benchmark gap bigger than the noise?
# Uses a normal approximation for two independent pass rates. Rough, but honest.
import math

def margin(p, n):
    """95% margin of error for a pass rate p measured on n tasks."""
    return 1.96 * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)

def compare(name_a, score_a, name_b, score_b, n_tasks):
    a, b = score_a / 100, score_b / 100
    gap = a - b
    se_gap = math.sqrt(a * (1 - a) / n_tasks + b * (1 - b) / n_tasks)
    low, high = gap - 1.96 * se_gap, gap + 1.96 * se_gap
    print(f"{name_a}: {score_a:.1f}% +/- {margin(a, n_tasks) * 100:.1f}")
    print(f"{name_b}: {score_b:.1f}% +/- {margin(b, n_tasks) * 100:.1f}")
    print(f"Gap: {gap * 100:+.1f} points, 95% range {low * 100:+.1f} to {high * 100:+.1f}")
    if low > 0 or high < 0:
        print("Verdict: the gap is larger than the noise for this many tasks.")
    else:
        print("Verdict: within the noise - the ranking could flip on a rerun.")
    print()

# Illustrative figures, not real results
compare("Model A", 72.4, "Model B", 70.8, n_tasks=500)
compare("Model A", 72.4, "Model B", 62.0, n_tasks=500)
compare("Model A", 90.0, "Model B", 80.0, n_tasks=40)

The third case matters for your own work: a ten-point gap on 40 test cases can easily be luck. Small internal evals need either more cases or repeated runs before you declare a winner.

Why this script is deliberately rough

The script treats the two scores as independent samples. When both models are run on the same tasks, a paired analysis (counting the tasks where only A succeeded versus only B) is tighter and more appropriate; McNemar's test is the classic method. Models are also non-deterministic, so each model's own score varies from run to run; repeating the whole run several times and reporting the spread is good practice. The rough version is still useful as a first sanity check: if even it says "within the noise", don't announce a winner.

33.5The common traps

Cherry-picking

A vendor runs its model on twenty benchmarks and shows the eight where it wins. Nothing on the chart is false; the picture is still misleading. Look for what's missing: a benchmark rivals usually report, or last generation's chart that included a test now dropped.

Contamination

Models train on huge scrapes of the internet. If a benchmark's questions and answers are online, they may end up in training data, and the model can score well by remembering rather than solving. This is one reason OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified in February 2026. Benchmarks built from new, private or regularly refreshed tasks resist contamination better.

In everyday life

If last year's exam paper with answers has been circulating among students, high marks this year prove less. Some students learned the subject; some learned the answers. The examiner can't tell from the marks alone.

Saturation

When top models all score near the ceiling (as with MMLU), the benchmark stops separating them. A one-point gain at 92% is mostly noise and possibly memorisation. New, harder benchmarks keep appearing for this reason.

Teaching to the test

Once a benchmark becomes a target, effort goes into raising that number specifically. This is Goodhart's law: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure". Testers know this from code-coverage targets that produce tests with no assertions.

Leaderboard gaming

Public leaderboards can be gamed too. A 2025 study of Chatbot Arena, "The Leaderboard Illusion" (Singh et al.), reported that some providers privately tested many variants and published only the best; the authors observed as many as 27 private Meta variants in the run-up to the Llama 4 release. These are the authors' findings; treat leaderboard positions as one signal, not a verdict.

"State of the art" marketing

"SOTA" (state of the art) just means "best reported score on this benchmark, under our conditions, at the time of writing". It may be best among open models only, best at one size, or best by 0.3 points. It also expires quickly.

Apples and oranges

Scores for different models are often gathered under different conditions: one at maximum effort with tools and a custom agent harness, another at default settings from its own launch post. Agentic coding scores in particular depend heavily on the harness (the code around the model that runs tools and retries). Only compare numbers from the same setup.

Chart tricks

A bar chart whose vertical axis starts at 60% makes a two-point gap look enormous. Always read the axis and the actual numbers.

Opinion · common among evaluation practitioners

Many people who build AI evaluations argue that public benchmarks are best used to shortlist models, never to make the final choice. The final choice should come from a small, representative eval on your own tasks, scored the way your users would judge the output. This book takes the same view; Chapter 36 shows how.

33.7From reading claims to running your own

Benchmarks and cards narrow the field. Your own testing decides. A QA team is already well placed for this: you know how to write test cases, define expected results, and report pass rates with caveats.

Chart shows ranges rather than single numbers: use the midpoint and note it. python3 not found on Windows: use py bench.py. A launch post you open requires login: pick a different public one.

Common misunderstandings
"The model at the top of the leaderboard is the best model."
It's the best on that leaderboard's tasks under its conditions. For your task, a different model may win, and close ranks are often within the noise.
"Vendor benchmark numbers are made up."
Usually they're real measurements. The issue is selection and settings, not fabrication. Read them carefully rather than dismissing them.
"A high score means the model understands the subject."
It might, or the test might be contaminated or saturated. Check whether the benchmark resists memorisation.
"Model cards are legal paperwork."
They are the maker's own statement of intended use, limits and test results: the best single place to find out what not to use the model for.
"Our 20-case pilot proved Model A is better."
On 20 cases, even a large-looking gap can be luck. Use more cases, repeat runs and report the margin.

33.8Review

Key takeaways
  • Claims come from launch posts, model cards, system cards, leaderboards, independent evaluations and your own tests; trust rises down that list.
  • Model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) set out intended use, factors, metrics, data, results and caveats; system cards add safety testing and deployment decisions.
  • Each benchmark measures something narrow; preference votes are not correctness, and knowledge quizzes are not coding skill.
  • Ask: which versions, which settings, who ran it, how many tasks, what's missing, and is it like my work?
  • Watch for cherry-picking, contamination, saturation, Goodhart's law, leaderboard gaming, "SOTA" wording and truncated axes.
  • Use public numbers to shortlist; decide with your own representative evals.
Check yourself

What is benchmark contamination?

Contamination inflates scores without real skill. It's one reason OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified in 2026.

A launch chart shows Model A at 72.4% and Model B at 70.8% on a 500-task benchmark, single run. What's the best reading?

With 500 tasks, each score carries roughly ±4 points of uncertainty; a 1.6-point gap isn't convincing on its own.

Which document is most likely to describe a model's dangerous-capability testing and the safety level it was deployed under?

System cards cover safety evaluations, red-teaming and deployment decisions, beyond the capability results in a model card.

What does Arena (formerly LMArena) mainly measure?

Preference is useful but different from accuracy: a confident, well-formatted answer can win a vote while being wrong.

How should a QA team use public benchmarks when choosing a model?

Benchmarks narrow the field cheaply; only your own representative tasks tell you how a model performs on your work.

Assignment

Pick two models your team might use. For each, find the model card or system card and complete a one-page "model fact sheet": exact version and date, intended uses and stated limits, three benchmark results relevant to your work (with settings and task counts), one regression or known weakness, and the source links. Then list five of your own real tasks you'd use to compare them, with what a "pass" looks like for each. Keep the fact sheets; you'll use them in Chapter 36.

Teach it

"Every AI launch comes with charts where the new model wins. The numbers are usually real, but chosen: the vendor picks the benchmarks, the settings and the comparison. So read them like a test report. Which exact versions? Same settings for everyone? How many test cases, and is the gap bigger than the noise? Could the questions have leaked into training data? What did they leave out? Model and system cards tell you intended use, limits and safety testing. Use all of that to shortlist, then test on our own work before deciding."
