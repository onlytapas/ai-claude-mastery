"AI makes us faster" is a feeling. Management needs evidence. The evidence comes from three simple steps: measure how things are today, try AI on a few specific tasks, and measure again, counting not only time saved but also coverage gained and any new risks. Do that honestly and you'll have a business case that holds up, and you'll know which uses of AI are actually worth keeping.

126.1Why measuring matters

Every organisation that adopts AI eventually gets asked the same question: "Is this worth what we're paying?" Teams that can't answer it lose budget, or keep paying for tools nobody uses well. Teams that can answer it get more investment, and they learn which practices to spread.

Measuring also protects you. AI can make some work faster while quietly making other work worse: for example, generating lots of test cases that someone then has to review, or automation that's quick to write but flaky. Only measurement shows the full picture.

In everyday life

If you start a new diet, you weigh yourself first. Otherwise, a month later you can't tell whether it worked. You also notice whether you feel tired or hungry, because losing weight isn't a win if you feel awful. Measuring AI is the same: a starting point, the main result, and the side effects.

126.2Start with a baseline

A baseline is a snapshot of how work is done before AI. It doesn't need to be perfect. One or two weeks of honest records for a handful of tasks is enough.

1 · Pick tasksThree to five repeatable QA tasks, e.g. writing test cases for a story
2 · Define a unit"Per story", "per bug report", "per failing build"
3 · RecordTime spent, output produced, quality notes, for 1–2 weeks
4 · Try AISame tasks, same people, with AI help, for 2–4 weeks
5 · CompareBefore vs after, including quality and side effects
Real-world example: a simple baseline sheet

A tester keeps a spreadsheet with one row per task done: date, task type ("test design", "bug report", "triage failing run"), story or ticket ID, minutes spent, and a short quality note ("two cases missed in review"). Ten minutes a day of note-taking for two weeks gives a usable baseline. Many teams already have some of this data in Jira, test management tools or CI dashboards; use it where you can.

Be fair to the "before"

Don't measure the baseline during an unusually quiet or chaotic period, and don't compare your best AI-assisted day with an average manual one. Compare like with like: similar stories, similar complexity, the same people.

Group your measurements into five families. You don't need all of them. Choose two or three per use-case.

The risk family is the one most teams forget, and it's the one that makes your case trustworthy (section 117.7).

Leading and lagging indicators

Some numbers move quickly; others take months. A leading indicator changes early and hints at where results are going, for example "requirement ambiguities raised before development" or "time to design tests". A lagging indicator shows the end result but only after a delay, for example "escaped defects per quarter".

A 90-day pilot (Chapter 128) is too short to prove much about lagging indicators. So report leading indicators during the pilot, and promise to track lagging ones over the following two or three releases. Saying this openly makes your case more credible, not less.

126.4Vanity metrics and Goodhart's law

Some numbers are easy to count and look impressive but don't show value. These are vanity metrics:

Vanity metrics (avoid)

  • Number of prompts sent
  • Number of test cases generated
  • Lines of test code written
  • "Percentage of people who tried the tool"

Value metrics (prefer)

  • Time to design tests for a story, at the same review quality
  • Requirement defects caught before development
  • Regression run time and manual hours removed
  • Escaped defects over several releases

Goodhart's law warns that when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. If you reward "test cases generated", people (and AI) will generate lots of shallow, duplicate test cases. If you reward "tests passing", someone may weaken assertions.

Real-world example: a QA version of Goodhart's law

A team set a target to "automate 80% of regression cases this quarter". With AI help they hit it, but many of the new tests only checked that pages loaded, not that the right data appeared. The number looked great; the safety net was thin. The fix was to pair the coverage target with a quality check: sample ten new tests a week and confirm each one fails when the feature is broken (Chapter 121). (Illustrative scenario.)

126.6Calculating ROI, step by step

The formula

ROI = (benefit − cost) ÷ cost, shown as a percentage. If you spend 100 and get 300 of value back, ROI = (300 − 100) ÷ 100 = 200%.

The hard part is turning "benefit" into a number. The usual approach for QA is to convert hours saved into money using a loaded hourly cost, then subtract the extra review time. Coverage and quality gains are often harder to price; list them separately rather than inventing a figure.

Worked example · all numbers are illustrative, not real data

Team: 8 testers. Use-cases piloted: test design from stories, and failure triage.

StepCalculationResult
1. Baseline test design3 hours per story × 20 stories a month60 hours/month
2. With AI (including review)1.75 hours per story × 20 stories35 hours/month
3. Hours saved, test design60 − 3525 hours/month
4. Baseline triage40 failing runs × 45 min30 hours/month
5. With AI (including checking)40 × 25 minabout 16.7 hours/month
6. Hours saved, triage30 − 16.7about 13.3 hours/month
7. Total hours saved25 + 13.3about 38 hours/month
8. Value of hours38 × an assumed loaded cost of $50/hour$1,900/month
9. Cost8 seats × an assumed $25 + an assumed $100 API usage$300/month
10. ROI(1,900 − 300) ÷ 300about 533%

Plus benefits listed but not priced: more scenarios designed per story, ambiguities caught at refinement, and faster feedback to developers.

Notice what makes this example honest:

  • "With AI" times include review. Savings that ignore review time are fake savings.
  • Assumptions (hourly cost, seat price, API spend) are stated, so anyone can change them.
  • Training and setup time are left out of the monthly figure but should appear as a one-off cost in the full case.
  • Hard-to-price benefits are listed, not invented.
Sensitivity: what if your assumptions are wrong?

Good business cases show a range, not a single number. Recalculate with pessimistic assumptions: say, half the hours saved and double the cost. In the illustrative example that gives benefit $950 and cost $600, so ROI = (950 − 600) ÷ 600 ≈ 58%. Still positive. If your case only works under optimistic assumptions, that's a sign to pick better use-cases or run a longer pilot.

Also calculate the payback period: one-off costs (training, setup) ÷ monthly net benefit. If setup costs $4,000 of time and monthly net benefit is $1,600, payback is about 2.5 months.

126.8Same team, more coverage

When AI saves time, someone will ask whether the team can shrink. How you frame the answer matters, for your colleagues and for the quality of the product.

Most QA organisations have a long list of things they should test but don't have time for: accessibility checks, negative and edge cases, performance smoke tests, better test data, regression of older features, exploratory sessions on risky areas. Saved hours can be reinvested in that list. That turns time saved into risk reduced, which is usually what the business actually wants.

Hours savedFrom routine design, triage, reporting
ReinvestedAccessibility, edge cases, exploratory testing, automation backlog
Business outcomeMore coverage and fewer escaped defects with the same team
Opinion · how to frame the case

Presenting AI purely as a way to cut headcount tends to damage trust, slow adoption and lose experienced people, and the savings often turn out smaller once review time is counted. Presenting it as "the same team covering more risk, faster" is usually more accurate and more persuasive. Organisations do make staffing decisions for many reasons; your job is to give them honest numbers and show the full range of options.

126.9Presenting the case on one page

One-page business case template
TITLE: AI-assisted QA pilot results – [team], [dates]

1. WHAT WE TRIED (2–3 lines)
   Use-cases: test design from stories; failure triage.
   People: 8 testers. Tools: Claude (Team plan), Claude Code.

2. RESULTS (leading indicators, vs baseline)
   Test design time per story:  3.0 h → 1.75 h incl. review
   Triage time per failing run: 45 min → 25 min
   Ambiguities raised at refinement: 4 → 11 per sprint

3. COST
   Monthly: seats + API usage = $___    One-off: training + setup = $___

4. ROI (stated assumptions)
   Expected: ___%   Pessimistic: ___%   Payback: ___ months

5. QUALITY AND RISK (counter-metrics)
   Review rejections: ___  Flaky rate: ___  Data incidents: 0

6. WHAT WE'LL DO WITH SAVED TIME
   Accessibility checks on top 10 journeys; edge-case automation backlog.

7. ASK
   Extend to [teams]; approve [MCP servers]; [training time].

Lead with results, show costs plainly, state your assumptions, and end with a specific request. Managers trust a modest, well-evidenced claim far more than a dramatic one.

Common misunderstandings
"We'll measure once we've started."
Then you have nothing to compare with. Take the baseline first, even a rough one.
"Time saved writing equals time saved overall."
Only if you subtract review time. AI output that isn't reviewed isn't a saving; it's a risk.
"More test cases means better coverage."
Duplicates and shallow cases add review work without reducing risk. Measure risk areas covered, not raw counts.
"Our ROI is 500%, so the case is proven."
A single optimistic figure invites doubt. Show assumptions, a pessimistic case and counter-metrics.
"The goal is fewer testers."
The stronger, more accurate case is usually more coverage and faster feedback with the same team.

126.10Review

Key takeaways
  • Measure a baseline before using AI: a few tasks, a clear unit, one or two weeks of honest records.
  • Track effort, speed, coverage, quality and risk; choose two or three measures per use-case.
  • Prefer value metrics over vanity metrics; pair every target with a counter-metric to avoid Goodhart's law.
  • AI costs include seats or API usage plus training, setup, review and governance time.
  • ROI = (benefit − cost) ÷ cost; include review time, state assumptions, show a pessimistic case and a payback period.
  • Frame the result as "same team, more coverage" and present it on one page with a clear ask.
Check yourself

Why must you measure a baseline before introducing AI?

The baseline is the comparison point for every claim you make later.

Which of these is a vanity metric?

It's easy to count and looks impressive but says nothing about risk reduced.

AI cut test design from 3 hours to 1 hour, but review takes 45 extra minutes. What's the real saving per story?

3 h − (1 h + 0.75 h) = 1.25 h. Always include review time.

A team spends $400 a month and gets $1,200 of value. What is the ROI?

(1,200 − 400) ÷ 400 = 2, which is 200%.

What is Goodhart's law?

People optimise the number instead of the real goal, so pair targets with quality checks.

Assignment

Run a two-week baseline for three QA tasks on your team, then a two-week AI-assisted trial on the same tasks. Produce the one-page business case from 117.9 with an expected and a pessimistic ROI, at least two counter-metrics and a plan for the saved time. Ask a colleague to challenge every assumption before you share it.

Teach it

"To prove AI is worth it, measure how long tasks take today, try AI on the same tasks, and measure again, including the time spent reviewing AI's work. Track quality alongside speed so we don't celebrate shortcuts. ROI is benefit minus cost, divided by cost; state the assumptions and show a pessimistic case. And frame it as the same team covering more risk, not as cutting people."
