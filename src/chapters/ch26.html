The AI industry is a stack. At the bottom are chip makers; above them, cloud providers that rent out data centres; then the labs that train foundation models; then platforms that offer those models to developers; and at the top, the apps and tools people actually use. Most big companies sit on several layers at once, so they are partners in one place and competitors in another. Huge sums move between them, often as computing power rather than cash. In 2026 the industry consolidated sharply, and knowing who owns and depends on whom helps you judge the risks of the tools you rely on.

26.1The stack: five layers

When you ask Claude Code to write a Playwright test, you're using the top of a tall stack. Every layer below has to work, and every layer has companies competing to own it.

1. ChipsNVIDIA, AMD, plus in-house chips: Google TPU, Amazon Trainium, Microsoft Maia, Meta MTIA
2. Cloud and data centresAWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle, and dedicated builds such as Stargate and Colossus
3. Model labsTrain foundation models: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI, DeepSeek, Mistral, Alibaba (Qwen) and others
4. PlatformsSell model access to developers: model APIs, Amazon Bedrock, Microsoft Foundry, Google Vertex AI, Hugging Face
5. ApplicationsWhat people use: ChatGPT, Claude apps, Gemini, Copilot, Cursor, Perplexity, and AI features inside every product
In everyday life

Think of the food industry. Farmers grow wheat (chips), mills grind it (cloud), bakeries make bread (model labs), wholesalers distribute it (platforms), and cafés make sandwiches (applications). A supermarket chain might own its own bakery and its own cafés, and still buy flour from a rival. AI works the same way: vertical integration in some places, buying from competitors in others.

A foundation model is a large, general model trained on broad data that many different products can be built on. GPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok and Llama/Muse are all foundation-model families. The labs that build them are often called frontier labs when they compete at the cutting edge.

A few distinctions worth holding on to:

  • Research lab vs foundation-model developer. Some groups mainly publish research (university labs, Meta's FAIR in its early years). Foundation-model developers turn research into products they sell. Most frontier labs now do both, but the commercial side drives most decisions.
  • Cloud provider vs model lab. A hyperscaler rents out computing at enormous scale. Three of them (Google, Microsoft, Amazon) also build their own models, and all three sell other labs' models too.
  • Application company vs model lab. Many successful AI products don't train their own frontier model; they build on someone else's through an API. Increasingly, the biggest app companies train smaller in-house models too (Cursor's Composer, Perplexity's Sonar).
  • Coding platforms are now a category in their own right: Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Devin, Google's Antigravity and Jules. Most read an instruction file such as CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md.

26.3Open and closed ecosystems

The industry has two broad camps, with plenty of overlap.

Closed (proprietary)

You can use the model only through the company's apps or API. The weights stay private. Examples: GPT-6 Astra, Claude, Gemini, Grok 4.7, Muse Spark. The company controls safety measures, updates and pricing, and you get a managed service with no hardware to run.

Open weights

The trained weights are published so anyone can download and run the model, subject to a licence. Examples: DeepSeek V4 (MIT), Mistral Large 4, many Qwen3.8 models, NVIDIA Nemotron, Google's Gemma, OpenAI's gpt-oss. You control where data goes, but you run (and pay for) the hardware.

An open-weight model is not the same as open source: the training data and code are usually not published, and licences vary from very permissive (Apache 2.0, MIT) to custom terms with restrictions. Chapter 32 covers this in depth.

Real-world example: the camps keep shifting

Meta made open weights mainstream with Llama from 2023. In April 2026 it launched Muse Spark, its first model from Meta Superintelligence Labs, as a proprietary model, though it has since released some open-weight Muse models such as Muse Glimmer. OpenAI went the other way in August 2025, releasing open-weight gpt-oss models alongside its closed frontier line. Few companies are purely one or the other.

Opinion · competing views

Supporters of open weights (including Mistral and, historically, Meta) argue they spread benefits, allow independent safety research and prevent a few companies controlling AI. Labs that keep frontier models closed, including Anthropic and OpenAI for their top models, argue that releasing the most capable weights can't be undone and may help misuse. Both are positions, held by those groups; the debate is unresolved.

26.4How money and compute flow

Training and serving frontier models needs enormous computing power (Chapter 25). Model labs need compute; cloud companies and chip makers have it and want customers. The result is a web of deals in which investment money often comes back as spending on the investor's own cloud or chips.

In everyday life

It's like a landlord who invests in a promising restaurant on condition it rents the landlord's building and buys from the landlord's suppliers. The restaurant gets money and space; the landlord gets a tenant and a share of success. If the restaurant thrives, both win. If it struggles, both are exposed.

Opinion · is it a bubble?

Some financial commentators argue these circular arrangements, where investment returns as cloud and chip revenue, inflate the apparent size of the AI market and resemble past tech bubbles. Company leaders argue demand for AI compute is real and growing faster than supply. Treat both as views, not facts; the honest answer is that nobody yet knows how the economics settle.

Under the hood: how model labs make money

Most frontier labs combine four income streams:

  1. Consumer subscriptions (ChatGPT Plus, Claude Pro and Max, Google AI Pro and Ultra, SuperGrok).
  2. Business seats (Team and Enterprise plans, Microsoft 365 Copilot).
  3. Pay-per-token API usage by developers and companies, directly or through cloud marketplaces such as Bedrock, Vertex AI and Foundry, where the cloud company sets its own price and takes a share.
  4. Large enterprise and government contracts.

Companies with other businesses can fund AI differently: Google and Meta through advertising, Amazon and Microsoft through cloud consumption, NVIDIA through hardware sales, and DeepSeek through its owner, the hedge fund High-Flyer. That's one reason some can offer models cheaply or free: the model doesn't have to pay for itself directly.

26.5The 2026 consolidation

2026 saw several deals that changed who owns what. These facts were verified on 9 November 2026; some are still in progress.

  1. Feb 2026SpaceX acquires xAI in an all-stock deal (announced Feb 2). xAI, maker of Grok, was later renamed, and its API documentation now uses "SpaceXSI" (SpaceX Super Intelligence). The exact rename dates are not confirmed by an official source.
  2. Apr 2026Microsoft–OpenAI deal rewritten (Apr 27). Microsoft's licence to OpenAI's technology becomes non-exclusive through 2032; OpenAI may serve products on any cloud, with Azure remaining primary; the "AGI clause" is removed; OpenAI's revenue share to Microsoft is capped through 2030.
  3. Apr 2026Meta moves to the mostly proprietary Muse family (Muse Spark, Apr 8), a shift away from open Llama releases.
  4. Jun 2026Google retires Gemini CLI for free and individual users (Jun 18), replacing it with Antigravity CLI. In the same month Cognition renamed Windsurf to Devin Desktop.
  5. Jun–Aug 2026SpaceX acquires Cursor's maker Anysphere for $60B (announced Jun 16, closed Aug 14). One of the most popular AI code editors is now owned by the same group as Grok.
  6. Sep 2026NVIDIA agrees to buy Hugging Face for about $12.9B (definitive agreement Sep 2). Not yet closed: expected in the first half of 2027, pending regulators.
  7. Sep 2026Cohere signs a merger agreement with Aleph Alpha (pending).

The pattern: companies at one layer of the stack are buying into others. A rocket company now owns a model lab and a coding tool. The leading chip maker is buying the leading open-model hub. A cloud provider that was one lab's exclusive home no longer is.

Pending is not done

The NVIDIA–Hugging Face and Cohere–Aleph Alpha deals were not complete on 9 November 2026. Regulators can block or change deals. Say "agreed to buy", not "owns", until a deal closes.

26.6Why this matters for your team

Industry structure sounds abstract, but it affects decisions a QA team makes every quarter.

QuestionWhy the stack matters
Where does our data go?The same Claude model can be reached directly from Anthropic or through AWS Bedrock, Google Cloud or Microsoft Foundry. Each path has its own contract, data handling and region options (Chapter 41, Chapter 42).
What if a vendor changes owner or terms?Cursor's ownership changed in 2026; Gemini CLI's free tier ended. Tools you depend on can change direction quickly.
Are we locked in?Prompts, instruction files, MCP servers and evals are more portable than vendor-specific features. AGENTS.md and MCP are shared across many tools.
Is a "partner" neutral?A cloud marketplace may also sell its own competing models. That's normal, but worth knowing when you read recommendations.
Real-world example

A test-automation team standardises on one AI code editor. In 2026 its owner changes. Nothing breaks on day one, but the team's lead asks three sensible questions: does the contract survive the change, can our tests and instruction files move to another tool, and do our evals (Chapter 125) let us compare a replacement quickly? Teams that kept their prompts, MCP configuration and evals tool-neutral answered in an afternoon.

Common misunderstandings
"Each AI company does one thing."
Most big players work on several layers. Google makes chips, runs a cloud, trains models and sells apps.
"Partners can't be competitors."
Microsoft invests in OpenAI, hosts Claude, and builds its own MAI models. Partnerships and rivalry coexist.
"Open weights means open source."
Usually only the weights are published, under licences that vary. Training data and full code are rarely released.
"An agreed acquisition is a done deal."
Until it closes, regulators or the parties can change or stop it.
"If an app uses Claude, the app company is Anthropic."
Many apps build on another company's model through an API; the app, the platform and the lab can all be different firms.

26.7Review

Key takeaways
  • The AI stack has five layers: chips, cloud, model labs, platforms and applications.
  • Big companies span several layers, so they are partners and competitors at the same time.
  • Closed models are offered as services; open-weight models can be downloaded and run under a licence. Many companies do both.
  • Investment often returns as spending on the investor's cloud or chips, tying labs and clouds together.
  • 2026 brought major consolidation: SpaceX bought xAI and Cursor's maker, NVIDIA agreed to buy Hugging Face, and the Microsoft–OpenAI deal was rewritten.
  • For teams, structure affects data handling, lock-in and vendor risk: keep prompts, instruction files, MCP setups and evals portable.
