Machines learn in three classic ways, and the difference is the kind of feedback they get. In supervised learning every example comes with the right answer, like a student with an answer key. In unsupervised learning there are no answers at all, and the machine finds structure on its own, like sorting a pile of photos into groups. In reinforcement learning the machine tries things and gets rewards or penalties, like learning a game. A fourth idea, self-supervised learning, makes the answers out of the data itself, and that is how large language models such as Claude learn most of what they know.

10.1One question tells them apart

All machine learning means improving at a task from experience. The approaches differ in what kind of feedback the learner receives while it practises.

ApproachFeedback the learner getsTypical question it answersEveryday exampleQA example
SupervisedThe correct answer for every example"Which category is this?" or "What number goes with this?"Spam filter trained on emails marked spam / not spamPredict a bug's severity from past triaged bugs
UnsupervisedNone. Just the data"What groups or patterns are in here?" "What's unusual?"A shop grouping customers by buying habitsGroup 50,000 log lines into a handful of failure types
ReinforcementA reward or penalty after acting, often delayed"What should I do next to get the best result over time?"A program learning to play a video gameLearn which retry strategy makes flaky CI jobs pass most often
Self-supervisedAnswers made automatically from the data itself"What's the missing or next piece?"Guessing the hidden word in a sentenceHow LLMs that write your test cases were pre-trained
In everyday life

Think of three ways to learn to cook. Supervised: a teacher tastes every dish and tells you exactly what it should have been. Unsupervised: you're handed a cupboard of unlabelled spices and sort them into groups by smell, without anyone naming them. Reinforcement: you cook for friends each week and only find out from their faces whether it worked, sometimes long after you made the choices that mattered.

10.2Supervised learning: learning with an answer key

Supervised learning is the most common kind of machine learning in business. You collect examples, each paired with the correct answer, called a label. The model learns to map from the example to the label, so that it can label new examples it has never seen.

Each example is described by features: the measurable facts the model looks at. For a bug report, features might be the number of customers affected, whether there's a workaround, the component and the words in the title.

Labelled examples2,000 past bugs, each with its final severity
TrainingThe model adjusts itself until its guesses match the labels as closely as possible
New exampleA bug filed this morning, no severity yet
Prediction"Probably critical (82%)"

Two kinds of supervised task

Classification

The answer is a category. Spam or not spam. Critical, major or minor. Flaky or genuine failure. A model that does this is a classifier.

Regression

The answer is a number. How many minutes will this test suite take? How many defects will this release have? Note: this "regression" is a statistics word and has nothing to do with regression testing.

Real-world example: defect triage

A support team receives hundreds of bug reports a week. Two years of reports already have a component and severity set by experienced triagers. That history is a ready-made labelled dataset. A supervised model trained on it can suggest a component and severity for each new report, and a person confirms or corrects it. Each correction is a new label for the next round of training. The quality of the model depends heavily on the quality of those old labels: if triagers were inconsistent, the model learns their inconsistency (Chapter 13).

How you know it worked

You never judge a supervised model on the examples it trained on, because it may simply have memorised them. You hold back some labelled examples it never saw, and measure how often it gets those right. This is the same instinct as not letting developers write the only tests for their own code. Splitting data into training, validation and test sets is covered in Chapter 12 and Chapter 13.

Under the hood: the simplest supervised learner

The easiest supervised method to understand is nearest neighbour. To label a new example, find the most similar labelled example and copy its label. "Similar" means close together when the features are treated as coordinates. A bug affecting 8 customers out of 10 with a workaround sits at the point (8, 1); its nearest known neighbour might be (7, 1), labelled critical, so the prediction is critical.

Nearest neighbour doesn't really "train"; it just stores examples. Most other methods (decision trees, logistic regression, neural networks) compress the examples into a set of learned numbers, which is faster and generalises better. All of them share the same goal: draw a boundary between categories that works well on examples not yet seen. The lab below runs a nearest-neighbour classifier in about ten lines of Python.

Labels are the expensive part

Supervised learning needs labels, and labels need people. Labelling thousands of screenshots, log lines or bug reports takes time and expertise, and different people label differently. Much of the effort in real supervised projects goes into getting consistent labels, not into the model.

10.3Unsupervised learning: finding structure without answers

In unsupervised learning there are no labels. The model looks only at the data and finds structure in it: groups of similar items, the main directions in which items vary, or items that don't fit any pattern.

TaskWhat it findsQA use
ClusteringGroups of items that are similar to each otherGroup thousands of failing-test messages into a few root causes; group crash reports so duplicates are triaged once
Anomaly detectionItems that are unlike the restSpot an unusual spike in API response times or an odd pattern in a log
Dimensionality reductionA simpler summary of data with many features, often so it can be drawn on a chartPlot thousands of test runs on a 2D chart to see whether failures form distinct islands
In everyday life

Tip a jar of mixed buttons onto a table and ask a child to sort them. Nobody tells them the categories. They might group by colour, by size or by number of holes. Their groups are real, but they might not be the groups you wanted. Unsupervised learning is exactly like that: useful structure, but a person still has to look at each group and decide what it means.

Real-world example: clustering a bad night in CI

A nightly run produces 1,400 failed tests. Reading them one by one would take a day. A clustering step groups the failure messages by similarity and finds, say, four groups: 1,100 timeouts reaching the payments service, 200 "element not found" errors on one page, 80 assertion failures in pricing, and 20 assorted others. (The numbers are illustrative.) Now a tester investigates four problems instead of 1,400. The algorithm didn't know what "payments outage" means; it only saw that those messages looked alike. A human gives each cluster its name and decides what to do.

Modern clustering of text usually works on embeddings: lists of numbers that capture meaning, so that "connection refused" and "could not connect" land close together even though the words differ. Chapter 15 explains embeddings and uses them to detect duplicate bugs.

Under the hood: k-means in four steps

The best-known clustering method is k-means. You choose how many groups you want (k). Then:

  1. Pick k starting "centres" (for example, k random items).
  2. Assign every item to its nearest centre.
  3. Move each centre to the average of the items assigned to it.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the centres stop moving.

The result depends on k and on the starting centres, which is why clustering results need a human sanity check. Choosing k is itself a judgement call: too few groups hide real differences, too many split one problem into several. The lab runs k-means on a list of API response times.

10.4Reinforcement learning: learning by trial and reward

Reinforcement learning (RL) is about decisions. A learner, called the agent, acts in an environment. After each action it may receive a reward (a number: higher is better). Its goal is to learn a policy, a way of choosing actions, that collects the most reward over time.

ObserveThe agent sees the current situation (state)
ActIt chooses an action using its current policy
RewardThe environment responds and gives a reward, maybe zero
UpdateThe agent adjusts its policy towards actions that paid off
RepeatThousands or millions of times

Two things make RL harder than supervised learning. First, nobody tells the agent the right action; it only finds out how well things went. Second, rewards are often delayed: in chess, the reward (win or lose) arrives only at the end, and the agent has to work out which of its many moves deserved the credit.

Explore or exploit?

Every RL agent faces the same dilemma. Should it exploit the action that has worked best so far, or explore other actions that might be even better? Exploit too early and it may never find the best option. Explore too much and it wastes effort on bad options. A common simple answer is to exploit most of the time and explore at random, say, 10% of the time. You'll see exactly that in the lab.

In everyday life

You have a favourite lunch place near the office. Going there every day is exploiting: reliable, but you'll never discover the better place round the corner. Trying somewhere new every day is exploring: you'll find good places, but you'll also eat a lot of bad sandwiches. Most people settle on "usually the favourite, occasionally something new". That's a policy.

Real-world example: game agents

The best-known RL success is in games, where the reward (the score, or winning) is clear and the agent can practise millions of times in simulation. In March 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo, which combined deep learning, reinforcement learning and search, beat the top Go player Lee Sedol 4–1. RL is also used in robotics and in tuning systems such as recommendation and resource scheduling, wherever there is a clear signal of success.

Reward hacking: a tester's favourite failure

An RL agent maximises the reward you define, not the goal you meant. If you reward "number of tests passing", an agent allowed to edit the test suite might learn to delete failing tests. This is called reward hacking or specification gaming, and it's Goodhart's law in machine form: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Testers are good at spotting it, because finding the gap between "what the spec says" and "what we meant" is the job.

RL matters for Claude in two ways. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) uses rewards based on people's preferences to make a model more helpful and honest (Chapter 18). And RL on tasks with checkable answers, such as maths problems and code that must pass tests, is a major part of how reasoning models are trained (Chapter 19).

10.5Self-supervised learning: the bridge to LLMs

Supervised learning works brilliantly, but labels are expensive. You could never pay people to label trillions of words. Self-supervised learning solves this with a trick: hide part of the data and ask the model to predict it. The hidden part is the label, and it came free.

Next-word prediction

Take any sentence: "The login test failed because the session ___". Hide the next word. The model guesses; the real text says "expired". Every position in every document is a free training example. This is how GPT-style models, including Claude, are pre-trained. (Strictly, they predict the next token; see Chapter 14.)

Fill in the blank

Hide a word in the middle: "The ___ test failed because the session expired." The model uses words on both sides to guess "login". This is how BERT (2018, Chapter 6) was trained, and similar ideas are used for images by hiding patches of a picture.

Mechanically, self-supervised learning is supervised learning: there's an input and a correct answer, and the model is corrected when it's wrong. The difference is where the answers come from. Because nobody has to write them, the amount of training data is limited only by how much text (or images, or audio) you can gather. That's what made it possible to train models on trillions of tokens, which is what made LLMs capable (Chapter 17).

Why it fits on the map

Some books file self-supervised learning under unsupervised learning, because no human labels are involved. Others treat it as its own category. The name matters less than the idea: the data supervises itself.

How Claude's training uses all of them

A modern LLM is not trained one way. Its life combines the approaches in stages:

1. Self-supervised pre-trainingPredict the next token across a huge text collection. Learns language, facts and patterns.
2. Supervised fine-tuningLearn from example conversations showing good assistant answers.
3. Reinforcement learningLearn from rewards: human or AI preference judgements, and checkable tasks like code that must pass tests.
ResultA helpful assistant model, frozen and served for inference

Anthropic also uses its Constitutional AI approach, in which written principles guide AI-generated feedback, as part of post-training. Chapter 17 covers stage 1 and Chapter 18 covers stages 2 and 3.

Opinion · Yann LeCun (deep learning pioneer, founding director of Meta's FAIR lab) and others

A widely quoted analogy from LeCun's 2016 NeurIPS keynote compares intelligence to a cake: the bulk of the cake is learning without human labels (he later called it self-supervised learning), the icing is supervised learning, and the cherry on top is reinforcement learning. The point is that most knowledge comes from unlabelled data. Since then, reinforcement learning has become a much bigger part of training reasoning models than "the cherry" suggests, and researchers disagree about how the ingredients will balance in future.

10.6Choosing an approach for a QA problem

You'll rarely build these models from scratch at work, but you'll often need to recognise which approach a problem calls for, or which one a tool uses.

ProblemDo you have answers for past examples?Fitting approach
Suggest severity and component for new bug reportsYes, two years of triaged bugsSupervised classification
Predict how long the regression suite will take after a changeYes, past run durationsSupervised regression
Find the main causes behind 1,400 failures from last nightNoUnsupervised clustering
Alert when production error logs look unusualMostly noUnsupervised anomaly detection
Decide test execution order to find failures fastest, improving over many runsNo answers, but a measurable result each runReinforcement learning (or simpler heuristics first)
Draft test cases from a user storyNot needed by youUse a pre-trained LLM (self-supervised plus fine-tuning and RL, done by the vendor)
Often you don't need to train anything

For many text tasks that once needed a custom supervised model, such as classifying bug reports, a capable LLM with a good prompt and a few examples now does well with no training at all. Try that first, measure it on labelled examples you already have, and only train a dedicated model if you need lower cost, more speed or more consistency at large volume.   2. Create a folder, and in it a file called three_ways.py containing this code:
    three_ways.py
    # three_ways.py - supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning in plain Python
import random

# ---------- 1. Supervised: learn bug severity from labelled examples ----------
# Each bug: (customers affected out of 10, has workaround 0/1) -> label
labelled = [
    ((9, 0), "critical"), ((8, 0), "critical"), ((7, 1), "critical"),
    ((2, 1), "minor"),    ((1, 1), "minor"),    ((3, 0), "minor"),
]

def nearest_label(bug):
    # 1-nearest-neighbour: copy the label of the most similar known bug
    def distance(other):
        return (bug[0] - other[0]) ** 2 + (bug[1] - other[1]) ** 2
    best = min(labelled, key=lambda pair: distance(pair[0]))
    return best[1]

print("SUPERVISED")
for new_bug in [(8, 1), (2, 0)]:
    print(f"  bug {new_bug} -> predicted {nearest_label(new_bug)}")

# ---------- 2. Unsupervised: group response times with no labels ----------
times_ms = [110, 95, 130, 120, 870, 910, 105, 950, 880, 100]
centres = [times_ms[0], times_ms[4]]          # two starting guesses
for step in range(5):                          # k-means with k = 2
    groups = [[], []]
    for t in times_ms:
        nearest = 0 if abs(t - centres[0]) <= abs(t - centres[1]) else 1
        groups[nearest].append(t)
    centres = [sum(g) / len(g) for g in groups]

print("UNSUPERVISED")
for i, g in enumerate(groups):
    print(f"  group {i}: centre {centres[i]:.0f} ms, members {sorted(g)}")

# ---------- 3. Reinforcement: learn which retry strategy works best ----------
# Hidden success rates the agent does NOT know (illustrative)
true_success = {"retry_now": 0.2, "wait_1s": 0.5, "wait_5s": 0.8}
actions = list(true_success)
value = {a: 0.0 for a in actions}   # agent's estimate of each action
tries = {a: 0 for a in actions}
random.seed(42)

for episode in range(500):
    if random.random() < 0.1:                       # explore 10% of the time
        action = actions[int(random.random() * len(actions))]
    else:                                           # otherwise exploit
        action = max(actions, key=lambda a: value[a])
    reward = 1 if random.random() < true_success[action] else 0
    tries[action] += 1
    value[action] += (reward - value[action]) / tries[action]

print("REINFORCEMENT")
for a in actions:
    print(f"  {a:10s} tried {tries[a]:3d} times, estimated success {value[a]:.2f}")
print(f"  best strategy learned: {max(actions, key=lambda a: value[a])}")

Troubleshooting

  • python3: command not found: on Windows use python or py; on macOS install Python from python.org or with your package manager.
  • IndentationError: a line's spaces don't match. Re-copy the code and don't mix tabs and spaces.
  • SyntaxError near an f" string: your Python is older than 3.6. Install a current version.
  • Reinforcement numbers differ slightly from the expected output: check you kept random.seed(42) and changed nothing above it. The supervised and unsupervised parts should always match exactly.
Common misunderstandings
"Unsupervised means the computer understands the groups."
It finds groups of similar items. Naming them and deciding whether they matter is a human job.
"Reinforcement learning means the model learns while I chat with it."
RL used on Claude happens during training at Anthropic. During a chat the weights are frozen (Chapter 9).
"LLMs are trained with unsupervised learning, full stop."
Pre-training is self-supervised, then supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning follow. All three shape the final model.
"Regression in machine learning is about re-testing."
It means predicting a number. Same word, unrelated meaning.
"More data always fixes supervised learning."
More badly labelled data teaches the model the same mistakes more confidently.

10.7Review

Key takeaways
  • The three classic approaches differ by feedback: correct answers (supervised), no answers (unsupervised), rewards for actions (reinforcement).
  • Supervised learning needs labels and splits into classification (categories) and regression (numbers). Label quality limits model quality.
  • Unsupervised learning finds groups, oddities and simpler summaries; people still interpret them.
  • Reinforcement learning balances exploring and exploiting, and optimises exactly the reward you define, gaps and all.
  • Self-supervised learning creates labels from the data itself, which is what allowed training on trillions of tokens.
  • Claude's training combines self-supervised pre-training, supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning.
Check yourself

You have 5,000 past bug reports, each already tagged with the component that was eventually fixed. You want to suggest a component for new reports. Which approach fits?

You have the right answers (labels) for past examples, and the answer is a category.

A clustering tool splits last night's failures into six groups. What's still needed?

Clustering finds similarity, not meaning. Groups can merge two causes or split one.

Why is next-token prediction called self-supervised?

There is still a correct answer and correction, which is why it works like supervised learning, but the labels are free.

An RL agent rewarded for "more tests passing" starts skipping tests. What is this called?

The agent maximised the reward as written, not the goal intended. It's Goodhart's law applied to machines.

In the lab, what happened when the exploration rate was set to 0?

Without exploration the agent only exploits what looked best so far, so it may never try the better option.

Teach it

"Machines learn in three classic ways, and the difference is the feedback. Supervised: every example has the right answer, like bugs already tagged with severity, and the model learns to predict it. Unsupervised: no answers, the model just finds groups, like clustering a night's CI failures into four causes, and a person names them. Reinforcement: the model tries actions and gets rewards, like learning a game, and it will exploit any loophole in the reward. LLMs like Claude add a fourth trick, self-supervised learning: hide the next word and predict it, so every sentence ever written becomes free training data. Then fine-tuning and reinforcement learning shape it into an assistant."
