A prompt is everything you give Claude to work with: your request, the background, the material and any examples. Claude can only use what is in front of it, so a vague prompt gets a generic answer and a precise prompt gets a precise one. Anthropic's advice is to treat Claude like a brilliant new colleague who knows nothing about your project: say exactly what you want, explain why, show the format, and give it the real material. Then check the answer and refine the prompt, the same way you would fix a test that fails.

44.1Why the words matter

A language model writes the most fitting continuation of the text it has been given (Chapter 16). It has no access to your Jira board, your team's conventions or what you had in mind, unless you put them into the conversation. Everything it doesn't know, it fills in with the most typical guess. That's why "Write test cases for login" produces the same generic list for everyone: email format, wrong password, empty fields.

Anthropic's prompting guide puts it this way: "Think of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows. The more precisely you explain what you want, the better the result." It also suggests a simple test:

The colleague test

"Show your prompt to a colleague with minimal context on the task and ask them to follow it. If they'd be confused, Claude will be too." (Anthropic, Prompting best practices.)

In everyday life

Imagine asking a skilled contractor who has never seen your house to "fix the kitchen". You might get new cupboards when you wanted a dripping tap repaired. Tell them "the cold tap on the kitchen sink drips; please replace the washer, keep the existing tap, and don't start before 9 because the baby is asleep" and you get exactly the job you wanted. The contractor's skill didn't change; your instructions did.

This skill is often called prompt engineering. The name sounds technical, but most of it is clear writing. You already practise it whenever you write a good bug report: steps, expected result, actual result, environment. A good prompt has the same discipline.

A useful order for longer prompts is: material first, then the instructions and question at the end. Anthropic's long-context advice is to put long documents "near the top of your prompt, above your query, instructions, and examples", and notes that queries at the end improved quality "by up to 30 percent" in its tests with complex, multi-document inputs.

44.3Be clear and direct

Say exactly what you want, including things that seem obvious to you. Anthropic's guide: "If you want Claude to do more than the task strictly requires, explicitly request it rather than relying on the model to infer this from vague prompts." When order or completeness matters, give the instructions as numbered steps.

The second prompt is longer, but it saves time: the answer is usable first time, the boundaries (£10,000.00, £10,000.01) are tested, and Claude flags gaps such as "does the limit include pending transfers?" instead of inventing an answer.

Real-world example

A tester asked Claude to "summarise this bug". It returned a friendly paragraph. She changed the prompt to "Rewrite this bug report in our format: Title (under 80 characters, starting with the component), Steps to reproduce (numbered), Expected, Actual, Environment, Severity with one-line justification." Every report after that dropped straight into Jira without editing.

44.4Give context and explain why

Telling Claude why a rule exists helps it apply the rule sensibly to cases you didn't think of. Anthropic's example: instead of "NEVER use ellipses", write "Your response will be read aloud by a text-to-speech engine, so never use ellipses since the text-to-speech engine will not know how to pronounce them." The guide adds: "Claude can generalize from the explanation."

Bare ruleRule with a reasonWhat Claude can now infer
"Keep it short.""Keep it short: this goes in a Slack message to the release manager, who reads it on her phone."Lead with the decision, avoid tables, no long code blocks
"Don't use real names.""Don't use real names: this test data will be shared with an offshore vendor and must contain no personal data."Also avoid real addresses, real card numbers and real email domains
"Use Gherkin.""Use Gherkin: these scenarios will be automated with our Cucumber step library."Reuse consistent step wording; keep one action per step

Good context usually covers: who the audience is, what the output will be used for, what "good" looks like on your team, and what Claude can't know (your product rules, your environments, your naming conventions).

Context is not a dumping ground

More context isn't always better. Irrelevant material competes for attention and costs usage. A 200-page specification when you only need section 4 makes the answer worse and slower (Chapter 20 on context rot). Give what's relevant, and say which part matters.

44.5Shape the output

If you care what the answer looks like, say so. Anthropic's guide gives several tips for controlling format:

  • Say what to do, not what not to do. Instead of "Do not use markdown", try "Your response should be composed of smoothly flowing prose paragraphs."
  • Match your prompt's style to the output you want. A prompt full of bullet points and headings tends to get one back; plain prose tends to get prose.
  • Be specific. Name the columns, the length, the headings, the order, the tone.
VagueSpecific
"Make it brief.""Maximum five bullet points, each under 20 words."
"Give me a summary of the test run.""Start with one line: GO or NO-GO. Then a table of failures with columns Test, Error, Likely cause. Then at most three recommended actions."
"Don't be so formal.""Write as a friendly colleague would in a team chat: short sentences, no headings."
"Output it as data.""Output CSV with the header row id,title,priority and nothing before or after it."

For data you will process with code, ask for a precise format such as CSV or JSON, and still check it. In the apps a model can occasionally add a sentence before the data; in the API you can enforce a schema with structured outputs (Chapter 45, Chapter 89).

44.6Show an example

Anthropic calls examples "one of the most reliable ways to steer Claude's output format, tone, and structure." One example of a finished bug report, test case or release note in your team's style often does more than a paragraph of description.

Prompt with one example
Write test cases for the story below in exactly the same style as this example.

<example>
TC-014 | Login locks after 5 failed attempts | High
Given a registered user "test.user01"
When they enter a wrong password 5 times in a row
Then the account is locked
And the message "Your account is locked. Reset your password." is shown
</example>

Story: ...paste the story here...

Two cautions. First, Claude copies examples closely, including their quirks: if your only example is about login, you may get login-flavoured wording everywhere. Second, wrap examples in tags (as above) so Claude can tell the example from the instruction. Choosing and designing several examples (few-shot prompting) is covered in Chapter 45.

44.7Use XML tags to separate the parts

When a prompt mixes instructions with material (a story, a log, a document), Claude needs to know which is which. XML tags are a simple way to label each part: you wrap text in a pair of made-up labels such as <story> and </story>. There's nothing special about the names; they just need to be clear and consistent. Anthropic's guide says tags "help Claude parse complex prompts unambiguously, especially when your prompt mixes instructions, context, examples, and variable inputs."

Prompt structured with XML tags
<context>
We test a UK online banking web app. Releases go out every two weeks.
</context>

<story>
As a customer I want to transfer money between my own accounts...
AC1: Transfers up to £10,000 per day are allowed.
AC2: The source account cannot go overdrawn.
</story>

<existing_tests>
TC-001 Transfer £50 between current and savings
TC-002 Transfer with insufficient funds
</existing_tests>

<instructions>
1. Compare the story with the existing tests.
2. List acceptance criteria that no existing test covers.
3. Write new test cases only for those gaps, in the same style.
</instructions>

Tags help in four ways: Claude can't confuse your log file with your instructions; you can refer to parts by name ("using only the tests in <existing_tests>"); you can swap the material and reuse the rest of the prompt; and you can ask for answers inside tags (for example <gaps> and <new_tests>) so they are easy to pick out.

Under the hood: why tags and ordering help

A model reads your prompt as one long stream of tokens. Nothing in the stream says "this bit is data" or "this bit is a command" unless the text itself signals it. Tags are an unambiguous signal, and Claude models have been trained on a great deal of structured text and are tuned to respect this kind of structure. They also give attention (Chapter 17) clear landmarks to find relevant parts again in a long prompt.

Putting the question at the end, after long material, means the instruction sits closest to where the answer begins. This doesn't make Claude immune to lost in the middle effects, but it helps, and asking Claude to first quote the relevant passages ("ground responses in quotes", in Anthropic's guide) helps further on long documents. Note that tags don't make untrusted text safe: they make the boundary clearer, not unbreakable (Chapter 45, Chapter 131).

44.8Iterate like a tester

Your first prompt is a first draft. Treat a poor answer as a failing test: work out why it failed, change one thing, and run it again.

Two habits make iteration faster. First, edit your original message rather than adding corrections on top: the bad answer then disappears from the context (Chapter 43). Second, when a prompt works well, save it in a shared document or a project's instructions so the team can reuse it. In Chapter 45 you'll turn good prompts into templates.

Opinion · many practitioners, including Anthropic's prompting guide

A prompt you haven't tested against a few real inputs isn't finished. Anthropic's guide assumes you already have "a clear definition of the success criteria" and "some ways to empirically test against those criteria" before you tune a prompt. For one-off chats that is overkill; for a prompt your team will reuse every sprint, it's worth doing (Chapter 125 on evals).

Ask Claude to ask you

When you're not sure what context to give, end with: "Before you start, ask me any questions you need answered to do this well." Anthropic's getting-started tutorial suggests the same. It turns Claude into the colleague who checks the brief before starting.

44.9A prompt checklist

Use this before sending any prompt that matters. Most everyday prompts need only the first three lines.

#CheckAsk yourself
1TaskDoes the first sentence say exactly what I want done?
2InputHave I pasted or attached the real material, not described it?
3FormatHave I said what the answer should look like?
4ContextWould a new colleague know who this is for and why?
5ConstraintsHave I stated scope, limits and what to do when unsure?
6Positive wordingHave I said what to do rather than only what not to do?
7StructureIf there's more than one kind of content, are the parts tagged?
8ExampleWould one example make the style unmistakable?
9SafetyHave I removed secrets, passwords and personal data?
10CheckHow will I verify the answer before I use it?
A good prompt doesn't guarantee a correct answer

Even with an excellent prompt, Claude can be wrong or hallucinate details. Prompting reduces the chance; it doesn't remove it. You remain responsible for checking test cases against the real requirement, and code against the real system.

Troubleshooting: answers look very similar between steps 1 and 3: check that you started a new chat, so the first answer isn't in the context. Table is cut off: ask "continue the table from the last row". Claude still invents rules: move the uncertainty instruction higher and give the reason ("these tests will be signed off as matching the requirement").

Common misunderstandings
"There are magic words that make Claude better."
Clarity, context and examples matter far more than any phrase. Anthropic's guidance is about clear communication, not secret keywords.
"Shorter prompts are always better."
Shorter is better only when nothing important is missing. A precise 15-line prompt often saves five rounds of correction.
"Writing in capitals and saying NEVER makes rules stick."
Explaining why usually works better than shouting, and current models can over-apply forceful wording.
"XML tags must use special names Claude knows."
Any clear, consistent names work. The point is to label the parts.
"If the prompt is good, I don't need to check the answer."
Good prompts reduce errors; they don't remove them. Always review.

44.10Review

Key takeaways
  • Claude only knows what is in the conversation. Treat it as a brilliant new colleague with no background on your project.
  • The building blocks: task, context, input, constraints, format and examples.
  • Be clear and direct; explain why rules exist; say what to do rather than what not to do.
  • Put long material first and the question last; use XML tags to separate instructions from material.
  • One good example often beats a paragraph of description.
  • Iterate like a tester: diagnose, change one thing, re-run, and save prompts that work.
Check yourself

What is Anthropic's "colleague test" for a prompt?

If a person with minimal context would be confused, Claude will be too.

Which rewrite follows the "say what to do" advice?

Positive instructions describe the target, so Claude knows what to aim for, not just what to avoid.

You're pasting a 40-page specification and asking one question about it. Where should the question go?

Anthropic recommends long material at the top and the query at the end, which improved quality in its tests.

Claude invents a business rule that isn't in the story. What's the best prompt fix?

An explicit instruction for uncertainty, with a reason, stops gap-filling far more reliably.

Why wrap a log file in <log> tags?

Tags label parts of the prompt; they add a few tokens rather than saving any.

Assignment

Choose three prompts you or your team actually used in the last month (for example: test cases, bug report rewrites, log analysis). For each, score it against the ten-point checklist, rewrite it, and run both versions on the same real input. Record in a short table: original score, new score, what changed in the answer, and how many follow-up messages each version needed. Share the best rewritten prompt with your team.

Teach it

"Claude is like a brilliant new starter who knows nothing about our project. If you just say 'write tests for login', you get generic tests. Tell it what to do, give it the real story, say who it's for and why, show the format with one example, and tell it to ask rather than guess when something's unclear. Put long material first and your question last, and label the parts with simple tags. Then check the answer, and if it's wrong, fix the prompt like you'd fix a failing test."
