Not legal advice

This chapter explains laws and standards so that testers, analysts and managers can understand them and work well with their legal and compliance colleagues. It is not legal advice. Laws change, their interpretation depends on facts, and penalties are serious. For any real decision, such as whether a system is "high-risk" or whether a transfer is lawful, ask your legal or data protection team. Dates and statuses were checked on 9 November 2026; check the official sources again before relying on them.

AI is now governed by a mix of binding laws and voluntary standards. The EU AI Act regulates AI systems by how risky their use is. Data protection laws (the EU's GDPR and India's DPDP Act) govern the personal data that AI systems are trained on and process. Frameworks like the NIST AI RMF and the certifiable ISO/IEC 42001 standard describe how an organisation should manage AI risk. They share one practical demand: show your work. Testing, evaluation, records and human oversight are exactly what these regimes ask for, so a QA team that keeps good evidence is already doing much of the compliance work.

133.1Laws, standards and frameworks

These words get mixed up, so separate them first.

TypeBinding?Who makes itExamples in this chapter

StandardVoluntary unless a law or contract requires it; some can be certified by auditorsStandards bodies (ISO, IEC, national bodies)ISO/IEC 42001 (AI management system)

In everyday life

Think of road safety. The law says you must wear a seat belt and stay under the speed limit. A standard says how a seat belt must be built and tested. A framework is the driving school's guide to driving safely. You need to obey the first, your car maker follows the second, and the third makes you a better driver. AI governance works the same way.

Why should an Indian QA team care about an EU law? Because laws follow the people and markets, not the office address. The EU AI Act applies to providers outside the EU whose AI systems are placed on the EU market or whose outputs are used in the EU. GDPR applies to organisations outside the EU that offer goods or services to people in the EU or monitor their behaviour there. India's DPDP Act similarly reaches processing outside India connected with offering goods or services to people in India. If your product has EU users, or your company is a vendor to an EU client, expect these requirements in contracts and test plans.

133.2The EU AI Act: the risk-based idea

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the world's first broad AI law. Its core idea is that rules should match risk: the more harm a use of AI could do to people's health, safety or fundamental rights, the stricter the rules.

TierWhat it coversWhat the law requires
Unacceptable risk (prohibited)Practices such as social scoring, manipulative techniques that cause significant harm, exploiting vulnerabilities, untargeted scraping of facial images to build recognition databases, emotion recognition in workplaces and schools (with exceptions), and certain uses of real-time remote biometric identification by police. The 2026 amendments add AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery or child sexual abuse material.Banned
High-riskAnnex III uses such as recruitment and worker management, credit scoring, education and exams, access to essential services, critical infrastructure, law enforcement, migration and justice. Annex I covers AI that is a safety component of products already regulated in the EU (medical devices, machinery, toys, vehicles and others).Risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency to deployers, human oversight, accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, a quality management system, conformity assessment and registration
Transparency riskChatbots and other systems that interact with people; systems generating synthetic audio, images, video or text; deepfakes; emotion recognition and biometric categorisationTell people they're dealing with AI; mark AI-generated content in a machine-readable way; label deepfakes (Article 50)
Minimal riskMost AI: spam filters, test-generation tools, code assistants, game AINo new obligations, beyond AI literacy measures

Separately, the Act regulates general-purpose AI (GPAI) models, such as the large language models behind Claude. Their providers must keep technical documentation, give information to companies building on them, have a copyright policy and publish a summary of training content. Models judged to carry "systemic risk" (the Act presumes this above a training-compute threshold of 1025 floating-point operations) face extra duties: model evaluations including adversarial testing, serious-incident reporting and cybersecurity. The Commission published a voluntary General-Purpose AI Code of Practice in July 2025 as one way to show compliance.

Roles decide your duties

Provider

Develops an AI system or model (or has it developed) and places it on the market under its own name. Carries most obligations. A company that builds a CV-screening product on top of Claude is the provider of that product.

Deployer

Uses an AI system in its own work. Lighter, but real, duties: use as instructed, assign human oversight, keep logs, inform affected workers, and for some deployers carry out a fundamental-rights impact assessment. A bank using a bought-in credit-scoring tool is a deployer.

Real-world example

Your company uses Claude Code to write Playwright tests. That's minimal risk: no new obligations beyond AI literacy. The same company sells HR software with an AI feature that ranks job applicants for EU clients. That's high-risk under Annex III, and your company is its provider. The testing team will be asked for evidence of accuracy, robustness, bias checks and logging, which are exactly the things QA can produce.

133.3The EU AI Act timeline, after the Digital Omnibus

The Act applies in stages. In 2026 the EU passed an amending regulation, the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026), mainly because the technical standards needed for high-risk compliance weren't ready. It moved the high-risk dates back. Many older articles and slides show the original dates, so check which version you're reading.

  1. 1 Aug 2024Entry into force. The Act becomes law; obligations start later.
  2. 2 Feb 2025Prohibitions and AI literacy begin to apply, with general provisions and definitions.
  3. 2 Aug 2025General-purpose AI model rules apply to providers; EU governance bodies and national authorities are set up; penalty rules adopted.
  4. 2 Aug 2026Most rules apply and enforcement starts, including Article 50 transparency duties and the Commission's enforcement powers over GPAI models.
  5. 2 Dec 2026Omnibus additions: the new prohibitions (non-consensual intimate imagery, child sexual abuse material) apply; deadline for providers of generative systems already on the market before 2 August 2026 to meet the content-marking duty in Article 50(2).
  6. 2 Dec 2027Annex III high-risk rules apply (moved by the Omnibus from 2 August 2026).
  7. 2 Aug 2028Annex I high-risk rules apply to AI in regulated products (moved from 2 August 2027).

The Omnibus also rewrote the AI literacy duty (Article 4): providers and deployers must take measures to support their staff's AI literacy, but aren't required to guarantee any particular level for each person. It added simplifications for small and medium-sized companies, such as simpler technical documentation and quality management.

Under the hood: what high-risk requirements look like to a tester

The high-risk chapter of the Act reads like a test strategy. Article 9 requires a risk management system running across the whole lifecycle, including testing "against prior defined metrics and probabilistic thresholds". Article 10 asks for training, validation and test data that are relevant, sufficiently representative and, as far as possible, free of errors, with examination for possible biases. Article 12 requires automatic logging of events. Article 14 requires designs that let humans oversee, interpret and override the system. Article 15 requires appropriate accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, declared accuracy metrics, resilience to errors and to attempts by third parties to exploit vulnerabilities, naming data poisoning, adversarial examples and model flaws. In QA terms: a documented eval suite, bias testing, log verification, oversight and override tests, robustness and security tests including prompt injection (Chapter 131), and traceable results.

133.4GDPR as applied to AI

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, Regulation (EU) 2016/679) has applied since 25 May 2018. It isn't an AI law, but most AI systems touch personal data, so it applies to many of them: when personal data is used for training, when users type personal data into prompts, and when a system makes decisions about people.

GDPR principle or articleWhat it means for an AI feature
Lawful basis (Art. 6)Every use of personal data needs a legal basis, such as consent, contract or legitimate interests. Training a model on customer data needs its own justification.
Purpose limitation and data minimisation (Art. 5)Use data only for the purposes stated; send the model only what it needs (Chapter 132).
Transparency and rights (Arts. 12–22)Tell people how AI uses their data; support access, correction and erasure requests, which is hard if data was used to train a model.
Automated decisions (Art. 22)People have the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that significantly affect them (such as a loan refusal), except in limited cases with safeguards including human intervention.
Data protection impact assessment (Art. 35)A DPIA is required where processing is likely to result in a high risk to people. New technologies such as AI are a common trigger.
Security and breach notification (Arts. 32–34)Appropriate security; notify the supervisory authority of a personal data breach within 72 hours where feasible.
International transfers (Chapter V)Transfers of EU personal data to countries such as India need a legal mechanism, typically standard contractual clauses.

Maximum fines reach €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. In December 2024 the European Data Protection Board adopted Opinion 28/2024 on AI models, covering when a model trained on personal data can be considered anonymous, how legitimate interests can apply to AI development, and what happens when a model was trained on unlawfully processed data.

GDPR changes are under discussion

The European Commission proposed targeted GDPR changes in November 2025 as part of a wider "Digital Omnibus" package, separate from the AI Omnibus above. As of early November 2026 the Council had agreed its negotiating position but the changes had not been adopted. Until they are, GDPR applies as written.

133.5India: the DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 was enacted on 11 August 2023. It applies to digital personal data (and paper data later digitised) processed in India, and to processing outside India connected with offering goods or services to people in India. The DPDP Rules, 2025, which make the Act work in practice, are dated 13 November 2025 in the Gazette (G.S.R. 846(E)); the government's explainer says they were notified on 14 November 2025.

When it applies

  1. 11 Aug 2023DPDP Act enacted.
  2. 3 Jan 2025Draft Rules published for consultation (6,915 inputs received, according to the government).
  3. 13–14 Nov 2025Rules notified. Rules 1, 2 and 17–21 (the Board and procedures) apply on publication.
  4. Nov 2026One year after publication: Rule 4 (Consent Manager registration and duties) comes into force.
  5. May 2027Eighteen months after publication: Rules 3, 5–16, 22 and 23 apply. These are the core duties: notice, security safeguards, breach intimation, retention and erasure, children's data, Significant Data Fiduciary duties and rights handling. Most commentators give 13 May 2027.
Status to check

In early 2026 the press reported that MeitY was considering shortening the 18-month window, at least for Significant Data Fiduciaries. I could not find a gazette notification making that change as of 9 November 2026. Check meity.gov.in for amendments before planning around these dates.

Two more points matter for AI. First, the Act does not apply to personal data that the person has made publicly available themselves, or that someone was legally required to publish. That's relevant to web-scraped training data, though lawyers debate its exact limits. Second, the Act doesn't ban transfers abroad in general: section 16 lets the government restrict transfers to countries it notifies.

Under the hood: India's wider AI rules

India has no single AI law. Two other instruments matter. The India AI Governance Guidelines, released by MeitY in November 2025 under the IndiaAI Mission, are non-binding principles that rely on existing laws and sector regulators for enforcement. Separately, the government amended the IT (Intermediary Guidelines) Rules in February 2026 to cover "synthetically generated information": platforms must label AI-generated audio, images and video that look real. Reports describe it as notified on 10 February 2026 and in force from 20 February 2026; further changes were proposed in April 2026. If you test a platform that hosts user content, labelling checks belong in your test plan. Sector regulators, such as the RBI for banking, may add their own rules.

133.6NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology released the AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, document NIST AI 100-1) on 26 January 2023. It's voluntary and free, and it's widely used worldwide as a common language for AI risk. It is organised into four functions:

Governculture, policies, roles and accountability; runs through everything
Mapunderstand the context: purpose, users, impacts, risks
Measureassess, analyse and track risks with tests and metrics
Manageprioritise and act on risks; respond and recover

The framework describes trustworthy AI as valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed. "Measure" is where QA lives: it calls for test, evaluation, verification and validation (TEVV) throughout the lifecycle.

On 26 July 2024 NIST added the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1). It lists twelve risks that are new or made worse by generative AI: CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear) information or capabilities; confabulation (what this book calls hallucination); dangerous, violent or hateful content; data privacy; environmental impacts; harmful bias and homogenisation; human-AI configuration; information integrity; information security; intellectual property; obscene, degrading or abusive content; and value chain and component integration. For each, it suggests actions mapped to the four functions.

Status

NIST's page says the AI RMF 1.0 "is being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan". No revised version had been published as of 9 November 2026. In April 2026 NIST also released a concept note for an AI RMF profile on critical infrastructure.

133.7ISO/IEC 42001: an AI management system

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (published December 2023) specifies requirements for an AI management system (AIMS): the policies, roles, processes and controls an organisation uses to develop, provide or use AI responsibly. It follows the same structure as other ISO management system standards, such as ISO/IEC 27001 for information security, so organisations already certified to 27001 find it familiar.

What it asks for

An AI policy and objectives; defined roles; AI risk assessment and AI system impact assessment; controls (in its Annex A) covering data, lifecycle, third parties and use; monitoring, internal audit and management review; continual improvement (the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle).

Why companies want it

Unlike the NIST framework, it can be certified by an accredited auditor, which customers and procurement teams can ask for. Anthropic announced accredited ISO/IEC 42001 certification for its AI management system on 13 January 2025.

The two fit together. Many organisations use NIST's AI RMF to decide what risk activities to do, ISO/IEC 42001 to run and certify them as a management system, and both as evidence towards EU AI Act and data protection duties. Neither is a legal safe harbour by itself.

Real-world example

A Bengaluru team builds a loan-eligibility assistant for an EU lender. Legal classifies it as high-risk under Annex III (credit scoring), with the lender as deployer and the team's company as provider. The QA lead's test strategy gains five new sections: an eval suite with declared accuracy targets; fairness tests across age and gender groups; adversarial tests, including prompt injection through uploaded documents; human-override tests; and log verification. Every run is stored with the model version and prompt version. When the auditor arrives, most of the evidence already exists, because QA designed for it.

Opinion · views differ

Supporters of the EU AI Act, including many civil-society groups, argue that firm rules build trust and that the 2026 delays weaken protection. Many industry groups argued the opposite: that the rules were too complex and the standards weren't ready, and they welcomed the Omnibus. In India, the government has so far chosen guidelines plus existing laws over a dedicated AI statute; some commentators expect binding AI rules later, while others prefer the lighter approach. These are policy debates, not settled facts.

Common misunderstandings
"The EU AI Act doesn't apply to us; we're in India."
It can, if your AI system is placed on the EU market or its outputs are used in the EU. The same goes for GDPR if you offer services to people in the EU.
"All high-risk rules applied from August 2026."
The 2026 Digital Omnibus moved Annex III high-risk rules to 2 December 2027 and Annex I to 2 August 2028. Transparency and GPAI rules were not delayed.
"Using ChatGPT or Claude at work makes us a high-risk AI provider."
Risk depends on the use. Writing tests is minimal risk; ranking job candidates is high-risk.
"The DPDP Act is fully in force."
The Board and procedures are; Consent Manager rules follow after one year; the core duties apply eighteen months after the Rules' publication (around May 2027), unless an amendment changes that.
"ISO 42001 certification means we comply with the AI Act."
It's strong evidence of good management, not legal compliance on its own. The AI Act has its own specific obligations.

133.9Review

Key takeaways
  • Laws bind; standards and frameworks guide, and can be certified (ISO/IEC 42001) or used as a common language (NIST AI RMF).
  • The EU AI Act sorts AI uses into prohibited, high-risk, transparency and minimal tiers, plus rules for general-purpose AI models. Your role (provider or deployer) sets your duties.
  • After the 2026 Digital Omnibus, Annex III high-risk rules apply from 2 December 2027 and Annex I from 2 August 2028.
  • GDPR applies to AI that processes personal data of people in the EU, including from India; watch Article 22 automated decisions and DPIAs.
  • India's DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, with core duties due eighteen months later; penalties reach ₹250 crore.
  • For QA: registers, evals, bias and robustness tests, oversight tests, logs and traceable evidence serve every regime at once. And always: not legal advice; ask your legal team.
Check yourself

Under the EU AI Act as amended in 2026, from when do the high-risk rules for Annex III uses (such as recruitment) apply?

The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) moved them from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027.

An Indian company's AI tool summarises bug reports for its own testers. Under the EU AI Act, this is most likely:

Internal productivity uses aren't in the high-risk annexes. If the reports contain personal data, GDPR or the DPDP Act may still apply.

Under India's DPDP Rules, how soon must a Data Fiduciary send the Board its detailed breach report?

Rule 7 requires an immediate intimation without delay, then detailed information within 72 hours. The 90-day period is for responding to people's rights requests.

What are the four functions of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework?

Plan-Do-Check-Act is the management-system cycle behind ISO standards; Identify, Protect, Detect and Respond come from NIST's cybersecurity framework.

What is the main difference between ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIST AI RMF?

Both are voluntary, and both cover AI risk. ISO/IEC 42001 can be audited and certified; the NIST framework is guidance.

Assignment

Take the register from the lab and turn the riskiest row into a one-page "regulatory test annex" for your test strategy: (1) which regimes apply and why (EU AI Act tier and role, GDPR and/or DPDP); (2) the requirements that translate into tests, using the table in 132.8; (3) at least eight test cases, with the evidence each will produce; (4) where that evidence will be stored, and for how long. Mark the document "draft, not legal advice" and review it with your legal or data protection contact.

Teach it

"There are laws, like the EU AI Act, GDPR and India's DPDP Act, and there are voluntary standards, like NIST's AI framework and ISO 42001. The AI Act sorts AI by how risky its use is: writing tests is low risk, while ranking job applicants is high-risk and needs proof of testing, bias checks, logs and human oversight. The data protection laws apply whenever personal data is involved, even from India if we serve EU or Indian users. Dates keep moving: the EU pushed high-risk rules to late 2027, and India's core DPDP duties land around May 2027. For us in QA, the message is simple: good evidence is good compliance. And none of this is legal advice, so talk to legal before deciding."
