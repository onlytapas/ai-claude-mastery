AI in late 2026 is powerful and widely used, but uneven. It writes, codes, analyses and increasingly carries out multi-step tasks, yet it still makes confident mistakes and needs human oversight. Its strengths and weaknesses don't line up with what feels "easy" or "hard" to people, and its reliability is harder to measure than a single benchmark score suggests. Where it goes next is genuinely uncertain: some experts expect transformative change within a few years, others expect slower, steadier progress. This chapter separates what is known from what is predicted, so you can think clearly about both.

The jagged frontier

A useful idea for thinking about today's AI is the jagged frontier. Picture the edge of what AI can do as a coastline, not a straight line. Some tasks that feel hard to people sit well inside it (summarising a 200-page document in seconds). Some tasks that feel easy sit just outside it (counting exactly how many rows in a screenshot are red, or quoting the exact name of a field in an API it has only half seen).

The term comes from a 2023 field experiment by researchers from Harvard Business School and other universities, run with 758 consultants at Boston Consulting Group using GPT-4. Consultants given AI did markedly better on tasks inside the frontier. On a task deliberately chosen to sit just outside it, those using AI were more likely to reach a wrong answer than those working alone, because the AI's answer looked convincing.

In everyday life

Think of a brilliant new colleague who learned everything from books. They can explain tax law, draft a contract and write a sorting algorithm, but they've never used your company's build system and don't know that "the staging box" means one particular server. Their gaps don't follow the pattern you'd expect from a human junior. You only learn where the gaps are by working with them, and checking.

8.2Where it still falls short

  • Reliability. Models still hallucinate: they state wrong things confidently, especially exact facts, recent events and niche topics (Chapter 22).
  • Long tasks. Agents can now work for long stretches, but errors can compound over many steps, and they need checkpoints and review (Chapter 107, Chapter 113).
  • Judgement and accountability. AI doesn't carry responsibility for decisions. People do. "The AI said it was fine" is not an acceptable release sign-off.
  • Learning on the job. Models don't permanently learn from your corrections; memory features and instruction files work around this (Chapter 20, Chapter 77).
  • Agreeing too easily. Models can lean towards telling you what you seem to want to hear, called sycophancy. Ask "is this test plan complete?" and you may get praise rather than gaps.
  • The physical world. Robotics is advancing but far behind language AI in everyday reliability.
  • Security. AI systems that read untrusted content can be manipulated by hidden instructions (prompt injection, Chapter 131). Agents with tools widen the attack surface (Chapter 130, Chapter 134).

A good mental model for today's AI at work: a very fast, very knowledgeable new colleague who never gets tired, sometimes misremembers facts with total confidence, and needs their work reviewed. You'd give that colleague lots of work, but you'd check anything that matters before it goes to a customer.

Watch out

The most dangerous failures are the plausible ones. A test that doesn't compile gets noticed. A test that compiles, runs green and asserts the wrong thing can sit in your suite for months. When AI writes tests, review what each test checks, not just whether it passes (Chapter 121).

8.3Under the hood: why reliability is hard to measure

"How reliable is it?" sounds like a simple question. For a calculator, you could test a million sums and give a number. For a language model, there are at least six reasons the answer is slippery. Knowing them helps you read headlines, vendor claims and your own team's results with a tester's eye. (This section is about understanding; Chapter 125 teaches you to build the measurements.)

  1. The same question gets different answers. Models are non-deterministic: ask twice, get two wordings, sometimes two conclusions. One successful run proves little. You need to run each case several times and report a pass rate.
  2. There is often no single right answer. Many summaries of a bug report are acceptable. So you can't compare against one expected string; you need graders (rules, a human, or another AI acting as judge), and every grader has its own error rate.
  3. Small step errors compound. An agent that is right 99% of the time per step and takes 100 steps finishes cleanly only about 37% of the time (0.99100). That is compounding error, and it is why long tasks are much harder than short ones.
  4. Tests leak and wear out. Public benchmarks can end up in training data (contamination), and once every model scores near 100% they stop telling models apart (saturation). In February 2026 OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified, a well-known coding benchmark, citing contamination and flawed tasks (Chapter 24).
  5. Your work isn't the benchmark. A model that scores well on public puzzles may do worse on your company's legacy code and jargon. Testers call this distribution shift: when the data in real use differs from the data you measured on, the old score stops predicting.
  6. Rare, severe errors need huge samples. If a chatbot gives dangerous advice once in 1,000 conversations, a 50-case test will almost certainly miss it. The rule of three makes this concrete: zero failures in 300 runs only tells you the true failure rate is probably below 1% (3 ÷ 300).

On top of all that, the target moves. Providers release new model versions every few months, and a change to your prompt or tools can shift results as much as a new model. Reliability is a measurement you repeat, not a fact you look up once.

"At least once" versus "every time": pass@k and pass^k

Reliability numbers depend heavily on what counts as success. Two measures are common in research on code and agents:

  • pass@k: give the model k tries; it succeeds if at least one works. This suits a developer who will look at several drafts and pick the good one.
  • pass^k (read "pass to the k"): it succeeds only if all k tries work. This suits an agent that runs unattended every night: you need it to work every time.

The gap between them is large. Take an illustrative task the model gets right 80% of the time, with independent tries. pass@3 = 1 − 0.23 = 99.2%. pass^3 = 0.83 = 51.2%. The same model sounds nearly perfect or no better than a coin toss, depending on which number you quote.

So when you read a reliability claim, ask: how many tries? Did it count "any success" or "every success"? Who graded it? On whose tasks? Chapter 24 and Chapter 33 show how to read model cards and benchmarks with these questions in mind, and Chapter 140 covers METR's "time horizon" research, which measures how long a task agents can complete about half the time.

8.6Three views about the future

Serious, well-informed people disagree. Here are three broad positions, presented fairly. None is established fact.

Opinion · "Rapid transformation"

Some AI company leaders and researchers argue that systems able to do most knowledge work, and to speed up science dramatically, could arrive within a few years, because capabilities keep rising with scale and new techniques like reasoning and agents. Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei, for example, has written about AI potentially compressing decades of scientific progress into years ("Machines of Loving Grace", October 2024). People holding this view often stress both the huge benefits and the need for serious safety work.

Opinion · "Steady, uneven progress"

Many researchers and economists expect AI to keep improving but to be absorbed more slowly. They point to reliability problems, the cost of changing how organisations work, regulation and physical-world limits. In this view, AI resembles earlier general-purpose technologies such as electricity or the internet: transformative, but over decades.

Opinion · "Current methods will hit limits"

Some prominent researchers argue that today's language models lack key ingredients for human-like intelligence, such as grounded understanding of the physical world or persistent learning, and that new ideas will be needed. They expect progress from current approaches to slow down. Benchmarks like ARC-AGI-3, where people still far outscore machines, are often cited in support.

A safety-focused thread runs across these views: if very capable AI does arrive, ensuring it behaves as intended matters enormously. This concern is why Anthropic was founded (Chapter 37) and why governments set up AI safety institutes from 2023 onward.

How to judge a prediction

Treat a prediction the way a tester treats a claim in a release note. Ask: what's the evidence? Who's making it, and what do they gain if people believe it? What would prove it wrong? Has this person's past prediction record been good? Remember the 1950s and 1960s (Chapter 3, Chapter 4): brilliant people can be right about direction and badly wrong about timing.

Opinion · this book's authors

For a QA team, the most useful stance is to ignore the argument about dates and watch the trend in your own measurements. If the tasks AI handles reliably in your eval set keep growing every quarter, plan for more of that; if they stall, plan for that instead. Your data beats anyone's forecast for your work.

8.7Directions to watch

These are areas of active development in 2026. Their pace is uncertain; their existence isn't.

  • Agents that work for longer, across more tools, with better self-checking (Part XIII). Research on how long a task agents can complete is covered in Chapter 140.
  • Reasoning: models spending more thinking time on hard problems (Chapter 19).
  • Interpretability: research into what's happening inside models, so they can be understood and trusted (Chapter 38).
  • Smaller, cheaper models that run on phones and laptops (Chapter 137).
  • Robotics using the same model techniques for physical tasks.
  • AI security: defences against prompt injection and safer ways to give agents tools (Chapter 134).
  • Regulation and standards taking effect in many countries, including the EU AI Act's phased obligations and management standards such as ISO/IEC 42001 (Chapter 133).

8.8Staying current

Half of what's "new" in AI is out of date within a year. These habits keep you current without drowning:

  1. Follow primary sources. Official announcements and documentation from the companies (Anthropic's news page and docs; equivalents for others) beat headlines.
  2. Read release notes for tools you use. Claude Code shows what's new after it updates.
  3. Test, don't trust. When a new model is announced, try it on three tasks from your real work. Better still, keep a small eval set and rerun it (the lab below gets you started).
  4. Separate the layers. Fundamentals (Parts I–II) change slowly. Products (Parts V–X) change monthly. Learn the first deeply and look up the second.
  5. Sort what you read. Is it an established result, a published finding, a vendor claim or a prediction? Weigh each differently (Chapter 140).
  6. Revisit this book's dates. Every chapter shows when its facts were last verified.

    reliability.py
    # reliability.py - turn repeated runs into honest reliability numbers.
# Usage: python3 reliability.py results.csv
import csv
import sys

path = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "results.csv"
with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    rows = list(csv.DictReader(f))

total_runs = total_pass = 0
print(f"{'task':40} {'rate':>5} {'any ok':>7} {'all ok':>7}")
for row in rows:
    runs = [v.strip().lower() for k, v in row.items() if k.startswith("run") and v]
    passes = sum(1 for v in runs if v == "pass")
    total_runs += len(runs)
    total_pass += passes
    rate = passes / len(runs)
    any_ok = "yes" if passes > 0 else "no"           # what pass@k rewards
    all_ok = "yes" if passes == len(runs) else "no"  # what pass^k demands
    print(f"{row['task'][:40]:40} {rate:5.0%} {any_ok:>7} {all_ok:>7}")

p = total_pass / total_runs
k = 3
print()
print(f"Overall pass rate: {p:.0%} ({total_pass}/{total_runs} runs)")
print(f"If runs were independent at that rate, k={k}:")
print(f"  pass@{k} (at least one of {k} works): {1 - (1 - p) ** k:.0%}")
print(f"  pass^{k} (all {k} work):             {p ** k:.0%}")
fails = total_runs - total_pass
if fails == 0:
    print(f"No failures seen. Rule of three: true failure rate is probably below {3 / total_runs:.0%}.")

Common misunderstandings
"Experts agree AI will soon surpass humans at everything."
They don't. Timelines and even definitions are hotly debated.
"AI progress has stalled."
Also not supported: capabilities kept rising through 2025 and 2026, though improvements in day-to-day reliability are slower than headlines suggest.
"If AI can do a task, the job disappears."
Jobs are bundles of tasks. AI usually changes the mix of tasks, and the effects vary widely between roles.
"If it handled the hard task, it'll handle the easy one."
The frontier is jagged. Difficulty for AI doesn't match difficulty for people, so check each kind of task separately.
"It passed when I tried it, so it works."
One run proves little for a non-deterministic system. Run several times, decide pass criteria first, and count every-time success for unattended work.
"The highest benchmark score means the best model for us."
Benchmarks can be contaminated, saturated or unlike your work. Your own small eval set is a better guide.

8.9Review

Key takeaways
  • Today's AI is strong at language, code, analysis, test design and increasingly multi-step tasks, but still unreliable on exact facts and needs oversight.
  • Its ability is jagged: strong on some hard tasks, weak on some easy-looking ones. You find the edges by testing, not by guessing.
  • Reliability is hard to measure because outputs vary, many answers are valid, errors compound, benchmarks leak and saturate, your work differs from benchmarks, and rare errors need large samples.
  • "At least once" (pass@k) and "every time" (pass^k) can describe the same results very differently; unattended automation needs the second.
  • Views on the future range from rapid transformation to steady progress to hitting limits; treat all as opinions and judge the evidence.
  • Fundamentals change slowly; products change monthly. Learn the first deeply and verify the second.

Assignment

Set up your "stay current" routine: pick two primary sources (for example, Anthropic's news page and the release notes of a tool you use) and one person or publication you trust for analysis. Schedule 30 minutes a week. Keep the five-task results.csv from the lab as your personal benchmark and rerun it whenever a new model or tool version arrives. After four weeks, write down the three most important changes you noticed, whether your own results moved, and how they affect your work.

Teach it

"AI today is excellent at language, code and analysis, and is starting to do multi-step tasks, but its abilities are jagged: it can nail a hard job and fumble an easy-looking one, and it makes confident mistakes. Because answers vary from run to run, you only know how reliable it is by trying the same task several times and counting. Experts genuinely disagree about how fast it will advance, so treat predictions as opinions and check the evidence. Learn the fundamentals deeply; look up product details, because they change every month."
