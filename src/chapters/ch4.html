Before you start: Chapter 3 (symbolic AI and the Perceptron).

Twice, AI was hyped far beyond what it could deliver, and twice the money and interest collapsed. These collapses are called AI winters. In between came a boom in "expert systems": programs built from thousands of rules taken from human experts. They worked impressively in narrow areas but were expensive, brittle and hard to maintain. Understanding why helps you judge today's AI more wisely.

4.1Hitting the wall

By the late 1960s it was clear that early AI programs didn't scale. Three problems stood out:

ProblemPlain meaningExample
Combinatorial explosionThe number of possibilities grows impossibly fast as problems get biggerChecking every possible chess game would take longer than the age of the universe
Common-sense knowledgeReal tasks need vast background knowledge nobody had written downKnowing that a "bank" by a river isn't a place to deposit money
Limited computersMachines had tiny memory and slow processorsSpeech or image tasks were simply out of reach
In everyday life: the combinatorial explosion

Choosing an outfit from 3 shirts and 3 trousers is 9 combinations. Add 3 jackets and 3 pairs of shoes and it's 81. With 10 of each of 10 clothing items, it's 10 billion. Every extra choice multiplies the total. Early AI tried to search through possibilities like this, and real-world problems quickly became impossible to search.

4.2The Perceptrons book (1969)

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published Perceptrons, a careful mathematical study of Rosenblatt's learning machine. They proved that a single-layer perceptron can't learn certain simple patterns. The famous example is "exclusive or" (XOR): output yes if exactly one of two inputs is on, but not both.

Networks with several layers could in principle solve XOR, but nobody yet knew a good way to train them. The book was widely read as showing that neural networks were a dead end, and research into them largely stalled for over a decade. It took until the mid-1980s for a training method for multi-layer networks to become widely known (Chapter 5).

Why a single layer can't learn XOR

A single perceptron draws one straight line to separate "yes" cases from "no" cases. Plot the four XOR cases on a grid: (0,0)→no, (1,1)→no, (0,1)→yes, (1,0)→yes. The two "yes" points sit on one diagonal and the two "no" points on the other. No single straight line can put both yeses on one side and both nos on the other. You need two lines, which means a second layer. This is why "deep" (many-layer) networks matter so much later on.

4.3The first AI winter (mid-1970s)

Funders noticed the gap between promises and results:

  • Machine translation: a 1966 US government report (the ALPAC report) concluded that machine translation was slower, less accurate and more expensive than human translation. Funding was cut sharply.
  • The Lighthill Report (1973): the British mathematician Sir James Lighthill, asked by the UK government to review AI research, concluded it had failed to meet its "grandiose objectives". UK funding was drastically reduced.
  • US defence funding through DARPA shifted towards projects with specific, near-term military uses.

From roughly 1974 to 1980, AI research shrank. Researchers often avoided calling their work "AI" at all.

4.4Expert systems: AI goes to work

AI recovered by narrowing its ambition. Instead of general intelligence, researchers captured the knowledge of human experts in one specialised area and wrote it down as rules. These expert systems became the first commercially successful AI.

ExpertA doctor or engineer explains how they make decisions
Knowledge engineerInterviews them and writes rules

Inference engineApplies the rules to a new case

IF   the infection is in the blood
AND  the organism is gram-negative
AND  the organism is rod-shaped
THEN there is suggestive evidence (0.6) that the organism is E. coli

By the mid-1980s, many large companies had AI departments, and a whole industry sold specialised "LISP machines" to run AI software. Japan launched its ambitious Fifth Generation Computer project in 1982, and other countries responded with their own programmes.

4.5Why expert systems faded

Strengths

  • Worked well in narrow, well-defined areas
  • Could explain their reasoning by showing the rules used
  • Behaved predictably for situations the rules covered

Weaknesses

  • Knowledge bottleneck: getting rules out of experts was slow and costly
  • Brittleness: anything slightly outside the rules caused nonsense
  • Maintenance: thousands of rules interacted unpredictably as they were changed
  • Couldn't learn from new data by themselves
Real-world example: a tester's view

If you've ever maintained a large set of business rules or a huge suite of hard-coded test conditions, you've felt the expert-system problem. Each new rule fixes one case and may break three others. Edge cases nobody anticipated slip through. That's brittleness, and it's exactly why the field turned towards systems that learn from data.

4.6The second AI winter (late 1980s to mid-1990s)

Around 1987 the market for specialised LISP machines collapsed as ordinary desktop computers became powerful enough. Expert systems proved too costly to maintain. Japan's Fifth Generation project ended in the early 1990s without meeting its goals. Funding and commercial interest dropped again. "AI" became an unfashionable label, though useful work continued under other names such as "machine learning", "knowledge systems" and "computational intelligence".

  1. 1966ALPAC report cuts machine-translation funding in the US.
  2. 1969Perceptrons dampens neural-network research.
  3. 1973–80First AI winter, triggered partly by the Lighthill Report.
  4. 1980–87Expert-system boom: XCON, LISP machines, corporate AI groups, Japan's Fifth Generation project (1982).
  5. 1984Cyc, Douglas Lenat's project to hand-encode common-sense knowledge, begins. It continued for decades.
  6. 1987–mid-90sSecond AI winter: the LISP machine market collapses and expert systems lose favour.

4.7Lessons that still apply

  1. Hype has a cost. Overpromising led to collapses that set research back years. Watch for claims that outrun evidence.
  2. Narrow success doesn't mean general success. A system brilliant in a demo can fail outside its comfort zone.
  3. Maintenance is part of the product. A system that's expensive to update will be abandoned.
  4. Explainability is valuable. Expert systems could show their reasoning; modern neural networks mostly can't, which is why research into explaining them (Chapter 38) matters.
  5. Useful work survives winters. Ideas from these years (search, logic, knowledge representation, and neural networks) returned later in better forms.
Opinion · a debate today

Some commentators argue that today's AI boom could be followed by another winter if products fail to justify the huge investment. Others argue this time is different because AI products already earn substantial revenue and are used by hundreds of millions of people. Both views are held by serious people; neither is settled fact.

  2. Give it three real or invented bug descriptions and ask it to apply only those rules, showing which rule fired.a classification for each bug, with the rule that triggered it.
  3. Now describe an unusual bug the rules don't cover (for example, a typo in the legal terms that changes their meaning).the rules either fail or give a poor answer. That's brittleness.
  4. Ask Claude to classify the same bug using its own judgement instead of the rules, and compare.a more sensible answer. You've just seen the difference between rule-based and learned systems.
Common misunderstandings
"The AI winters proved AI was impossible."
They showed that particular approaches and timelines were wrong. The field kept advancing underneath.
"Rule-based systems are obsolete."
They're still widely used where predictability and auditability matter, such as some fraud checks, tax calculations and validation logic, often alongside machine learning.
"Minsky and Papert killed neural networks on purpose."
Their analysis was correct about single-layer networks. The wider reaction to it, and the lack of a training method for deeper networks, caused the stall.

4.8Review

Key takeaways
  • Early AI ran into combinatorial explosion, missing common-sense knowledge and weak computers.
  • Minsky and Papert (1969) showed single-layer perceptrons can't learn some simple patterns, and neural-network research stalled.
  • The first AI winter (about 1974–1980) followed the ALPAC and Lighthill reports.
  • Expert systems (DENDRAL, MYCIN, XCON) brought commercial success in the 1980s using hand-written rules.
  • They were costly to build, brittle and hard to maintain, and a second winter followed from about 1987.
  • The lessons about hype, brittleness, maintenance and explainability still apply to AI products today.
