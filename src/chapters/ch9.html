Before you start: nothing technical. It helps to have read the history in Chapter 1 (what intelligence is), Chapter 5 (machine learning and deep learning) and Chapter 7 (the arrival of ChatGPT and Claude), but this chapter stands on its own.

People use "AI", "machine learning", "deep learning", "generative AI" and "LLM" as if they meant the same thing. They don't. They fit inside each other like nesting dolls: AI is the whole field, machine learning is the part that learns from examples, deep learning is machine learning with many-layered neural networks, generative AI is the part that creates new content, and large language models are generative models for text. A model is the learned thing at the centre: a big set of numbers that turns an input into an output. Building it is training; using it is inference. With this map you can place any product, read any article and ask better questions.

9.1Why you need a map

Open any news site or vendor brochure and you'll see sentences like "Our AI-powered, machine-learning-driven platform uses deep generative models." Most readers nod and move on. But each of those words means something specific, and the differences matter when you have to decide whether to trust, buy, test or build something.

Here's a typical workplace moment. A tool vendor says their test automation product has "AI self-healing". That could mean any of these:

  • A hand-written rule: "if the button ID changed, try the button text instead". No learning at all.
  • A small machine-learning model trained to guess which element on a page is "the same" as before.
  • A call to a large language model such as Claude, asking it to pick the right element.

All three get marketed as "AI". They behave very differently. The first is predictable and cheap. The second needs training data and can drift. The third costs money per call, can give different answers on different runs, and sends your page content to a third party. Knowing the map lets you ask which one it is.

In everyday life

"Vehicle", "car", "electric car" and "Tesla Model 3" are all correct words for the same object, at different levels of detail. If a colleague says "I bought a vehicle", you don't know whether to ask about charging. AI words work the same way: each one is correct at its own level, but only the specific one tells you how the thing behaves.

Real-world example

Your team uses three "AI" things. A rule in Jira automatically sets priority to "Highest" when a ticket contains "payment" and "production". A dashboard predicts which test files a pull request will break, from two years of history. And Claude Code writes Playwright tests. On the map: the first is plain AI in the broadest sense (really just automation with rules), the second is classic machine learning, and the third is an LLM. They need three different kinds of testing: the rule needs ordinary test cases, the predictor needs accuracy measured on fresh data, and the LLM needs evals that cope with varied answers (Chapter 125).

Where the neat picture is a simplification

The nesting-doll picture is the standard teaching map, and it's right in spirit. Real life has some blurry edges worth knowing:

  • AI outside machine learning is still alive. Search algorithms, planners, constraint solvers and rule-based expert systems (Chapter 4) are AI without learning. Modern systems often mix them: a chess program can combine classic search with a learned evaluation.
  • Not all generative models are LLMs. Image generators usually use a different design called a diffusion model. Speech generators and music generators are generative but not language models.
  • Not all language models are generative chatbots. BERT (2018, Chapter 6) is a language model built for understanding text, used for search and classification, not for writing replies.
  • Generative AI could, in principle, exist without deep learning. Older statistical methods generated text too (the n-gram models in Chapter 6). In practice every generative system worth using today is deep learning.
  • "Multimodal" LLMs read images, PDFs and sometimes audio as well as text (Chapter 21). Some people prefer the broader term foundation model for these large, general-purpose models.

9.3What a "model" actually is

"Model" is the most important word on the map, and the most misunderstood. In machine learning, a model is a mathematical function with adjustable numbers inside it. You give it an input; it gives you an output. Training sets those numbers so that the outputs are useful.

InputAn email; a photo; a sentence; a test-run history
ModelA fixed recipe of arithmetic plus millions or billions of learned numbers
Output"Spam: 97%"; "cat"; the next word; "likely to fail"

The learned numbers are called parameters, or weights. A small spam filter might have thousands. A large language model has billions or more. Physically, a model is a set of files: a description of the arithmetic (the "architecture") and the weights. It is not a database of facts and not a program full of if-statements. Nobody wrote its rules; they emerged from training.

In everyday life

Think of a sound-mixing desk with thousands of sliders. The wiring of the desk (which slider affects which channel) is the architecture. The positions of the sliders are the weights. Training is the long process of nudging every slider until the music sounds right. Once the sliders are set, you can copy their positions onto another identical desk and get exactly the same sound. That copy is the model.

Model, product, tool: three different things

A common mix-up is between the model and the products built around it.

When someone says "Claude got this wrong", it's worth asking which layer. Was it the model's reasoning, the instructions the product gave it, a missing file the tool didn't read, or a setting? Testers do this instinctively for normal software. Do it for AI too.

Model names change; the idea doesn't

The Claude models named above are current as of 9 November 2026 (see Chapter 40 for details and how to choose). New versions arrive every few months. The map in this chapter will stay true long after the names change.

9.4Training versus inference

Every model has two lives. First it is built. Then it is used. These are completely separate activities with different costs, timings and risks.

Training: learning

The model sees enormous numbers of examples. After each batch, its weights are nudged so it does a little better. For a large language model this takes weeks or months on thousands of specialised chips and costs a great deal. It happens a few times per model, at the AI company. When it ends, the weights are frozen.

Inference: answering

The frozen model is given a new input and produces an output. This happens every time anyone sends a message. Each single answer is cheap and quick (seconds), but there are millions of them, so total inference cost is large. The weights do not change.

In everyday life

A medical student spends years studying: that's training. A qualified doctor seeing patients is inference. The doctor uses what they learned, but seeing one more patient doesn't rewrite their medical school education. And a doctor who qualified in 2020 won't know about a drug approved last month unless someone tells them.

Three practical consequences

  1. Models have a knowledge cutoff. A model only knows what was in its training data. Anything later must be given to it at inference time: pasted into the chat, found through web search, or fetched by a tool.
  2. Chatting doesn't retrain the model. When you correct Claude in a conversation, its weights don't change. It "remembers" only because the earlier messages are sent back with each new one, inside its context window (Chapter 20). Memory features in apps work the same way: they store notes and feed them back in as context.
  3. Testing targets inference. As a tester you almost always evaluate a frozen model through inference: same inputs, judged outputs. When the vendor releases a new version, the weights are different, so you re-run your checks. That's regression testing for AI.
Fine-tuning: training after training

Training happens in stages. Pre-training teaches a model general patterns from a huge, broad dataset (Chapter 17). Then post-training, including fine-tuning and RLHF, shapes it into a helpful, honest assistant (Chapter 18). Some providers also let customers fine-tune models on their own data. Either way, it's still training: weights change, and it happens before the model answers your questions, not during.

Context is the other lever. Giving a model the right documents at inference time, a technique called RAG (Chapter 23), is usually cheaper, faster to update and easier to check than fine-tuning. Most workplace "teach the AI about our product" needs are solved with context, not training.

Privacy is a separate question

"Chatting doesn't change the weights in real time" is not the same as "my chats are never used for training". Whether a provider may use your conversations to train future models depends on the product, the plan and your settings. Check your organisation's agreement and the provider's current policy before pasting customer data or confidential code. Chapter 98 covers secrets and access.

Opinion · researchers and commentators

Whether LLMs count as "narrow" AI is genuinely contested. Some researchers argue that models which write code, analyse contracts and pass professional exams are already a form of general intelligence, at least in early form. Others argue that they remain pattern-matchers with serious gaps in reliability, planning and learning from experience. This book treats it as an open debate, not a settled fact.

9.7Using the map at work

The map turns vague claims into testable questions. When someone presents an "AI" feature, work inward through the circles:

Does it learn?If not, it's rules. Test it like any other software.
From what data?If yes, ask what it was trained on and how recently.
What kind of model?A small classifier, a vision network, or an LLM?
Where does inference run?On your machine, in your cloud, or on a third-party API? What data leaves?
How is it measured?Accuracy on what test set? How often does it change?
Real-world example: a vendor call

A vendor demos "AI test generation". You ask: "Is this an LLM? Which one, and through which API? Does our application's page content get sent to it? Is it the same model every run, or can it change underneath us? How do you measure the quality of the tests it writes?" Five questions, straight from the map. Their answers tell you more than the slide deck did.

Common misunderstandings
"AI and machine learning are the same thing."
Machine learning is one part of AI. Search, planning and rule-based systems are AI without learning.
"Claude learns from my conversation."
Its weights don't change during a chat. It uses the earlier messages as context. Whether your data might be used to train future models is a separate policy question.
"A model is a database of facts."
It's a set of learned numbers that turn input into output. It can reproduce facts it saw often, but it doesn't look them up, which is one reason it can be confidently wrong (Chapter 22).
"All generative AI is an LLM."
Image, audio and video generators are generative but usually not language models.
"If a product says 'AI', it uses a neural network."
It might be a few if-statements. Ask.

9.8Review

Key takeaways
  • AI ⊃ machine learning ⊃ deep learning ⊃ generative AI ⊃ LLMs. Each inner circle is a special case of the one outside it.
  • A model is a fixed arithmetic recipe plus learned numbers (parameters or weights) that turn input into output.
  • The model is different from the API, the app and the agent tool built around it.
  • Training sets the weights, rarely and expensively. Inference uses the frozen weights, constantly and cheaply per request.
  • Chatting doesn't retrain a model; context does the "remembering". Knowledge stops at the training cutoff unless you supply more.
  • The narrowest accurate circle tells you how a product behaves and how to test it.
Check yourself

Which statement about the map is true?

Deep learning is a subset of machine learning. AI also includes non-learning techniques, and much machine learning uses simpler methods than deep networks.

You correct Claude in a chat and it does better for the rest of the conversation. What happened?

During inference the weights are frozen. Earlier messages are sent back as context, so the model can follow your correction within that conversation.

A visual regression tool compares screenshots pixel by pixel with a 1% tolerance. Where does it sit?

Nothing is learned. It's a useful algorithm, but no model is trained.

What is a model, physically?

The architecture says what arithmetic to do; the weights are the numbers training found. The app is a product built around the model.

Which costs happen every time someone sends a message?

Each message runs the frozen model once (inference). Training is done ahead of time.

Teach it

"Think nesting dolls. AI is the whole field. Machine learning is AI that learns from examples. Deep learning is machine learning with many-layered neural networks.
