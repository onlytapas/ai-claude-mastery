Before you start: nothing. This is the first chapter.

Before we can talk about artificial intelligence, we need to ask a harder question: what is intelligence in the first place? Nobody has a perfect answer. That's why arguments about whether AI "really" thinks never end. In practice, AI means computer systems that do tasks we'd normally say need intelligence, such as understanding language, recognising faces or solving problems. Today's AI is very good at some of these tasks and surprisingly poor at others.

1.1What do we mean by intelligence?

Ask ten people to define intelligence and you'll get ten answers. Most include some of these abilities:

AbilityEveryday example
Learning from experienceAfter touching a hot stove once, a child avoids it
Reasoning and problem-solvingWorking out why your car won't start
Understanding languageGetting the joke, not just the words
Adapting to new situationsFinding your way in a city you've never visited
PlanningOrganising a wedding, or a release schedule
Perceiving the worldRecognising a friend's face in a crowd
Common senseKnowing that if you turn a full cup upside down, the water falls out

Psychologists have argued for over a century about whether intelligence is one general ability or many separate ones. In 1983 the psychologist Howard Gardner proposed "multiple intelligences" (linguistic, logical, musical, spatial, social and others). Whatever the right answer is for people, it matters for AI: a machine can be brilliant at one kind of intelligence and have none of the others.

In everyday life

Think of a calculator. It does arithmetic far faster and more accurately than any person. Is it intelligent? Most people say no, because it can only do that one thing and doesn't understand what the numbers mean. Now think of a modern AI that can write an essay, explain a joke and fix computer code. It's much harder to say no, yet it still has gaps no person would have. The line between "clever tool" and "intelligent" is blurry, and that blurriness is the heart of this chapter.

1.2What makes intelligence "artificial"?

Artificial intelligence (AI) simply means intelligence shown by machines rather than by people or animals. The word "artificial" means "made by people", not "fake".

In practice, people use "AI" in three different ways, and mixing them up causes a lot of confusion:

  1. The goal: machines that can think or act intelligently.
  2. The field of study: the science and engineering of building such machines, started formally in 1956 (Chapter 3).
  3. The products: specific systems such as Claude, ChatGPT, a spam filter or a self-driving car.

A useful working definition, and the one this book uses:

Definition

Artificial intelligence is the field of building computer systems that perform tasks which would normally require human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognising images, making decisions or solving problems.

Notice the definition is about what a system does, not about whether it "really" understands. That keeps the discussion practical. Whether machines can truly understand is a deep question for philosophers (see 1.5); whether a system can reliably do a task is a question you can test.

Real-world example

You probably use a dozen AI systems every day without noticing: the spam filter in your email, face unlock on your phone, the route your map app suggests, product recommendations in online shops, the autocorrect on your keyboard, voice assistants, and the system that flags unusual card payments as possible fraud. Claude and ChatGPT are simply the most visible kind.

1.3Narrow AI, general AI and superintelligence

People sort AI into three broad categories. Only the first definitely exists today.

CategoryWhat it meansDoes it exist?
Narrow AI (also "weak AI")Good at a specific task or range of tasks: playing chess, translating, recognising facesYes. All AI products today are narrow in some sense.
Artificial general intelligence (AGI)Able to learn and perform almost any intellectual task a person can, at a human level or betterDisputed. There's no agreed definition or test, and experts disagree sharply on how close current systems are.
SuperintelligenceFar smarter than the best humans in practically every areaNo. It's a hypothetical future possibility.

Modern language models such as Claude complicate this neat picture. They aren't narrow in the old sense: the same system can write poetry, analyse a spreadsheet, explain chemistry and debug software. But they also make mistakes no expert would make, can't learn permanently from a conversation, and lack a body and direct experience of the world. Many researchers describe them as "broadly capable but not general" in the full sense.

Opinion · not settled fact

Whether and when AGI will arrive is one of the most argued-about questions in technology. Some AI company leaders have said publicly they expect very capable general systems within years; many academic researchers expect it to take much longer or doubt current methods will get there. This book doesn't take a side. When you hear a confident prediction, treat it as an opinion and ask what evidence it rests on.

1.4Easy for us, hard for machines

In the 1980s, the roboticist Hans Moravec and others noticed something surprising. Tasks that feel hard to people, like chess or advanced maths, turned out to be relatively easy to program. Tasks that feel effortless, like walking across a cluttered room, folding laundry or recognising sarcasm, turned out to be extremely hard. This is known as Moravec's paradox.

The usual explanation is evolution. Humans have been refining perception and movement for hundreds of millions of years, so we do them without noticing. Abstract reasoning is a recent skill, so it feels hard to us even though it can be simpler underneath.

Things AI does well today

  • Writing and summarising text
  • Translating languages
  • Writing and explaining code
  • Spotting patterns in huge amounts of data
  • Playing games with clear rules
  • Recognising objects in photos

Things AI still finds hard

  • Reliable common sense in unusual situations
  • Knowing when it doesn't know something
  • Physical tasks in messy real-world settings
  • Learning permanently from a single experience
  • Long, multi-step tasks without supervision
  • Exact facts it hasn't seen, such as recent events
Real-world example: a QA view

If you test software, Moravec's paradox will feel familiar. An AI can write fifty well-structured test cases in seconds, yet it may miss an obvious real-world scenario that any experienced tester would think of immediately, such as a user double-clicking "Pay". That's why AI works best with an experienced person reviewing its work.

1.5Can a machine really understand?

This is one of the oldest debates in AI. Two famous ideas frame it.

The Turing Test (1950). Alan Turing suggested we stop arguing about the word "think" and use a practical test instead: if a judge chatting by text can't reliably tell a machine from a person, we should accept that the machine is doing something like thinking. You'll meet Turing properly in Chapter 3.

The Chinese Room (1980). The philosopher John Searle replied with a thought experiment. Imagine a person who speaks no Chinese, locked in a room with a huge rule book. Notes in Chinese are pushed under the door. By following the rules exactly, the person writes back perfect Chinese answers. People outside believe someone in the room understands Chinese. But the person inside understands nothing. They are only matching symbols. Searle argued that computers are like the person in the room: following rules is not the same as understanding.

In everyday life

Think of a student who memorises answers for an exam without understanding the subject. They might score well on familiar questions but fall apart when a question is phrased differently. Critics say AI is like that student; supporters say modern AI handles new phrasings too well for that to be the full story. Both sides have a point, which is why the debate continues.

For practical purposes, this book takes a simple position: judge AI by what it reliably does, test it carefully, and don't assume it understands things the way a person does. That attitude will serve you well whatever the philosophers eventually decide.

The "AI effect": why AI keeps disappearing

There's a well-known pattern: as soon as a computer can do something, people stop calling it intelligence. Chess was once seen as the peak of human intellect; when Deep Blue beat the world champion in 1997, many said it was "just search". Recognising handwriting, translating, and transcribing speech followed the same path. A remark often attributed to the computer scientist Larry Tesler sums it up: "AI is whatever hasn't been done yet."

This matters because it distorts how we judge progress. Each success gets reclassified as "not real intelligence", while the remaining unsolved problems get labelled as the real test. Keep this in mind when reading confident claims either way.

1.6Why this matters for you

You don't need to settle these debates to use AI well. But understanding them protects you from two opposite mistakes:

  • Over-trusting AI because it sounds fluent and confident. Fluent language isn't proof of understanding or accuracy.
  • Dismissing AI as "just autocomplete". Modern systems can do genuinely useful reasoning, and ignoring that means missing real benefits.

The skilled position sits between them: use AI for what it does well, check its work where it's weak, and keep learning, because the boundary moves every year.

Common misunderstandings
"AI means robots."
Most AI has no body at all. It's software running on computers in data centres.
"AI thinks like a person."
AI systems work very differently from brains, even when the results look similar. Chapter 11 shows how.
"If it sounds confident, it's correct."
Confidence and accuracy are separate in AI. A model can be fluent and wrong at the same time (Chapter 22).
"AGI is already here" or "AGI is impossible."
Both are opinions. Experts disagree, and there's no agreed test.

1.7Review

Key takeaways
  • Intelligence has no single agreed definition; it covers learning, reasoning, language, perception, planning and common sense.
  • Artificial intelligence means machines doing tasks that would normally need human intelligence. Judge it by what it reliably does.
  • All AI today is narrow to some degree. AGI is disputed; superintelligence is hypothetical.
  • Moravec's paradox: what's hard for people can be easy for machines, and the reverse.
  • Whether AI truly "understands" is unresolved. Use it, test it, and don't assume human-like understanding.
