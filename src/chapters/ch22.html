A language model writes the most likely next words, not the checked truth. Most of the time likely and true are the same thing, so the answers are right. When the model doesn't know, it can still produce text that looks exactly like a correct answer: a confident date, a ticket number, a court case, a function that doesn't exist. That is a hallucination. You can't switch it off, but you can make it much rarer by giving the model the facts, letting it say "I don't know", asking for sources, and checking the output the way a tester checks any system.

22.1What a hallucination is

A hallucination is output that is fluent and confident but not supported by facts or by the material the model was given. The word is borrowed loosely from psychology; some researchers prefer "confabulation", which in medicine means filling a memory gap with a plausible story the person believes. Either way, the key point is the same: there is no intent to deceive. The model isn't hiding the truth. It produced likely-looking text, and this time likely-looking text was wrong.

In everyday life

Think of a new colleague who is eager to help and hates saying "no idea". You ask them which build fixed the checkout bug. They don't know, but they've seen a hundred build numbers that look like "4.2.17", so they say "I think it was 4.2.17" in a perfectly normal voice. Nothing about their tone tells you it's a guess. Language models behave a lot like this colleague, except they work at enormous speed and never get tired of answering.

Two kinds of "wrong" are worth separating from the start, because the fixes differ:

Factual errors (closed-book)

The model answers from what it absorbed in training, and the answer doesn't match the world. "Playwright was released by Google in 2015." (It was released by Microsoft in 2020.) The fix is usually to give it the facts or a tool to look them up.

Faithfulness errors (open-book)

You gave the model a document, and its answer says something the document doesn't. "The release notes say the rate limit is 500 per minute" when they say nothing about rate limits. The fix is grounding: quotes, citations and checks against the source.

22.2Why it happens

There isn't one cause. Four work together.

1. The model is trained for plausibility, not truth

Pre-training (Chapter 17) teaches the model to predict the next token. The training signal rewards text that resembles the training data. True statements are common in that data, so the model learns a lot of truth along the way. But nothing in the next-token objective itself checks facts. A well-formed made-up ticket number "fits" just as well as the real one.

Question"Which ticket fixed the login bug?"
PatternAnswers like this look like "It was fixed in ABC-1234"
GapThe real number isn't in the context or reliably in memory
OutputA plausible number in the right format, stated confidently

2. Training and testing reward guessing

In September 2025 researchers at OpenAI and Georgia Tech (Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang) published "Why Language Models Hallucinate". Their argument is easy to follow if you've ever sat a multiple-choice exam. If a wrong answer and a blank both score zero, guessing is always the best strategy. Most AI benchmarks are graded the same way: right or wrong, with no credit for "I don't know". So training that chases higher benchmark scores quietly teaches models to guess. The authors suggest changing how mainstream benchmarks are scored, so that confident errors cost more than honest uncertainty. You'll see this effect in the lab's scoring script.

3. Gaps and fuzzy memory

A model has a knowledge cutoff: it knows nothing that happened after its training data was collected (for the current Claude models, the research notes list June 2026). It also remembers rare facts poorly. A fact seen thousands of times (the capital of France) is solid; a fact seen once (the author of a small open-source library) is blurry. Your company's Jira tickets, test data and internal APIs were never in the training data at all.

4. Sycophancy: telling you what you want to hear

Sycophancy is the tendency to agree with the user, or to bend the answer towards what the user seems to believe. Anthropic researchers (Sharma et al., 2023, "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models") found it in five leading assistants. They traced part of the cause to human preference data used in RLHF (Chapter 18): people rating answers sometimes preferred a convincing answer that agreed with them over a correct one. In practice, a leading question ("This failure is caused by the new caching layer, right?") invites a confident "Yes" with an invented explanation.

Under the hood: what Anthropic's interpretability work found

In March 2025 Anthropic published "On the Biology of a Large Language Model", which traced the internal steps a model (Claude 3.5 Haiku) takes on various tasks. One finding is directly about hallucination. The model appears to have a default circuit that pushes it towards "I can't answer that". When it recognises a familiar entity, "known entity" features switch that default off, and it answers.

Hallucinations can happen when this switch misfires. The name is familiar enough to suppress the "can't answer" default, but the model doesn't actually hold the specific fact. In one example, the researchers asked about papers by a well-known AI researcher. The model recognised the name, the refusal was suppressed, and it produced a plausible but wrong paper title. The researchers could cause the same failure on purpose by artificially boosting the "known answer" features for a made-up name.

This fits everyday experience: hallucinations cluster around things that are half-familiar. Questions about completely unknown names often get "I'm not sure"; questions about well-known topics with obscure details (a specific version number, a specific clause) are where invented answers appear.

Real-world example

In 2023, in the US case Mata v. Avianca, lawyers filed a brief that cited court decisions invented by ChatGPT, complete with plausible names and quotations. The judge sanctioned them. In 2024 a Canadian tribunal (Moffatt v. Air Canada) held Air Canada responsible for wrong refund information its website chatbot gave a customer. Both cases show the same lesson: the organisation, not the model, owns the consequences of an unchecked answer.

Invented packages are a security risk

When a coding assistant suggests a package name that doesn't exist, an attacker can register that name and fill it with malware, waiting for someone to install it. Researchers have studied this ("package hallucination"; the attack is sometimes called "slopsquatting"). Before installing any dependency an AI suggests, check it exists, is the one you meant and is actively maintained. Chapter 98 covers secrets and supply-chain hygiene.

Anthropic's documentation ("Reduce hallucinations") lists a set of techniques. Here they are, with what each one does and when to use it.

The API has a citations feature

When you build on the Claude API (Chapter 86), you can attach documents with "citations": {"enabled": true}. Claude's answer then comes back with citation blocks that point to exact passages (for example char_location for plain text, page_location for PDFs), and the quoted text is extracted by the API, so the pointers are guaranteed to refer to the documents you supplied. It doesn't guarantee the claim is a fair reading of the passage; that still needs checking. The docs also note that citations can't be combined with structured outputs in the same request.

In everyday life

A good auditor doesn't ask "Are the accounts correct?" and accept "Yes". They ask "Show me the invoice for that line." Asking for quotes and citations is asking the model for the invoice.

Habits that make it worse

  • Leading questions. "Confirm this was caused by the cache" invites sycophancy. Ask "What could cause this? Rank the possibilities and say what evidence supports each."
  • Asking for specifics it can't know. "What's the ticket number?" without giving it Jira access is an invitation to invent one.
  • Very long, noisy context. Important facts buried in the middle of huge inputs are used less reliably (Chapter 20).
  • Treating fluent as correct. Confidence in the wording tells you nothing about accuracy (the ELIZA effect, Chapter 6).
Opinion · Kalai et al. and the wider research community

The authors of "Why Language Models Hallucinate" argue that hallucinations won't fall far until the benchmarks and leaderboards everyone competes on stop rewarding confident guesses. Others argue some hallucination is unavoidable in any system that generates text from compressed knowledge, and that the real fix is architectural: tools and retrieval for facts, with the model used for language and reasoning. Both positions are opinions about the future; neither is settled.

22.5How to test for hallucinations

For a tester, a hallucination is a defect class, and it can be tested like any other. The difference is that the output varies from run to run (non-deterministic), so you measure rates over many cases instead of a single pass or fail. Chapter 125 builds full eval suites; here is the hallucination-specific core.

Test ideaWhat you doPass looks like
Unanswerable questionsAsk about things the source document doesn't containThe model says it doesn't know or that the information isn't there
False premises"Why did release 4.3 remove dark mode?" when it didn'tThe model corrects the premise
Planted factsPut a unique, made-up fact in the document ("the code word is MARIGOLD") and ask about itCorrect answer from the document, not from general knowledge
Contradicting memoryGive a document that deliberately disagrees with common knowledgeIn open-book mode, follows the document and says so
Citation checkFor every citation, check the quote exists and supports the claim100% of quotes found; claims supported
ConsistencyAsk the same question 5 timesSame facts each time (wording may vary)
Leading questionsAsk with a wrong suggestion built inThe model disagrees politely when the evidence does

Count three outcomes, not two: correct, abstained ("I don't know") and hallucinated (a confident wrong answer). A system that abstains a bit too often is annoying; one that hallucinates is dangerous. Your scoring should reflect that difference, which is exactly the point Kalai and colleagues make about benchmarks.

halluscore.py (Python 3, no packages needed)
# halluscore.py - score answers for hallucinations (Python 3, no packages)
import json, sys

ABSTAIN = ["i don't know", "i do not know", "not in the", "no information",
           "cannot find", "can't find", "not enough information", "unable to"]

def grade(case):
    reply = case["reply"].lower()
    abstained = any(p in reply for p in ABSTAIN)
    if case["answer"] is None:          # unanswerable: the right move is to abstain
        return "correct" if abstained else "hallucinated"
    if abstained:
        return "abstained"
    hit = any(a.lower() in reply for a in case["answer"])
    return "correct" if hit else "hallucinated"

cases = json.load(open(sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "cases.json"))
counts = {"correct": 0, "abstained": 0, "hallucinated": 0}
for c in cases:
    g = grade(c)
    counts[g] += 1
    print(f'{c["id"]:<4} {g:<13} {c["question"][:50]}')

n = len(cases)
print("-" * 40)
for k, v in counts.items():
    print(f"{k:<13} {v:>2}  ({v / n:.0%})")
# Two ways to score the same run
plain = counts["correct"] / n
penalised = (counts["correct"] - counts["hallucinated"]) / n
print(f"accuracy only          {plain:.2f}")
print(f"wrong answers cost -1  {penalised:.2f}")

Look at the last two lines. With plain accuracy, this run scores 0.50. When a confident wrong answer costs a point, it scores 0.17. Q6 (an honest "I don't know" to an answerable question) and Q4 (a confident wrong ticket number) both count as "not correct" in plain accuracy, but only Q4 could cause a wrong decision. Q4 is also a typical hallucination: QA-1432 instead of QA-1423, close enough to look right in a review.

Know the limits of simple grading

The script detects abstentions and correct answers by matching phrases. That's fine for a lab and for short factual answers, but it misses paraphrases ("the notes are silent on that"). For real suites, combine code checks with an LLM-as-judge and a human spot-check (Chapter 125).

A rule of thumb

Let the model do language and reasoning; let deterministic systems do facts and counts. If a number matters (test pass rate, defect count, ticket ID), get it from a query, a script or a tool, and let the model write the words around it.

22.7How Claude is trained for honesty

Model makers work on hallucination during post-training (Chapter 18). Anthropic's published constitution for Claude (January 2026) lists honesty properties it wants Claude to have. Among them:

  • Truthful: "Claude only sincerely asserts things it believes to be true."
  • Calibrated: it "avoids conveying beliefs with more or less confidence than it actually has", and acknowledges uncertainty.
  • Transparent and non-deceptive: no hidden agendas, and never trying "to create false impressions of itself or the world".

The same document says Claude should avoid being sycophantic, and should be "diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic". Training towards these goals is why current Claude models often say "I don't have access to that" instead of inventing a ticket number.

Honesty training lowers the rate; it doesn't remove it

Anthropic's own hallucination guide ends with the point that these techniques "don't eliminate them entirely" and that critical information must always be validated. A calibrated model can still be wrong while believing it's right. Treat every model, Claude included, as a capable but fallible colleague whose output is reviewed.

Common misunderstandings
"The AI is lying."
Lying needs intent. A hallucination is likely-looking text that happens to be false. The practical response is the same as for any unreliable input: verify.
"Newer models don't hallucinate."
They hallucinate less in many tests, but no vendor claims zero. Rare facts, recent events and your private data remain risky.
"If it sounds confident, it's probably right."
Fluency and confidence are produced the same way for true and false answers.
"RAG solves hallucination."
It reduces closed-book errors, but the model can still misread, over-summarise or ignore retrieved text, and retrieval can fetch the wrong document (Chapter 23).
"Asking it to double-check fixes it."
Self-checks help, especially with quotes, but the same model can repeat the same mistake. Real verification uses an independent source.

22.8Review

Key takeaways
  • A hallucination is fluent, confident output that isn't supported by facts or by the given source. There's no intent behind it.
  • Causes: next-token training rewards plausibility; right-or-wrong grading rewards guessing; knowledge has gaps and a cutoff; preference training can produce sycophancy.
  • Separate factual (closed-book) errors from faithfulness (open-book) errors: they have different fixes.
  • Reduce them with permission to abstain, grounding, quotes first, citations, retrieval, tools and verification.
  • Test them like any defect class: unanswerable questions, false premises, planted facts, citation checks, repeated runs. Score correct, abstained and hallucinated separately.
  • Claude is trained to be truthful and calibrated, which lowers the rate. Critical facts still need checking against the system of record.
Check yourself

Why can a model produce a confident, well-formatted ticket number that doesn't exist?

Next-token training rewards plausibility. There's no ticket database inside the model and no intent; a well-formed guess simply fits the pattern.

According to Kalai and colleagues (2025), how do common benchmarks encourage hallucination?

Like a multiple-choice exam without negative marking, binary grading makes guessing the best strategy.

You ask "This crash is caused by the new cache, right?" and the model agrees with an invented explanation. What is this?

The leading question pushed the model towards agreement. Ask neutral questions and require evidence.

Which is the most reliable way to get the exact number of failed tests into an AI-written release summary?

Deterministic tools do facts and counts; the model does language. Repetition helps spot guesses but doesn't guarantee accuracy.

In a hallucination eval, why count "abstained" separately from "hallucinated"?

Lumping them together hides the difference between a cautious system and a dangerous one.

Teach it

"AI models write the most likely next words, not checked facts. Usually likely and true match. When the model doesn't know, it can still write something that looks exactly like a real answer: a ticket number, a method name, a court case. That's a hallucination, and it isn't lying; it's a guess that looks confident. Training that rewards guessing and our own leading questions make it worse. To reduce it: give it the source, tell it 'I don't know' is fine, ask for quotes and citations, let it use tools, and verify anything important against the real system. To test for it, ask questions the source can't answer and check it says so."
